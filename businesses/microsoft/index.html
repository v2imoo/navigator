Software & cloud

Microsoft

The company that owned the PC era, nearly missed the next two, and then reinvented itself into a cloud-and-AI giant — proof that a near-dead incumbent can come back.

8 min read · Founded 1975

Contents

Part I — The story

Three eras in one company

Few companies have lived three distinct lives as completely as Microsoft. In the first, from the mid-1970s, it won the personal-computer era so decisively that its software ran on nearly every PC on earth. In the second, through the 2000s, it grew complacent, missed the shifts to internet search and mobile, and was widely written off as a lumbering, past-its-prime giant. In the third, from 2014, it reinvented itself around cloud computing and then artificial intelligence, and became one of the most valuable companies in the world again. The arc — dominance, decline, and genuine renewal — is the reason Microsoft is one of the most instructive business stories there is.

Most dominant incumbents, having missed two major platform shifts, simply fade. Microsoft is the rare case that came back, and the how of that comeback — a change of strategy and, crucially, of culture — is a masterclass in the possibility and the difficulty of reinventing a company that has already won.

How Microsoft won the PC era

Microsoft's original genius was not a product but a deal and a model. In 1980 it licensed an operating system to IBM for its new personal computer — crucially, retaining the right to license that software to other manufacturers too. As a flood of IBM-compatible PCs from other makers filled the world, they nearly all ran Microsoft's operating system. Microsoft had positioned itself to win no matter which hardware maker prevailed: it sold the software layer that every PC needed, and let the hardware makers fight the brutal, low-margin battle below it.

On top of the operating system it built Office — Word, Excel, PowerPoint — which became the standard productivity software of the working world. Two reinforcing monopolies resulted: Windows was valuable because all the software ran on it, and the software ran on it because everyone used Windows; Office was the standard because everyone used it, and everyone used it because it was the standard. The lock-in was enormous, the margins extraordinary, and for two decades Microsoft was the most powerful company in technology.

Our industry does not respect tradition — it only respects innovation.Satya Nadella

The lost decade

Success calcified into complacency. Through the 2000s Microsoft, fixated on protecting Windows and Office, missed the defining shifts of the age. It ceded internet search to Google. It missed the smartphone revolution almost entirely, and its later attempts to compete in mobile failed expensively. Internally, the company became known for a culture of politics and infighting — famously caricatured by a 'stack ranking' performance system that pitted employees against each other and a siloed structure that rewarded turf-protection over collaboration. The giant was still hugely profitable, but it was no longer where the future was being built, and the market treated it as a value stock going nowhere.

This is the pivotal, honest part of the story: Microsoft's decline was not caused by a lack of resources or talent, but by the deadening effect of past success — the instinct to defend the cash cows rather than cannibalise them, and a culture that had turned inward. The lesson is that the hardest thing for a dominant company is not to win, but to keep changing after it has won.

The Nadella turnaround

When Satya Nadella became chief executive in 2014, he inherited a company with enormous assets and a broken sense of direction. His turnaround rested on two linked moves. Strategically, he bet the company on the cloud — Azure, Microsoft's cloud-computing platform — and reoriented its crown jewels around it, moving Office to a subscription model (Microsoft 365) delivered over the internet rather than sold as boxed licences. Culturally, he set out to replace the know-it-all, internally competitive culture with what he called a 'learn-it-all' growth mindset, tearing down silos and the stack-ranking system and insisting the company partner and collaborate rather than defend turf.

He also made a symbolic break with the past: where the old Microsoft fought every rival and insisted everything run on Windows, Nadella's Microsoft embraced competitors' platforms, open source, and the idea that Microsoft's software should run everywhere. The transformation was not instant and not only Nadella's doing, but under his leadership Microsoft went from an afterthought to a cloud-and-AI leader, and its value multiplied many times over. The culture change, he has argued, was the thing that made the strategy possible.

The AI chapter

Microsoft's most recent reinvention is its aggressive move into artificial intelligence, anchored by a major partnership with OpenAI and the integration of AI 'copilots' across its products — from coding tools to Office to Windows — and the provision of AI computing power through Azure. It positioned itself early as a leading platform for the AI era, bringing the technology to the enterprise customers it already dominated. Whether this translates into durable advantage, and how the economics and the OpenAI relationship evolve, is genuinely uncertain and the subject of intense debate. But the strategic logic is consistent with everything Microsoft does best: own the platform layer that others build on, and sell it to the businesses that already depend on you.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Own the layer everyone needs, let others fight below it

Microsoft licensed its operating system to every PC maker rather than tying its fate to one, and let the hardware makers wage the brutal low-margin war beneath it. It sold the software layer every PC required and won regardless of which hardware brand prevailed.

Benefit: Owning a horizontal layer that all players depend on lets you win no matter who wins below you, and capture high-margin value while others compete on thin margins.
Tradeoff: It requires a genuinely essential layer and the model to license it widely; if a rival layer or a shift in the stack displaces you, the whole position can erode.
Tactic for operators: Identify the layer of your industry's stack that everyone must use regardless of who else wins, and position to own it. Selling the common layer can be far more profitable than betting on one competitor in the layer below.
Principle 2

Build reinforcing standards that lock each other in

Windows was valuable because all the software ran on it; the software ran on it because everyone used Windows. Office was the standard because everyone used it, and everyone used it because it was the standard. Each monopoly reinforced the other.

Benefit: Mutually reinforcing standards create lock-in far stronger than any single product, because dislodging you means replacing an entire interdependent system.
Tradeoff: Standards-based lock-in invites antitrust scrutiny and can breed the complacency of assuming the position is permanent.
Tactic for operators: Look for ways your products can reinforce each other's position so that your ecosystem, not any single product, is what customers can't leave. But don't let the strength of the lock-in lull you into stopping innovating.
Principle 3

Beware the complacency that success breeds

Microsoft's lost decade was caused not by weakness but by success — the instinct to defend Windows and Office rather than cannibalise them, and a culture that turned inward and political. It missed search and mobile while protecting its cash cows.

Benefit: Naming complacency as the enemy keeps a winning company alert to the shifts that dethrone incumbents, which almost always come from protecting the old rather than building the new.
Tradeoff: Fighting complacency means deliberately disrupting profitable businesses and unsettling a comfortable organisation, which is painful and resisted.
Tactic for operators: When you're winning, treat complacency as your biggest threat. The instinct to defend today's profits against tomorrow's shift is exactly how dominant companies decline — guard against it actively, not rhetorically.
Principle 4

Change the culture to change the strategy

Nadella's turnaround rested on replacing a know-it-all, internally competitive culture with a 'learn-it-all' growth mindset — tearing down silos and the stack-ranking that pitted employees against each other. He argued the culture change was what made the new strategy possible.

Benefit: Strategy a broken culture can't execute is just a plan; fixing how people work together can unlock a transformation that no strategy memo could.
Tradeoff: Culture change is slow, hard to measure, and easily dismissed as soft — and it demands the leader model it consistently, not just announce it.
Tactic for operators: When a strategy keeps failing to take hold, look at the culture executing it. Sometimes the highest-leverage change isn't the plan but how your people collaborate, learn and treat each other.
Principle 5

Cannibalise your cash cow before it declines

Nadella moved Office from boxed, one-off licences to a cloud subscription (Microsoft 365) and reoriented the company around Azure — deliberately transforming the crown jewels rather than defending the old licence model until it faded.

Benefit: Moving a profitable business to a new model on your own terms captures the future and keeps the customer, instead of clinging to a declining revenue stream until a rival takes it.
Tradeoff: It can mean trading high-margin legacy revenue for lower-margin recurring revenue in the short term, and it unsettles a business that is still making money.
Tactic for operators: When the model underneath your cash cow is shifting, lead the transition yourself — move your profitable product to the new model before someone forces you to. Recurring revenue you build beats legacy revenue you defend.
Principle 6

Stop fighting every rival; run on their platforms too

The old Microsoft fought every competitor and insisted everything run on Windows. Nadella's Microsoft embraced open source, partnered with rivals, and made its software run on competitors' platforms — meeting customers where they were rather than forcing them onto Microsoft's turf.

Benefit: Abandoning reflexive hostility opens up customers and partnerships that tribal loyalty closed off, and meets the market where it actually is.
Tradeoff: It means giving up the comfort of a walled garden and the control that came with forcing everything onto your platform.
Tactic for operators: Question whether your insistence on your own platform is costing you customers who live elsewhere. Sometimes meeting people on rivals' turf, and partnering where you once fought, opens more value than defending the walls.
Principle 7

Sell the new thing to the customers you already own

When Microsoft moved to cloud and then AI, it brought those technologies to the enterprise customers it already dominated — layering Azure and AI copilots onto the relationships it had with the world's IT departments rather than hunting for new customers.

Benefit: An existing base of deep, trusted customer relationships is the fastest channel for a new product, lowering the cost and risk of adoption for a shift like cloud or AI.
Tradeoff: Leaning on the existing base can blind you to new customer segments and to disruptors attacking from below where you're not looking.
Tactic for operators: When you launch into a new wave, start with the customers who already trust you. Deep existing relationships are a distribution advantage for new technology that pure-play newcomers can't match.
Principle 8

Partner for a capability you can't build fast enough

Rather than try to build frontier AI entirely in-house from a standing start, Microsoft partnered deeply with OpenAI, gaining early access to leading models and the ability to integrate them across its products while providing the cloud scale in return.

Benefit: A deep partnership can give you a frontier capability years faster than building it alone, and let each side do what it does best.
Tradeoff: It creates dependence on and entanglement with another organisation whose interests, governance and stability you don't fully control — a strategic and governance risk.
Tactic for operators: When a critical new capability is moving faster than you can build it, a deep partnership can buy you time and position — but weigh carefully the dependence and the loss of control that come with relying on someone else's crown jewels.
Principle 9

Use scale as a weapon in capital-intensive races

Cloud and AI are scale games requiring tens of billions in infrastructure. Microsoft's size and cash flows let it invest at a level few can match, turning its balance sheet into a competitive weapon in the races that will define the next era.

Benefit: In capital-intensive platform races, the scale to out-invest rivals is itself a durable advantage that compounds over time.
Tradeoff: Scale can breed wasteful spending and the arrogance of assuming money solves everything; it must be deployed with discipline and the right strategy.
Tactic for operators: In a capital-intensive race, treat your ability to invest at scale as a strategic asset — but pair it with discipline and a clear thesis, because out-spending rivals only wins if you're spending on the right things.
Principle 10

Remember that renewal is always possible

Microsoft's story is proof that a company written off as a past-its-prime giant, having missed two major shifts, can come back and lead the next era. The decline was real; so was the renewal.

Benefit: Believing renewal is possible — and acting on it — is what separates the incumbents that come back from the ones that fade; despair is self-fulfilling.
Tradeoff: Renewal is genuinely hard and most attempts fail; believing it's possible is necessary but far from sufficient, and false confidence can delay needed change.
Tactic for operators: If your organisation has stalled, don't accept decline as destiny. Microsoft shows that even a company that has missed major shifts can reinvent itself — but only by honestly facing what went wrong and changing both strategy and culture.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Microsoft makes money

Microsoft earns across three broad engines: productivity and business software (Office/Microsoft 365, LinkedIn, Dynamics), intelligent cloud (Azure and server products — now a central growth driver), and personal computing (Windows, devices, gaming including Xbox). The strategic shift under Nadella was toward recurring subscription and cloud revenue, which is stickier and more predictable than the one-off licence sales of the old model. The enterprise relationships — Microsoft sells to the IT departments of most of the world's large organisations — underpin all of it.

Competitive position and the moat

Microsoft's moat is a compound of enterprise lock-in (switching away from Office, Windows and Azure is painful and costly for a large organisation), a vast ecosystem of developers and partners, the power to bundle products together, and the sheer scale to fund cloud and AI investment few can match. In cloud it is one of a small handful of giants; in productivity software it remains dominant; in AI it is an early leader but in a fast-moving, contested field.

Key risks and debates

Complacency risk, again — severity: medium-high

Microsoft's own history warns that dominance breeds the complacency that caused its lost decade; sustaining the 'learn-it-all' culture against the gravity of success is a permanent challenge.

AI economics and the OpenAI relationship — severity: medium-high

The scale of AI investment is enormous and its returns unproven; the complex, evolving partnership with OpenAI carries strategic and governance uncertainty.

Regulatory and antitrust scrutiny — severity: medium

A company this dominant across software, cloud and AI, with a history of antitrust battles, attracts ongoing regulatory attention over bundling and acquisitions.

Cloud competition — severity: medium

Azure competes against deeply resourced rivals; cloud is a scale game where a slip in investment or execution is costly.

Why Microsoft matters

Microsoft matters because it is the clearest proof that a dominant company which has grown complacent and missed major shifts can genuinely reinvent itself — and because that reinvention hinged as much on changing the culture as on changing the strategy. For operators the lessons are a complete cycle: how to win a platform by owning the layer everyone needs; how success itself breeds the complacency that causes decline; and how renewal requires the courage to cannibalise your own cash cows, embrace what you once fought, and rebuild a culture that can learn again. Few companies teach all three.

Frequently asked questions

How did Microsoft originally become so dominant?

By licensing its operating system to every PC maker (not just IBM) and building Office into the standard productivity software — creating two reinforcing monopolies, Windows and Office, with enormous lock-in and margins across the PC era.

What caused Microsoft's 'lost decade'?

Complacency bred by success. Fixated on protecting Windows and Office, it missed internet search and the smartphone revolution, and developed an inward, politically competitive culture that rewarded defending turf over building the future.

How did Microsoft turn itself around?

Under Satya Nadella from 2014, it bet the company on the cloud (Azure), moved Office to a subscription model, embraced open source and rivals' platforms, and — crucially — replaced a know-it-all culture with a 'learn-it-all' growth mindset that made the new strategy possible.

What is Microsoft's AI strategy?

To own the platform layer of the AI era — anchored by its OpenAI partnership — and bring AI 'copilots' and Azure AI computing to the enterprise customers it already dominates. It's an early leader, but the economics and the OpenAI relationship remain genuinely uncertain.

Fintech & payments

PayPal

The company that made it safe to send money to strangers online — solving trust in digital payments, riding eBay to scale, and spawning the 'PayPal Mafia' that reshaped Silicon Valley.

9 min read · Founded 1998

Contents

Part I — The story

Making it safe to send money to strangers

PayPal solved one of the fundamental problems of the early internet: how to send money safely to someone you don't know and can't see. In the physical world, payment is bound up with trust — you hand over cash, or use a card backed by a bank, in a transaction with some accountability. Online, in the late 1990s, there was no easy, safe way for one person or small business to pay another; the infrastructure assumed established merchants and banks, not individuals transacting with strangers across the world. PayPal, formed in 1998 (partly through the merger of companies co-founded by a now-legendary group including Peter Thiel, Max Levchin and Elon Musk), built a service that let anyone with an email address send and receive money online, safely, handling the trust, the security and the fraud so that strangers could transact. It is the definitive case study in solving trust and security in digital payments, in the power of riding a platform to scale, and in the extraordinary network of talent — the 'PayPal Mafia' — that it produced.

PayPal's story carries several distinct lessons: how solving the hard problem of trust and fraud made digital peer-to-peer payments possible; how attaching itself to a fast-growing platform (the auction site eBay) gave it the scale to win; and how the exceptional group of founders and early employees it gathered went on to create or fund an astonishing share of the most important technology companies of the following two decades. Understanding PayPal means understanding that in payments, trust and security are the product; that riding another platform's growth can be the fastest path to scale; and that the talent a company attracts and trains can be a legacy as significant as the company itself.

Solving trust and fraud

The core of PayPal's achievement was solving trust and security in a context where they didn't exist. Letting strangers send money to each other online is a magnet for fraud — fraudsters could exploit any weakness to steal money, and in the early days fraud was an existential threat that nearly killed the company. PayPal's survival and success depended on becoming extraordinarily good at fighting fraud: building sophisticated systems to detect and prevent fraudulent transactions, verify identities, and manage risk, so that legitimate users could transact safely while fraudsters were kept out. Max Levchin and the engineering team pioneered much of this fraud-fighting technology, which was genuinely hard and genuinely novel. The trust that let people use PayPal — the confidence that their money was safe — was not a given; it was engineered, through relentless work on security and fraud.

This is the deep lesson of PayPal's early years: in a payments or marketplace business, trust and security are not features but the core of the product — the thing that makes the whole business possible. Without solving fraud, PayPal would have been destroyed by it; by solving it, PayPal made safe digital payments between strangers a reality. And the capability it built — the fraud-fighting, risk-management expertise — became a lasting source of advantage, hard for others to replicate. The principle generalises: wherever you are asking people to trust you with something valuable (their money, their data, their safety), the systems that earn and protect that trust are not an add-on but the foundation on which everything else rests.

If you want to move money between strangers, you have to beat the fraudsters — or they will beat you.(the existential challenge PayPal had to solve)

Riding eBay to scale

PayPal's path to dominance ran through another company's platform. In the early days, the booming online-auction site eBay was full of individuals and small sellers who needed exactly what PayPal offered — a safe, easy way to pay strangers for the things they bought and sold. PayPal attached itself to this fast-growing ecosystem, becoming the preferred way to pay on eBay, and rode eBay's explosive growth to reach enormous scale quickly. The symbiosis was so strong that eBay ultimately acquired PayPal, recognising how essential the payments service had become to its marketplace. Riding eBay's platform gave PayPal the users, the transaction volume and the scale it might have taken far longer to build alone — a powerful demonstration of how attaching yourself to another platform's growth can be the fastest route to winning a market.

Years later, PayPal was spun back out as an independent public company, and it grew beyond its eBay roots into a broad digital-payments platform serving consumers and merchants far beyond auctions — including through acquisitions (such as the peer-to-peer payment service Venmo) and expansion across e-commerce. The honest picture of modern PayPal is of a large, established payments company that pioneered the category and holds a trusted brand and a substantial two-sided network, but that now competes in a crowded, fast-evolving payments landscape — against the card networks, big-tech wallets, other fintechs, and newer peer-to-peer and checkout services. Its early moat (being first to solve trust in digital payments) has long since become table stakes, and it must compete on scale, brand, breadth and execution in a market where payments innovation is relentless.

The honest ledger: competition, and the PayPal Mafia legacy

A fair account notes both PayPal's competitive challenges and its most remarkable legacy. Competitively, payments is a crowded and relentlessly innovative field, and PayPal — though a pioneer with a strong brand and network — faces intense competition from many directions, and the question of how it sustains growth and relevance as newer, often more seamless or lower-cost payment methods proliferate is a genuine, ongoing one. Being first to solve a problem builds a brand and a network, but it does not confer permanent advantage in a field where the next innovation is always coming.

PayPal's most extraordinary legacy, though, may be the people. The exceptional group of founders and early employees — the 'PayPal Mafia' — went on to create or fund an astonishing share of the most important technology companies of the following two decades, including Tesla and SpaceX (Elon Musk), LinkedIn (Reid Hoffman), YouTube, Yelp, Palantir (Peter Thiel) and many more, and to become some of the most influential investors in Silicon Valley. This makes PayPal a case study in something beyond its own business: the idea that the talent a company gathers, trains and sets loose can reshape an entire industry, and that a company's legacy can be measured not only in its own success but in what the people it produced went on to build. Few companies have spawned a talent network remotely as consequential.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Solve the trust problem that blocks the market

PayPal's achievement was making it safe to send money to strangers online — solving the trust and security problem that had made digital peer-to-peer payments impossible. Solving the trust barrier unlocked the whole market.

Benefit: Solving the trust and security problem that blocks a market unlocks behaviour that couldn't happen before, creating a business where none was possible.
Tradeoff: Solving trust is genuinely hard (PayPal nearly died fighting fraud) and requires sustained, novel work, not a quick fix.
Tactic for operators: Find the trust or safety problem that's blocking a market from existing, and solve it — unlocking behaviour people couldn't safely do before can create an entirely new business. The trust barrier is often what stands between a market and its potential; whoever removes it can win the market.
Principle 2

Treat trust and security as the core product

In payments, letting strangers transact is a magnet for fraud, which nearly killed PayPal. Its survival depended on becoming extraordinarily good at fighting fraud — trust and security were not features but the core of the product.

Benefit: Treating trust and security as the core product, not an add-on, in a business where people entrust you with something valuable, builds the foundation everything else rests on.
Tradeoff: World-class security and fraud-fighting are expensive, hard and never finished, and under-investing until a crisis hits is a constant temptation.
Tactic for operators: Where you ask people to trust you with something valuable — money, data, safety — treat the systems that earn and protect that trust as the core product, not an add-on. Without solving it you have no business; the trust-and-security foundation is what makes everything else possible.
Principle 3

Build the hard capability rivals can't easily copy

PayPal's fraud-fighting and risk-management expertise, pioneered by its engineers, was genuinely hard and novel, and became a lasting source of advantage hard for others to replicate. The hard capability was a moat.

Benefit: Building a genuinely hard capability — like world-class fraud-fighting — creates a moat rivals can't easily copy, because it took real, novel work to develop.
Tradeoff: Hard capabilities take significant investment and talent to build, with uncertain payoff, and the advantage can erode as the capability becomes more widely understood.
Tactic for operators: Invest in building a genuinely hard capability at the core of your business — the kind that takes real, novel work to develop. A capability rivals can't easily replicate becomes a moat; the harder the problem you've solved, the more durable your advantage.
Principle 4

Ride a fast-growing platform to scale

PayPal attached itself to the booming eBay, becoming the preferred way to pay, and rode eBay's explosive growth to enormous scale quickly — far faster than building alone. Riding a platform's growth was the fastest path to winning.

Benefit: Attaching yourself to a fast-growing platform whose users need exactly what you offer can give you scale, volume and reach far faster than building a market alone.
Tradeoff: Dependence on another platform makes you vulnerable to its decisions and health, and the platform may ultimately absorb or displace you.
Tactic for operators: Look for a fast-growing platform whose users need exactly what you offer, and attach yourself to it — riding its growth can be the fastest path to scale. Just be aware of the dependence you take on; the platform's decisions and health become yours, and it may one day absorb or replace you.
Principle 5

Become essential to the platform you ride

PayPal became so essential to eBay's marketplace that eBay acquired it — the payments service was indispensable to the platform. Becoming essential to the platform you ride deepens the symbiosis and your position.

Benefit: Becoming essential to the platform you ride deepens the relationship and your leverage, potentially leading to acquisition or a durable partnership.
Tradeoff: Being essential to one platform concentrates your fate in it, and the platform may acquire you on its terms or seek to replace you once it sees your value.
Tactic for operators: As you ride a platform, aim to become genuinely essential to it — indispensable to how it works. The more essential you are, the stronger your position and the deeper the symbiosis; it may even lead to acquisition, though it also ties your fate tightly to that one platform.
Principle 6

Expand beyond your original platform

After its eBay era, PayPal grew into a broad digital-payments platform far beyond auctions — through acquisitions like Venmo and expansion across e-commerce. Expanding beyond the original platform reduced dependence and widened the opportunity.

Benefit: Expanding beyond the single platform that gave you scale reduces your dependence on it and widens your market, turning a platform-specific business into a broad one.
Tradeoff: Expansion beyond a proven platform is harder and less certain than the original symbiosis, and it may stretch you into areas where you lack advantage.
Tactic for operators: If you scaled by riding one platform, work to expand beyond it — reducing your dependence and widening your market. The platform that gave you scale is also a vulnerability; building a broad business beyond it turns a platform-specific success into a durable, independent one.
Principle 7

Recognise when your first-mover edge becomes table stakes

PayPal's early moat — being first to solve trust in digital payments — long since became table stakes, so it must now compete on brand, scale, breadth and relentless innovation in a crowded field. First-mover advantage fades.

Benefit: Recognising when your first-mover advantage has become table stakes pushes you to compete on execution and keep innovating, rather than resting on a lead that no longer exists.
Tradeoff: Continually innovating to stay ahead is costly and relentless, and a pioneer can struggle to keep pace once its original innovation is universal.
Tactic for operators: Recognise when your first-mover advantage has become table stakes — being first to solve a problem builds a brand and network but confers no permanent edge once others catch up. Compete then on execution, breadth and relentless innovation, not on a lead that has become the baseline.
Principle 8

Compete through relentless innovation in a fast field

Payments is crowded and relentlessly innovative, with newer, often more seamless or lower-cost methods always emerging. PayPal must keep innovating to stay relevant — in a fast field, standing still is falling behind.

Benefit: Competing through relentless innovation in a fast-moving field keeps you relevant as newer methods emerge, where resting on your position would let rivals pass you.
Tradeoff: Relentless innovation is exhausting and costly, and in a crowded field even good innovation may not be enough to stay ahead.
Tactic for operators: In a fast-moving, crowded field, compete through relentless innovation — the next better method is always coming, so standing still is falling behind. Keep improving the experience, the cost, the breadth; your established brand and network buy you time, but only continued innovation keeps you relevant.
Principle 9

Treat the talent you gather as part of your legacy

PayPal's most extraordinary legacy is the 'PayPal Mafia' — founders and early employees who went on to create or fund Tesla, SpaceX, LinkedIn, YouTube and more. The talent it gathered and set loose reshaped an industry.

Benefit: Treating the talent you gather and develop as part of your legacy — people who go on to build great things — multiplies your impact far beyond your own business and builds a lasting network.
Tradeoff: Great talent you develop may leave to build elsewhere, and you can't fully capture the value of what they go on to do.
Tactic for operators: Recognise that the talent you gather, train and set loose can be a legacy as significant as the company itself — the people who leave to build great things extend your impact across an industry. Gather and develop exceptional people; even those who leave become part of a network and a legacy that outlasts the company.
Principle 10

Measure legacy in what you make possible, not just what you build

PayPal is consequential twice over — for solving digital payments, and for the extraordinary people and companies it produced. Its legacy is measured not only in its own success but in what it made possible for others.

Benefit: Measuring your legacy in what you make possible for others — the markets you open, the talent you produce, the companies you enable — reveals an impact far larger than your own business's success.
Tradeoff: Impact through others is hard to measure and capture, and focusing on it shouldn't distract from building your own business well.
Tactic for operators: Consider that your legacy may be measured not only in what you build but in what you make possible for others — the markets you open, the talent you develop, the companies you enable. The most consequential enterprises are often those whose impact ripples far beyond their own walls.

Part III — Business breakdown

How PayPal makes money

PayPal makes money chiefly by taking fees on the transactions that flow across its network — a cut when people and businesses send, receive or process payments through PayPal and its services (including Venmo and its checkout and merchant products). Its economics are driven by the volume and value of payments (total payment volume), the fees it earns, the number of active users and merchants on its two-sided network, and its ability to add higher-value services. As a payments platform, its scale, its trusted brand and its network of users and merchants underpin the business, in a landscape where it must keep innovating to compete.

Competitive position and the moat

PayPal's moat is a trusted brand in payments (built by being first to solve digital-payment trust), a large two-sided network of consumers and merchants, and the scale and data of an established payments platform. These are real but no longer unique — being first to solve trust in digital payments has long since become table stakes. It competes in a crowded, fast-evolving field against card networks, big-tech wallets, other fintechs and newer payment methods, and must compete on brand, scale, breadth and relentless innovation rather than on a durable structural lock.

The flywheel

PayPal's network flywheel
  1. Solve trust and fraud so strangers can pay safely online
  2. Attach to a fast-growing platform (eBay) to reach scale quickly
  3. More users attract more merchants, and more merchants more users
  4. A trusted brand and two-sided network reinforce each other
  5. Fees on growing payment volume fund expansion and acquisitions
  6. Broaden services (Venmo, checkout) to deepen engagement
  7. Scale and brand sustain the network — but innovation must continue (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Relentless competition — severity: high

Payments is crowded and fast-innovating; PayPal faces intense competition from card networks, big-tech wallets, fintechs and newer payment methods, and must keep innovating to stay relevant.

First-mover advantage now table stakes — severity: medium

Being first to solve digital-payment trust built a brand and network but confers no permanent edge in a field where the next innovation is always coming.

Sustaining growth and relevance — severity: medium

As newer, often more seamless or lower-cost payment methods proliferate, how PayPal sustains growth and relevance is a genuine, ongoing question.

Dependence on partners and rails — severity: medium

Like many payment services, PayPal depends in part on underlying card networks and banking rails it doesn't fully control.

Why PayPal matters

PayPal matters because it solved one of the fundamental problems of the early internet — how to send money safely to a stranger — making digital peer-to-peer payments possible, and because its story carries lessons well beyond payments: that trust and security are the core of the product in any business where people entrust you with something valuable; that riding another platform's growth can be the fastest path to scale; and that the talent a company gathers and sets loose can reshape an entire industry. The 'PayPal Mafia' makes it a case study in legacy itself — the idea that a company's most lasting impact may be measured not only in its own success but in what the extraordinary people it produced went on to build. Few companies have been so consequential twice over: once for what they did, and again for whom they produced.

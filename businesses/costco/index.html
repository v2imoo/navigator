Costco

The retailer that makes almost all its profit from membership fees — and deliberately barely profits on the products — so its entire incentive is to serve the member, not exploit them.

9 min read · Founded 1983

Contents

Part I — The story

The shop that makes money by not making money on what it sells

Costco, the membership warehouse club founded in 1983, runs on a counter-intuitive model that inverts how most retailers think. Most shops make money on the goods they sell — buy low, sell higher, keep the margin. Costco does something stranger: it sells its goods at razor-thin margins, deliberately capping how much it will mark anything up, and makes almost all of its profit not from the products at all but from the annual membership fees customers pay simply for the right to shop there. The profit is in the membership; the products are sold at close to cost. This single structural choice shapes everything about Costco, and it is the reason the company inspires an almost cult-like loyalty while quietly being one of the most successful retailers in the world.

The brilliance of this model is that it aligns Costco's interests with its customers' interests more tightly than almost any other retailer. Because Costco makes its money from memberships, its incentive is not to extract maximum margin from each sale but to deliver so much value that customers feel their membership is unquestionably worth renewing. The whole company is organised around that single goal: give members such good value that they keep paying to belong. Costco is the definitive case study in how a business model — specifically, where you choose to make your profit — can align you with your customer and build extraordinary loyalty and trust.

Where you make your profit changes everything

The heart of Costco's genius is the decision about where to make its profit. By making money from membership fees rather than product margins, Costco removes the usual conflict between retailer and customer. A normal shop is tempted to mark things up as much as customers will bear; every extra bit of margin is extra profit, even if it means charging the customer more. Costco deliberately gives up that temptation — it famously caps the mark-up on its products at a strict limit — because its profit comes from somewhere else. This means Costco's incentive on every product is to offer it as cheaply as possible, exactly aligning it with what the customer wants. The customer and Costco are, structurally, on the same side.

This alignment is the source of the trust and loyalty Costco enjoys. Members believe — correctly — that Costco is working to get them the best possible price, not to maximise what it extracts from them, because its business model is literally built that way. That trust is a powerful, durable moat: it is very hard for a conventional retailer, whose profit depends on margins, to credibly match a retailer whose profit depends on keeping those margins low. The lesson is profound and general: the choice of where in your business you make your profit can align you with or against your customer, and aligning with them builds a trust that is extraordinarily hard to compete with.

We're going to be a company that's known for having the lowest prices. If you build that reputation, you don't ever want to violate that trust.Jim Sinegal

Limited selection, enormous buying power

Costco reinforces its low prices with a second, clever choice: it carries a deliberately limited selection of products. Where a typical supermarket stocks tens of thousands of items, Costco carries far fewer — often just one or two options in a category, chosen to be the best value. This seems like a limitation, but it is a strategic weapon. By concentrating all its buying on a small number of products, Costco buys each one in enormous volume, giving it immense bargaining power to drive down the price from suppliers. Fewer products bought in vastly larger quantities means lower costs per item, which Costco passes to members as lower prices. Limited selection is not a constraint; it is the engine of Costco's buying power.

The limited selection has other benefits too: it simplifies operations and logistics, reduces costs, and creates a 'treasure hunt' feeling for members (you never quite know what you'll find), which drives visits and impulse purchases. It also means the products Costco does carry are curated — members trust that if Costco stocks something, it has been chosen as a good value, which reinforces the trust at the heart of the model. The combination of membership-funded low margins and limited-selection buying power lets Costco offer prices that are genuinely hard for broader-selection rivals to beat.

The honest ledger: growth constraints and the model's limits

A fair account notes the real questions. Costco's model, for all its strength, has inherent constraints. The limited selection and warehouse format don't suit every shopper or every market, and the membership requirement is a barrier that some customers won't cross. The model depends on maintaining the trust and value proposition flawlessly — any erosion of the sense that membership is worth it (through creeping prices, worse value, or raising the fee too far) could weaken the renewal loyalty the whole model rests on. Costco also operates on genuinely thin margins, which leaves little room for error and makes it sensitive to cost pressures. And like all physical retailers, it faces the long-term questions posed by e-commerce, though its model — a destination experience built on value and treasure-hunt discovery — has proved more resilient to online competition than many expected.

None of this undermines the achievement; it clarifies that Costco's model is a superbly coherent system optimised for one thing — delivering value to members who pay to belong — and that its discipline is precisely in not betraying that system for short-term gain. The temptation every public company faces, to raise margins and boost short-term profit, is exactly what Costco must resist, because the moment it starts making real money on products rather than memberships, it breaks the alignment that is its entire advantage. The honest story of Costco is a story of disciplined restraint as strategy.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Choose where you make your profit deliberately

Costco makes its profit from membership fees, not product margins, and sells goods at close to cost. This single choice — where in the business to make money — shapes everything and removes the usual conflict between retailer and customer.

Benefit: Deliberately choosing where you make your profit can align you with your customer rather than against them, which builds trust and loyalty that margin-dependent rivals can't easily match.
Tradeoff: Making profit from one source (memberships) means forgoing it elsewhere (product margins), which requires discipline and a model that genuinely works that way.
Tactic for operators: Think carefully about where in your business you make your profit, because it determines whether you're aligned with or against your customer. Making money from their loyalty rather than from extracting margin on each sale can build a trust that becomes your deepest advantage.
Principle 2

Align your incentives with your customer's

Because Costco profits from memberships, its incentive on every product is to offer it as cheaply as possible — exactly what the customer wants. The customer and Costco are structurally on the same side, which is the source of the trust and loyalty.

Benefit: Structurally aligning your incentives with your customer's builds a trust that is extraordinarily hard for a rival with opposing incentives to compete against.
Tradeoff: True alignment requires giving up the profit you'd make by exploiting the customer, and designing a model where serving them is how you win.
Tactic for operators: Design your business so that your incentives genuinely align with your customer's — so that what's best for them is how you make money. A competitor whose profit depends on extracting more from the customer can't credibly match a business built to be on the customer's side.
Principle 3

Use restraint as a strategy

Costco famously caps the mark-up on its products at a strict limit, deliberately refusing the extra margin it could extract, because that restraint is what keeps members' trust and loyalty. Disciplined restraint — not maximising short-term profit — is the strategy.

Benefit: Deliberate restraint — refusing short-term profit you could take — builds the long-term trust and loyalty that is worth far more than the margin forgone.
Tradeoff: Restraint means leaving money on the table every day and resisting constant pressure (from markets, from within) to raise margins, which demands real discipline.
Tactic for operators: Consider where disciplined restraint — refusing profit you could easily take — builds trust worth more than the gain. The willingness to not maximise short-term margin, visibly and consistently, can create a loyalty that becomes a moat rivals chasing every dollar can't cross.
Principle 4

Make limited selection a source of power

Costco carries far fewer products than a typical retailer, concentrating all its buying on a small number of items. This lets it buy each in enormous volume, giving it immense bargaining power to drive down supplier prices — limited selection is the engine of its buying power.

Benefit: Concentrating your buying (or focus) on fewer things gives you far greater volume and bargaining power on each, lowering costs in a way a broad, diluted selection can't.
Tradeoff: Limited selection forgoes the sales from products you don't carry and doesn't suit customers who want breadth, so it's a genuine trade-off.
Tactic for operators: Consider whether concentrating on fewer offerings, bought or made in far greater volume, gives you more power than spreading across many. Limited selection can be a weapon — the buying power and simplicity of focus often beat the appeal of breadth.
Principle 5

Curate so your choices build trust

Because Costco carries only a limited, chosen selection, members trust that anything Costco stocks has been selected as a good value. The curation itself reinforces the trust at the heart of the model.

Benefit: Curating a limited selection so that your choices signal quality and value builds trust — customers rely on you to have chosen well, which deepens loyalty.
Tradeoff: Curation puts the burden on you to choose well every time, and a few bad choices can damage the trust that curation is meant to build.
Tactic for operators: Where you offer a limited, curated selection, make your choices so reliably good that customers trust whatever you stock. When people rely on you to have chosen well on their behalf, the curation itself becomes a reason for their loyalty.
Principle 6

Turn your format into an experience

Costco's limited, rotating selection creates a 'treasure hunt' feeling — members never quite know what they'll find — which drives visits and impulse purchases. The format itself becomes an engaging experience, not just a place to shop.

Benefit: Turning your format into an engaging experience (like the treasure hunt) drives repeat visits and engagement beyond the pure transaction, strengthening the customer relationship.
Tradeoff: An experience built on novelty or scarcity must be continually refreshed, and it can frustrate customers who just want reliable availability of specific items.
Tactic for operators: Look for ways to make the experience of using your business engaging in itself, not just transactional. Costco's treasure-hunt format turns shopping into an event that drives repeat visits — consider what in your format could become a reason to come back, not just a way to buy.
Principle 7

Measure what proves you're delivering value

Costco's key metric is membership renewal — the share of members who keep paying, which directly reflects whether they feel the value is worth it. It measures the thing that proves the model is working, not just sales.

Benefit: Measuring the metric that proves you're genuinely delivering value (like renewal) keeps you focused on the customer's experience, not just short-term sales that can mask eroding value.
Tradeoff: The right metric can be slower to move and less immediately gratifying than sales, and focusing on it requires resisting the pull of short-term numbers.
Tactic for operators: Identify and track the metric that truly proves you're delivering value to customers — like renewal or repeat rates — not just the sales figures that can hide an eroding relationship. What you measure shapes what you optimise, so measure the thing that proves the model works.
Principle 8

Resist the temptation to raise margins

The constant temptation Costco faces, as a public company, is to raise margins and boost short-term profit — but doing so would break the alignment that is its entire advantage. Resisting that temptation, year after year, is the discipline the model requires.

Benefit: Resisting the pull to raise margins protects the customer alignment and trust that is your real moat, where giving in to it would quietly destroy the advantage.
Tradeoff: Resisting margin pressure means consistently disappointing those who want higher short-term profits, which takes conviction and the right ownership and culture to sustain.
Tactic for operators: If your advantage rests on customer alignment, treat the temptation to raise margins as a threat to the moat, not an easy win. The moment you start profiting at the customer's expense, you break the alignment that made you special — resisting that pull is the ongoing discipline the model demands.
Principle 9

Treat trust as something never to violate

Jim Sinegal's principle was that once you build a reputation for the lowest prices, you never want to violate that trust. Costco treats the members' trust as sacred — a thing to protect at the cost of short-term gain, because it's the foundation of everything.

Benefit: Treating customer trust as something never to be violated, even for gain, protects the foundation the whole business rests on and compounds loyalty over time.
Tradeoff: Protecting trust absolutely means forgoing opportunities to profit at the customer's expense, and the discipline must hold even under pressure.
Tactic for operators: Treat your customers' trust as something never to be violated, even when violating it would pay. Trust built over years can be destroyed in one betrayal — guard it as the foundation it is, and let it compound into loyalty that money can't buy.
Principle 10

Build a model coherent enough to resist short-termism

Every element of Costco — membership profit, capped margins, limited selection, renewal focus — reinforces the single goal of delivering value to members. The model's coherence is what lets it resist the short-term temptations that would break it.

Benefit: A deeply coherent model, where every element reinforces one goal, is both hard to copy and resistant to the short-term temptations that erode less coherent businesses.
Tradeoff: Total coherence around one model makes you less flexible and more exposed if the market shifts away from what the model optimises for.
Tactic for operators: Build a model where every element reinforces a single, clear goal — the coherence makes it both hard to copy and resistant to the short-term temptations that erode focus. A system where all the pieces serve one purpose is far stronger than a collection of tactics.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Costco makes money

Costco makes money chiefly from membership fees — the annual payments members make for the right to shop — while selling goods at razor-thin margins close to cost. This inverts normal retail economics: the products are, in effect, the draw that makes the membership worth buying, and the membership is the profit. The model's health is measured less by product margin than by membership growth and, crucially, renewal rates — the share of members who keep paying, which reflects whether they feel the value is worth it. High renewal is both the profit engine and the proof the model is working.

Competitive position and the moat

Costco's moat is its membership model, which structurally aligns it with its customers (its profit comes from their loyalty, not from marking goods up), enormous buying power from a deliberately limited selection, and the fierce trust and loyalty that alignment creates. It is very hard for a conventional, margin-dependent retailer to credibly match a retailer whose profit depends on keeping margins low. The model's limits — the format, the membership barrier, thin margins — are real but have proved remarkably durable, including against e-commerce.

The flywheel

Costco's membership flywheel
  1. Profit comes from membership fees, not product margins
  2. So Costco's incentive is to offer the lowest possible prices
  3. Limited selection concentrates buying into vast volume per item
  4. Enormous buying power drives supplier prices down
  5. Members get genuine value and trust Costco is on their side
  6. High renewal rates and new members grow the fee base
  7. More members mean more buying power and lower prices (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Maintaining the value proposition — severity: medium-high

The model rests entirely on members feeling membership is worth it; any erosion of value (creeping prices, raising the fee too far) could weaken the renewal loyalty the whole model depends on.

Thin-margin fragility — severity: medium

Genuinely thin product margins leave little room for error and make Costco sensitive to cost and supply pressures.

Format and membership limits — severity: medium

The warehouse format, limited selection and membership barrier don't suit every shopper or market, constraining the model's reach.

E-commerce — severity: medium

Like all physical retailers Costco faces long-term online competition, though its value-and-discovery destination model has proved more resilient than many expected.

Why Costco matters

Costco matters because it is the definitive proof that where you choose to make your profit can align you with your customer and build a trust and loyalty that is extraordinarily hard to compete with. By making its money from membership fees and deliberately capping product margins, Costco put itself structurally on the same side as its members — and reinforced it with the buying power of a limited selection. For operators the lessons are deep: that the design of your business model, not just your prices or products, determines whether you're aligned with or against your customer; that limiting your selection can be a source of power, not weakness; and that disciplined restraint — refusing the short-term temptation to raise margins — can be the key to long-term loyalty and success.

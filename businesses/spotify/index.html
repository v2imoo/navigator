Music streaming & audio

Spotify

The company that beat piracy by making legal music easier than stealing it — then spent years fighting the brutal economics of licensing the very product it sells.

9 min read · Founded 2006

Contents

Part I — The story

The company that beat piracy with convenience

Spotify was founded in Sweden in 2006, at a moment when the music industry was being hollowed out by piracy. Services like Napster had shown that people would happily download music for free and illegally, and the industry's response — lawsuits and clunky, restrictive legal alternatives — had failed to stop it. Daniel Ek's insight was deceptively simple: people pirated music not because they wanted to steal but because stealing was easier and better than the legal options. Beat piracy, then, not by fighting the pirates but by building a legal service so good — instant, comprehensive, convenient — that it was easier than stealing. Give people free, instant access to nearly all the world's music, and most would never bother to pirate again.

That is exactly what Spotify built: a vast catalogue, instantly streamable, with a free ad-supported tier so anyone could use it and a paid tier that removed the ads and added features. It worked. Spotify became the dominant force in music streaming and a major reason the music industry returned to growth. But Spotify's story is not a simple triumph — it is a long, instructive struggle against the brutal economics of a business where it must license, at great cost, the very product it sells, from suppliers who hold most of the power.

The freemium funnel

Spotify's growth engine is the freemium model: a free, ad-supported tier that removes every barrier to trying the service, funnelling users toward a paid subscription that removes ads and unlocks features like offline listening. The free tier is both a marketing channel (anyone can start instantly, at no cost) and a revenue source (advertising), while the paid tier provides the higher-margin, recurring subscription revenue. The art is in the conversion — designing the free experience to be genuinely useful yet leave enough friction that a meaningful share of users upgrade. Done well, freemium turns the enormous reach of 'free' into a steady stream of paying subscribers.

This funnel let Spotify grow to a massive user base faster and more cheaply than a paid-only service could, and it directly attacked piracy by making the free, legal option better than the illegal one. But the free tier also costs money — Spotify pays to license the music that free users stream — so the model only works if enough free users eventually pay, and if the economics of the paid tier are sound. And there lies Spotify's central, enduring problem.

We don't have a business where we can just raise prices, because we don't own the content.(the structural reality Spotify has long faced)

The licensing problem

Spotify's defining strategic challenge is that it does not own the thing it sells. The music it streams is owned by record labels and rights-holders — and a large share of the industry is controlled by a small number of powerful 'major' labels. To operate, Spotify must license their catalogues, and it pays a very large portion of its revenue to these rights-holders in royalties. This leaves Spotify with thin margins and limited pricing power: it cannot freely raise prices or cut costs on the core product, because the suppliers hold the leverage. It is, in a sense, a middleman in a business where the suppliers are concentrated and essential.

This is the structural trap of Spotify's model, and it explains almost every strategic move the company has made. Unlike Netflix, which responded to a similar supplier-dependence by becoming a studio and making its own content, Spotify cannot easily 'make its own music' at the scale it needs — music discovery is driven by the specific artists people already love, who belong to the labels. So Spotify has had to find other routes to better economics: growing scale to negotiate harder, improving the experience to justify its existence, and — most importantly — expanding into audio it can own or control more cheaply.

Discovery as the differentiator

If Spotify can't compete on owning the music, it competes on the experience of finding and enjoying it. Its investment in personalisation and discovery — algorithmically curated playlists, personalised recommendations, features that surface music a listener is likely to love — became a genuine differentiator. The catalogue is largely the same across streaming services (they all license from the same labels), so the battle is over which service best helps you discover and enjoy music. Spotify's data and recommendation engine, and signature features that made personalisation feel delightful, became a reason to choose and stay with Spotify over rivals with identical catalogues.

This is Spotify's answer to a world where it can't own the product: make the experience around the product so good that users prefer your version. It is a real but limited moat — rivals invest in discovery too — and it points to the deeper strategic truth that when you can't differentiate on what you sell, you must differentiate on how you deliver it.

The push into podcasts and owned audio

Spotify's boldest strategic move has been to expand beyond music into podcasts and audio more broadly — a response aimed squarely at the licensing trap. Podcasts and other spoken-word audio can be far cheaper to license, produced independently, or owned outright, which improves Spotify's margins and reduces its dependence on the powerful music labels. By becoming a broad 'audio' platform rather than only a music service, Spotify sought content it controls, new advertising opportunities, and a way to differentiate and improve its economics. The push was expensive and its results have been mixed — big bets on exclusive podcasts did not all pay off — but the strategic logic is sound: when your core product leaves you with thin margins and no pricing power, find adjacent content you can own.

The honest picture in the mid-2020s is of a company that decisively won music streaming and beat piracy, but still wrestles with the economics of a licensed product — managing the tension between growth and profitability, pushing into owned audio for better margins, and competing against deep-pocketed technology giants (Apple, Amazon, Google) for whom music is a feature, not the whole business. Spotify's survival and leadership are real; its struggle to turn dominance into durable profit is the ongoing story.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Beat an illicit competitor by being better, not by fighting

The music industry fought piracy with lawsuits and clunky legal alternatives, and lost. Spotify beat it by building a legal service so good — instant, comprehensive, free to start — that it was easier than stealing. It out-competed piracy on experience.

Benefit: Defeating an illicit or free competitor by making the legitimate option genuinely better converts pirates into customers, where enforcement only antagonises them.
Tradeoff: Building something better than free is expensive and hard, and in Spotify's case it meant accepting the thin economics of a licensed, partly-free product.
Tactic for operators: When you're losing to a free, illicit or 'good enough' competitor, ask what would make your legitimate option genuinely easier and better than theirs. People usually choose convenience over principle — win on experience rather than trying to enforce your way to victory.
Principle 2

Use free as the top of your funnel

Spotify's free, ad-supported tier removes every barrier to starting, acting as both a marketing channel and an ad-revenue source, while funnelling users toward paid subscriptions. Free is the engine that fills the paid funnel cheaply and at scale.

Benefit: A well-designed free tier acquires users at enormous scale and low cost, seeds the paid funnel, and (via ads) earns revenue from those who never upgrade.
Tradeoff: Free costs real money to serve — especially when you pay to license what free users consume — and the model only works if enough free users eventually pay.
Tactic for operators: Consider a free tier as the top of your funnel — but design it deliberately: useful enough to attract and retain, with enough reason to upgrade that conversion pays for the cost of serving 'free'. Free is a strategy, not a giveaway.
Principle 3

Design the friction that drives conversion

Spotify's free tier is genuinely useful but leaves enough friction — ads, limits — that a meaningful share of users upgrade to paid. The art of freemium is calibrating that gap so free is good enough to keep users but not so good they never pay.

Benefit: Calibrating the gap between free and paid is what turns a large free base into a sustainable paying business; too generous and no one upgrades, too stingy and no one stays.
Tradeoff: Getting the balance wrong in either direction undermines the model, and the right balance shifts over time and across markets.
Tactic for operators: In a freemium model, treat the gap between free and paid as a core design problem. Make free valuable enough to attract and retain, but leave clear, honest reasons to upgrade — the calibration of that friction is what makes the business work.
Principle 4

Know when you don't own what you sell

Spotify's defining constraint is that it doesn't own the music — powerful labels do — so it pays most of its revenue in royalties and has thin margins and little pricing power. Every strategic move follows from honestly facing that structural trap.

Benefit: Clearly recognising that you don't own your core product — and that powerful suppliers hold the leverage — lets you build a realistic strategy around that constraint instead of pretending it away.
Tradeoff: There may be no full escape from supplier power; recognising the trap doesn't dissolve it, and the honest answer is sometimes a permanently thinner business than you'd like.
Tactic for operators: Be clear-eyed about whether you truly own your core product or merely license it from powerful suppliers. If you don't own it, your margins and pricing power are constrained — build your strategy around escaping or mitigating that dependence, not ignoring it.
Principle 5

Differentiate on experience when you can't on product

Because every streamer licenses the same catalogue from the same labels, Spotify couldn't differentiate on the music itself — so it competed on discovery and personalisation, making the experience of finding and enjoying music a reason to choose Spotify.

Benefit: When you can't differentiate on what you sell, differentiating on how you deliver it — the experience, the discovery, the delight — can still win and retain customers.
Tradeoff: Experience advantages are narrower and more copyable than owning the product, and rivals invest in the same discovery features, so the edge must be continually renewed.
Tactic for operators: If you can't differentiate on the product itself, compete relentlessly on the experience around it. When everyone sells the same thing, the best experience of finding, using and enjoying it can be enough to win — but you'll have to keep out-innovating to hold the edge.
Principle 6

Use scale to shift the balance of power

Spotify's growth to a massive user base is partly a strategy to negotiate harder with the labels: the bigger and more essential Spotify is to the industry's revenue, the more leverage it has in the royalty negotiations that determine its margins.

Benefit: Building scale in a supplier-dominated business shifts bargaining power toward you, improving the terms on which you access the product you depend on.
Tradeoff: Scale alone may not overcome suppliers who remain essential and concentrated, and growing to gain leverage can mean years of thin or negative margins.
Tactic for operators: When powerful suppliers hold the leverage, growing your scale — becoming essential to their revenue — is one of the few ways to shift the balance of power. But don't assume scale alone frees you; pair it with routes to content you can actually own.
Principle 7

Expand into adjacencies you can own

To escape the licensing trap, Spotify expanded from music into podcasts and audio — content that can be cheaper to license, produced independently or owned outright — improving its margins and reducing dependence on the music labels. When you can't own the core, find an adjacency you can.

Benefit: Expanding into adjacent content you can own or control more cheaply directly attacks the margin problem that supplier dependence imposes on your core.
Tradeoff: Adjacent bets are expensive and uncertain — Spotify's big exclusive-podcast bets had mixed results — and they take you into content businesses you may not understand.
Tactic for operators: When your core product leaves you with thin margins because you don't own it, look for adjacent content or products you can own or control. Owning the adjacency can fund and strengthen a core you'll never fully own.
Principle 8

Learn from how others escaped the same trap — and why you can't

Netflix escaped supplier-dependence by becoming a studio; Spotify studied that but recognised it can't 'make its own music' at scale, because discovery is driven by the specific beloved artists the labels control. The right lesson was adapted, not copied.

Benefit: Understanding how others escaped a similar trap — and honestly why their exact solution won't work for you — leads to a strategy fitted to your real constraints rather than a borrowed one that fails.
Tradeoff: Misreading the analogy (assuming you can simply copy Netflix) leads to expensive strategic errors; the differences matter as much as the similarities.
Tactic for operators: Study how others escaped the trap you're in, but analyse honestly why their solution may not transfer to your business. The goal is a strategy adapted to your specific constraints, not a famous playbook copied without regard to what's different.
Principle 9

Compete knowing rivals don't need to win

Spotify competes against Apple, Amazon and Google, who bundle music into larger ecosystems and don't need it to be profitable on its own — an unequal fight Spotify must navigate as a pure-play that does need music to pay.

Benefit: Recognising that some rivals treat your whole business as a loss-leading feature lets you compete on focus, specialisation and experience rather than on a price war you can't win.
Tradeoff: Competing against players who don't need to profit is structurally disadvantageous, and there may be no way to match their bundling and cross-subsidy.
Tactic for operators: When rivals treat your core business as a feature they can run at a loss, don't try to out-subsidise them. Compete on focus, specialisation and being the best at the one thing that is your whole business — and be honest about the structural disadvantage.
Principle 10

Balance growth against the proof of profit

Spotify spent years prioritising growth and user scale; its ongoing challenge is converting that dominance into durable profitability on a structurally thin-margin product. At some point, scale must be turned into proven, sustainable profit.

Benefit: Knowing when to pivot from growth-at-all-costs to proving durable profitability is what separates a dominant company from a merely large one that never pays off.
Tradeoff: Pivoting too early can cede growth to rivals; too late can exhaust patience and capital — and on a thin-margin product the path to profit may be genuinely hard.
Tactic for operators: Treat the shift from chasing growth to proving profitability as a deliberate strategic decision, timed to your market and your margins. Dominance that never converts into sustainable profit is a trophy, not a business.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Spotify makes money

Spotify earns from two main sources: premium subscriptions (the large majority of revenue — recurring payments from paid users) and advertising (from the free, ad-supported tier). Its central economic challenge is the cost of content: a very large share of revenue goes to music rights-holders as royalties, leaving thin gross margins. The strategic levers are growing subscribers and revenue per user, improving the mix (advertising and owned audio can carry better economics than licensed music), and using scale to manage royalty costs — all in service of turning a dominant but thin-margin business into a durably profitable one.

Competitive position and the moat

Spotify's moat is scale (the largest dedicated music-streaming base), a personalisation and discovery experience that differentiates near-identical catalogues, a free-to-paid funnel that acquires users cheaply, and a growing push into owned audio. Its vulnerability is structural: it licenses its core product from powerful suppliers, leaving thin margins and little pricing power, and it competes against technology giants who bundle music into larger ecosystems and don't need it to be profitable on its own.

Big-tech competition — severity: high

Why Spotify matters

Spotify matters because it beat piracy not by fighting it but by making the legal, convenient option better than stealing — a lesson in defeating an illicit competitor through experience rather than enforcement — and because it is the clearest case study in the strategic trap of selling a product you don't own. For operators the lessons are how a freemium funnel turns the reach of 'free' into paying customers, how to differentiate on experience when you can't differentiate on the product itself, and how a business dependent on powerful suppliers must fight — through scale, through discovery, and through acquiring content it can own — to escape the thin margins that dependence imposes.

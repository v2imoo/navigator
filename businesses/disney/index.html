Media & entertainment

Disney

The company that turned stories into a flywheel — a character becomes a film, a toy, a ride, a show — and built the most valuable intellectual-property machine in entertainment.

8 min read · Founded 1923

Contents

Part I — The story

The company built on a mouse

The Walt Disney Company began in 1923 and found its footing on the back of a cartoon mouse. But Walt Disney's real invention was not a character — it was a business model. He grasped, earlier and more completely than anyone, that a beloved story and the characters within it could be monetised not once but many times over: as a film, then a television show, then a toy, a book, a comic, and — his boldest leap — a physical place you could visit, a theme park where the stories came to life. Each form fed the others. The film made people love the character; the love drove them to the merchandise and the park; the park and merchandise kept the character alive and sold more films.

This flywheel — create intellectual property once, then monetise it across every possible channel, with each channel reinforcing the rest — is the deep structure of Disney, and it has made the company the most valuable storytelling machine in entertainment. Everything else, including the decades of acquisitions and the modern streaming wars, is an elaboration on Walt's original insight.

The flywheel Walt built

Walt Disney famously sketched the company's strategy as a diagram showing how its parts fed one another: the films created characters and stories; those fuelled the theme parks, the television, the music, the merchandise and the publishing; and each of those, in turn, promoted the films and deepened the audience's attachment to the characters. It was a self-reinforcing system in which the whole was worth far more than any part. A rival could make a good film, but it could not easily replicate the flywheel — the decades-deep library of characters people loved and the many reinforcing ways Disney turned that love into money.

The theme parks were the masterstroke. They turned fleeting screen experiences into a physical destination families would travel to and pay a premium for, creating both enormous revenue and an emotional bond that no competitor could copy — you cannot build another Disneyland's worth of accumulated meaning. The parks are a near-perfect example of a moat: capital-intensive, brand-dependent, and impossible to recreate from scratch.

I only hope that we never lose sight of one thing — that it was all started by a mouse.Walt Disney

Growth by acquiring the best storytellers

For much of its modern history Disney grew not only by creating IP but by buying it — and specifically by buying the companies that made the IP people loved most. Under Bob Iger's long leadership, Disney acquired Pixar (the animation studio that had eclipsed Disney's own), Marvel (a universe of superhero characters), and Lucasfilm (Star Wars). Each acquisition brought not just a library of beloved characters but the creative teams capable of making more of them. Disney then fed all of it into the flywheel: the films drove merchandise, park attractions, television and, later, streaming.

This was a disciplined strategy with a clear logic: in a business where the scarcest resource is characters and stories that audiences genuinely love, acquiring the proven creators of that love — and the engines that keep producing it — is a powerful way to grow, provided you don't crush the creativity you bought. Disney's acquisitions are studied precisely because they mostly worked, feeding fresh IP into a machine built to monetise it many ways.

The streaming disruption

Disney's model was built for a world of cinemas, cable television and physical media — and that world was disrupted by streaming, led by Netflix. For years Disney licensed its content to others and earned richly from cable. Streaming threatened that: it changed how audiences watched, and it meant that continuing to license content to a rival platform was feeding the very thing disrupting Disney's business. The strategic response, launched emphatically with Disney+ in 2019, was to pull its content back, build its own streaming service, and go direct to consumers — accepting the loss of lucrative licensing revenue and the heavy cost of building a streaming business in order to own the customer relationship in the new era.

This was a genuine self-disruption, and an expensive, still-unfinished one. Building streaming meant sacrificing established, profitable revenue for an uncertain future, and it coincided with the structural decline of the cable television business that had been a profit engine. The honest picture in the mid-2020s is of a company managing a difficult transition: a priceless IP library and park business on one hand, and on the other the hard economics of streaming, the decline of cable, and questions about succession and strategy that have played out publicly.

The honest ledger: cable decline, streaming economics and succession

No fair account omits the strains. The decline of traditional television has eroded a business that long subsidised the rest of Disney. Streaming, while strategically necessary, has been costly and its path to the profits cable once delivered is not assured. And the question of who leads Disney — and how — has been unusually public, with leadership transitions that did not go smoothly. These are real challenges, not signs that the underlying machine is broken: the IP library, the brands and the parks remain extraordinary assets. But they show that even the best flywheel must be re-engineered when the channels it runs on are disrupted.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Monetise one creation across many channels

Walt Disney's core insight was that a beloved character could be monetised not once but many times — as a film, a show, a toy, a book, a ride. Each form fed the others: the film built love, the love drove merchandise and parks, which kept the character alive to sell more films.

Benefit: Monetising a single creation across many reinforcing channels multiplies the return on every creative asset and builds a system rivals can't copy by making one good product.
Tradeoff: It requires the capability and capital to operate across many channels (studios, parks, retail), which is expensive and complex to build and coordinate.
Tactic for operators: Ask how a single creation could be monetised across more than one channel, with each channel feeding the others. The company that turns one asset into a reinforcing system captures far more value than one that sells it once.
Principle 2

Build the flywheel, not just the product

Walt literally diagrammed how Disney's parts fed one another — films fuelling parks, merchandise, TV and music, each promoting the films. The strategy was the self-reinforcing system, in which the whole was worth far more than any part.

Benefit: A flywheel of mutually reinforcing businesses is a far deeper moat than any single product, because a rival must replicate the whole interlocking system, not just one piece.
Tradeoff: Flywheels take years and heavy investment to build and only pay off at scale; early on you're funding parts that don't yet reinforce each other.
Tactic for operators: Design your businesses so each one strengthens the others, and map those connections explicitly. The flywheel — not any single product — is what becomes impossible for competitors to copy.
Principle 3

Create an asset that can't be replicated

Disney's theme parks turned fleeting screen experiences into physical destinations with decades of accumulated emotional meaning. You cannot build another Disneyland's worth of accumulated love from scratch — the parks are a near-perfect, irreplaceable moat.

Benefit: An asset that compounds emotional meaning and capital over decades becomes literally impossible to recreate, giving you a moat time only deepens.
Tradeoff: Such assets are enormously capital-intensive and slow to build, and they tie up resources in physical infrastructure.
Tactic for operators: Look for the asset in your business that gets more valuable and more irreplaceable the longer it exists. Investing in something a well-funded rival still couldn't replicate from scratch is one of the strongest moats there is.
Principle 4

Grow by acquiring the scarce resource

In a business where the scarcest thing is characters and stories audiences love, Disney grew by acquiring the proven creators of that love — Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm — gaining both their libraries and the creative teams that keep producing more.

Benefit: Acquiring the scarce, hard-to-build resource at the heart of your business — and the teams that produce it — can be faster and surer than trying to grow it organically.
Tradeoff: Acquisitions are expensive and risky; integrating and, above all, not crushing an acquired creative culture is where most such deals fail.
Tactic for operators: Identify the scarcest resource in your industry, and consider whether acquiring the proven producers of it beats building from scratch — but only if you can preserve what made them valuable rather than smothering it.
Principle 5

Protect the creativity you acquire

Disney's acquisitions mostly worked because it largely let Pixar, Marvel and Lucasfilm keep making what made them great, rather than imposing a deadening corporate hand. The value was the creative engine, and crushing it would have destroyed what was bought.

Benefit: Preserving the autonomy and culture of an acquired creative team protects the very thing that made the acquisition worth making.
Tradeoff: Light-touch integration sacrifices some synergy and control, and requires the discipline to resist meddling with a business you now own.
Tactic for operators: When you acquire a company for its creative or cultural engine, protect that engine from your own corporate instincts. The synergies aren't worth it if you destroy the thing you paid for.
Principle 6

Disrupt your own distribution when the channel shifts

When streaming disrupted cinema and cable, Disney pulled its content back from the rival platforms it had licensed to, built Disney+, and went direct to consumers — sacrificing lucrative licensing revenue to own the customer relationship in the new era.

Benefit: Taking control of your own distribution in a new channel protects your IP from feeding a disruptor and wins the direct customer relationship that defines the next era.
Tradeoff: It means sacrificing established, profitable revenue (licensing, in Disney's case) for an uncertain and costly new business, during a painful transition.
Tactic for operators: When a new channel disrupts how your product reaches customers, be willing to stop feeding the disruptor and build your own direct path — even at the cost of profitable revenue you're used to. Owning the customer relationship matters more than defending the old channel.
Principle 7

Don't let licensing feed your disruptor

Disney had earned richly by licensing content to others, but once streaming rivals were using that content to disrupt Disney's own business, continuing to license it was feeding the enemy. The strategic shift was to reclaim the IP for its own platform.

Benefit: Recognising when your licensing revenue is strengthening a competitor that threatens you lets you cut off the supply before it's used against you.
Tradeoff: Pulling back licensing sacrifices real, immediate revenue and bets on your ability to monetise the IP yourself instead.
Tactic for operators: Watch whether the thing you license to others is being used to disrupt you. Easy licensing revenue can quietly fund your own disruptor — and there's a point where keeping the asset for yourself is worth more than the fees.
Principle 8

Treat beloved brands as assets to steward, not exploit

Disney's century of value rests on audiences' deep trust in and love for its characters and brand. That trust is an asset that must be stewarded carefully — overexposing or cheapening the IP can erode the very love the flywheel depends on.

Benefit: Treating brand love as a long-term asset to protect, not a resource to strip-mine, preserves the emotional moat that makes everything else work.
Tradeoff: Stewardship means passing up some short-term monetisation that would cheapen the brand, which can look like leaving money on the table.
Tactic for operators: Guard the emotional trust your brand holds as carefully as any financial asset. Short-term exploitation that cheapens what people love can quietly destroy the moat that took decades to build.
Principle 9

Re-engineer the flywheel when the channels change

Disney's flywheel was built for cinema, cable and physical media. When streaming disrupted those channels, the machine itself had to be re-engineered — new distribution, new economics — even though the underlying IP and parks remained as valuable as ever.

Benefit: Recognising that a disrupted channel requires rebuilding the system around your assets — not abandoning the assets — keeps a strong business from being written off when its distribution breaks.
Tradeoff: Re-engineering a flywheel mid-flight is expensive and disruptive, and the transition can depress results for years before the new machine runs smoothly.
Tactic for operators: When the channels your business runs on are disrupted, separate your durable assets from your distribution. Re-engineer the machine around the assets rather than concluding the business is broken — the IP may be fine even when the pipes aren't.
Principle 10

Invest behind creative hits, don't just chase them

The flywheel depends on continually producing IP audiences love. Disney's strength is backing creative excellence — through its studios and acquired teams — rather than chasing trends, knowing that a genuine hit powers years of downstream monetisation across every channel.

Benefit: Investing in genuine creative quality, which powers the whole flywheel, yields far more than chasing formulaic trends that don't build lasting attachment.
Tradeoff: Creative success is unpredictable and can't be manufactured; a run of misses hurts, and over-relying on past hits risks staleness.
Tactic for operators: In a creative business, invest in the quality that makes audiences genuinely love your work, because a real hit powers everything downstream. Chasing trends to fill a schedule rarely builds the attachment a flywheel runs on.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Disney makes money

Disney earns across a few large arenas: entertainment (its film studios, television and the Disney+ streaming service); experiences (the theme parks, resorts, cruises and consumer products — long a reliable profit engine); and sports (its ESPN business). The strategic logic that ties them together is the flywheel: IP created in the studios drives attendance and spending in the parks, sales of merchandise, and subscriptions to streaming, while the parks and merchandise keep the IP alive and valuable.

Competitive position and the moat

Disney's moat is one of the strongest in media: a library of beloved characters and stories built over a century and continually replenished (including through Pixar, Marvel and Lucasfilm), iconic brands, and theme parks that are effectively impossible to replicate. The flywheel means an asset created once earns many times over. The vulnerability is not the moat but the channels: the shift from cinema and cable to streaming has disrupted how the IP is distributed and monetised, forcing an expensive transition.

The flywheel

Disney's IP flywheel
  1. A film or show creates beloved characters and stories
  2. Audiences' love drives merchandise, books and licensing
  3. The IP becomes theme-park attractions families travel to
  4. Parks and merchandise deepen emotional attachment to the characters
  5. That attachment drives demand for more films, shows and streaming
  6. Acquisitions (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm) feed fresh IP into the machine
  7. Each channel promotes the others, compounding the value of every asset

Key risks and debates

Cable decline — severity: high

The structural erosion of traditional television is shrinking a business that long subsidised the rest of Disney, and streaming has not yet replaced its profits.

Streaming economics — severity: high

Building Disney+ meant sacrificing lucrative licensing revenue for a costly, still-unproven streaming business whose path to cable-level profits is uncertain.

Creative consistency — severity: medium

The flywheel depends on continually producing IP audiences love; a run of films that don't connect weakens the whole machine, and acquired creative cultures must be nurtured, not crushed.

Leadership and succession — severity: medium

Public, uneven leadership transitions have raised questions about strategy and continuity at the top of a company that depends on steady creative stewardship.

Why Disney matters

Disney matters because Walt Disney invented one of the most powerful ideas in business: that intellectual property, created once, can be monetised across many reinforcing channels, each deepening the value of the others. The flywheel — film to merchandise to park to streaming — and the moat of a beloved, century-deep IP library and irreplaceable parks are a masterclass in building compounding advantage from creativity. For operators the lessons are how to turn a single creative asset into a system that monetises it many ways, how to grow by acquiring the scarce resource (great storytellers) and feeding it into that system, and how even the strongest flywheel must be re-engineered when the channels it runs on are disrupted.

Frequently asked questions

What is Disney's core business model?

The flywheel Walt Disney invented: create beloved intellectual property once, then monetise it across many reinforcing channels — films, television, merchandise, theme parks, streaming — with each channel deepening audiences' attachment and driving the others.

Why are Disney's theme parks so valuable?

They turn fleeting screen experiences into physical destinations with decades of accumulated emotional meaning, generating enormous revenue and a bond no competitor can replicate — you can't build another Disneyland's worth of accumulated love from scratch.

Why did Disney acquire Pixar, Marvel and Lucasfilm?

Because in entertainment the scarcest resource is characters and stories audiences love. Those acquisitions brought both proven libraries and the creative teams that keep producing beloved IP — fresh fuel for Disney's flywheel, largely without crushing the creativity it bought.

What are Disney's biggest challenges today?

The structural decline of cable television that long subsidised the company; the heavy cost and uncertain economics of building Disney+ streaming; and public, uneven leadership transitions — all while the underlying IP library and parks remain extraordinary assets.

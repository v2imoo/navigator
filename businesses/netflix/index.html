Media & streaming

Netflix

The company that kept killing its own business before anyone else could — DVDs by post, then streaming, then its own studio — and reinvented how the world watches.

9 min read · Founded 1997

Contents

Part I — The story

The company that kept killing itself on purpose

Netflix's genius is not any single product but a repeated act of corporate courage: at least three times, it deliberately destroyed its own successful business before a competitor could do it for them. It began in 1997 as a service that mailed DVDs to your door — a business that worked. It then undermined that business by building streaming. It undermined its position as a mere distributor of others' shows by becoming a studio that made its own. Each leap put the existing, profitable business at risk; each was made anyway, on the conviction that it is better to be the one who obsoletes you than the one who gets obsoleted.

That discipline — cannibalise yourself before someone else does — is the thread running through everything, and it is the hardest thing for a successful company to do. Netflix did it again and again, and in doing so reinvented how hundreds of millions of people around the world watch television and film.

From red envelopes to streaming

The founding myth — that Reed Hastings started Netflix after a large late fee on a rented videotape — is tidier than the truth, but the early business was real and clever: a subscription that mailed DVDs in distinctive red envelopes, with no late fees, letting members keep films as long as they liked and queue up the next. It out-competed the dominant video-rental chains by removing the friction and the penalties customers hated. Famously, Blockbuster had the chance to buy Netflix early and declined — a decision that became a parable of incumbents failing to see the threat.

But Hastings always understood the DVD-by-post business as a bridge, not a destination. The company was named Netflix, not DVD-by-post, for a reason. When internet speeds made it viable, Netflix launched streaming in 2007 — and deliberately began steering customers away from the very DVD business that was then its profit engine. It even, clumsily and controversially, tried to split the two apart, a stumble that cost it customers and trust. But the direction was right: the future was streaming, and Netflix chose to lead the transition rather than be killed by it.

Most entrenched companies invest too little in the new and too much in the old. The challenge is to lead the change before you're forced to.Reed Hastings (paraphrased from his writing on the company)

Becoming a studio: the second self-cannibalisation

By the early 2010s Netflix faced a new vulnerability. It was a distributor of other companies' shows and films, licensing content from the very studios and networks that were starting to build their own streaming services and could, at any point, pull their libraries. Dependence on suppliers who were becoming competitors was an existential risk. Netflix's answer, beginning emphatically with House of Cards in 2013, was to become a studio itself — commissioning and owning original content on a massive scale.

This was a second, enormous self-disruption. Making your own content is capital-intensive, risky and a completely different competence from licensing it. But it did several things at once: it reduced dependence on suppliers who were turning into rivals, it created content Netflix owned outright and could offer everywhere in the world, and it gave members reasons to subscribe and stay that no competitor could license away. The shift from distributor to owner of content reshaped the entire entertainment industry and triggered the 'streaming wars' as every major studio scrambled to follow.

The recommendation engine and the data advantage

From early on, Netflix treated data as central. Its recommendation engine — which learns what you watch and surfaces what you're likely to want next — was a genuine advantage in the DVD era and remains one in streaming. The data does more than personalise: it informs which shows to commission, how to present them, and how to keep members engaged and subscribed. Netflix competes not only on the size of its library but on the machine that matches each member to the content that will keep them watching.

This is a quieter moat than original content but a real one. A newer rival can spend heavily on shows, but it cannot instantly replicate the accumulated data and the engine that turns a vast, sometimes overwhelming catalogue into a personalised, sticky experience for hundreds of millions of distinct tastes.

The honest ledger: the streaming wars, saturation and the password crackdown

Netflix's success summoned its own competition. By provoking every major studio to launch a rival service, it turned a market it once had largely to itself into a crowded, expensive battlefield. Growth in mature markets slowed as streaming approached saturation, and for a period subscriber losses rattled investors and forced a strategic rethink. Netflix responded in ways that tested customer goodwill — cracking down on the password-sharing it had long tolerated, and introducing a cheaper, advertising-supported tier after years of insisting it would never carry ads.

These moves were, in their way, another round of self-reinvention — monetising sharing it once ignored, and embracing advertising it once rejected — and they largely worked to restart growth. But they also show the limits of the model: in a saturated, competitive market, the easy growth is gone, and the company must extract more from existing members and defend its content spend against deep-pocketed rivals. The debate over whether Netflix can sustain its lead, and its margins, as the streaming wars grind on is genuine and unresolved.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Cannibalise yourself before a rival does

Netflix repeatedly destroyed its own successful business before someone else could — DVDs for streaming, licensing for owned studio content, ad-free for ad-supported. Each put a profitable business at risk; each was made on the conviction that it's better to obsolete yourself than be obsoleted.

Benefit: Self-disruption lets you control the timing and direction of a transition and keep the customer relationship through it, instead of having a competitor seize the future from you.
Tradeoff: The internal resistance is fierce and the financial logic looks wrong in the short term; you are deliberately wounding a business that is still making money, and the new bet might not fully replace it.
Tactic for operators: Identify which of your successful businesses a new technology or model could make obsolete, and decide to be the one who obsoletes it. Build the new thing with the resolve to let it cannibalise the old — because the alternative is letting a rival do it to you.
Principle 2

Name the company for the destination, not the starting point

It was called Netflix, not DVD-by-post, from the beginning — a signal that mailing DVDs was a bridge to internet delivery, not the business itself. The name kept the company oriented toward where it was going.

Benefit: Framing your company around the enduring mission rather than the current product keeps you oriented to the future and makes the eventual pivot feel like fulfilment rather than betrayal.
Tradeoff: A broad name and vision can tempt unfocused expansion if you forget what you're actually good at today.
Tactic for operators: Define your company by the lasting customer need or destination you serve, not the current technology you use to serve it. It frees you to change the 'how' — the product — without losing your identity when the time comes.
Principle 3

Own what your suppliers could take away

Netflix depended on licensing shows from studios that were becoming its competitors and could pull their content at any time. Its answer was to become a studio and own original content outright — removing the dependence and creating assets no rival could license away.

Benefit: Owning your critical input removes an existential dependence on suppliers-turned-rivals and creates a differentiated asset competitors can't simply buy access to.
Tradeoff: Vertical integration into content is capital-intensive, risky and a different competence; you take on the hit-or-miss economics of production.
Tactic for operators: Map your dependence on suppliers who could become competitors or cut you off. Where that dependence is existential, consider owning the input yourself — the cost of building it is often less than the risk of losing access to it.
Principle 4

Compete on the machine that matches supply to demand

Netflix's recommendation engine turns an overwhelming catalogue into a personalised, sticky experience, and the data informs what to commission. It competes not just on library size but on the machine that matches each member to what will keep them watching.

Benefit: A recommendation and data engine honed over years is a quiet, durable moat — a rival can buy content but cannot instantly replicate the accumulated data that personalises it at scale.
Tradeoff: Over-reliance on the algorithm can narrow discovery and create a sameness; the data advantage must be continually fed and refined.
Tactic for operators: Invest in the system that matches your supply to each customer's demand, not just in the supply itself. At scale, the engine that makes a vast catalogue feel personal can be as strong a moat as the catalogue.
Principle 5

Use global scale to amortise a fixed cost

Netflix's content is a largely fixed cost: a show costs roughly the same to make whether ten million or two hundred million people watch it. By operating globally with the largest subscriber base, Netflix spreads that cost across more members than any rival, improving its economics.

Benefit: Spreading a large fixed cost across the widest possible base gives you a structural cost advantage rivals with smaller audiences can't match.
Tradeoff: Going global means navigating local tastes, regulations, languages and payment systems, and some content doesn't travel — scale is not free.
Tactic for operators: If your biggest costs are fixed (content, R&D, infrastructure), pursue the scale that lets you spread them across the largest base. The player who amortises a fixed cost over the most customers often wins the cost war.
Principle 6

Lead the transition, don't be dragged through it

When streaming became viable, Netflix actively steered customers off its profitable DVD business rather than cling to it — accepting short-term pain and even a damaging stumble to get ahead of the shift it knew was coming.

Benefit: Leading a transition lets you shape the new market on your terms and emerge as its leader, rather than defending a declining business until it collapses.
Tradeoff: Moving too aggressively can alienate customers mid-transition — as Netflix's clumsy attempt to split DVD and streaming showed — and the timing is genuinely hard.
Tactic for operators: When you can see a shift coming, lead your customers through it deliberately rather than being dragged. But bring them with you — a transition executed with too little care for the existing customer can cost you the goodwill you'll need on the other side.
Principle 7

Reinvent your monetisation when growth stalls

When subscriber growth in mature markets slowed, Netflix found new revenue from the same members — cracking down on shared passwords and launching an ad-supported tier after years of rejecting ads. It changed how it made money rather than only chasing new users.

Benefit: When user growth saturates, deepening monetisation of existing customers can restart growth without the cost of acquiring new ones.
Tradeoff: Monetisation changes can test customer goodwill — charging for what was free, adding ads after promising never to — and risk churn if handled clumsily.
Tactic for operators: When new-customer growth slows, look to the value you can unlock from existing customers: new tiers, new revenue models, previously-tolerated behaviour now monetised. But move carefully — goodwill spent clumsily is hard to win back.
Principle 8

Build a culture that can keep changing

Netflix's famous high-performance, high-freedom-and-responsibility culture — documented in its widely-read culture deck — was built to keep a company capable of repeatedly disrupting itself: candid, fast-moving, and willing to make hard calls. A culture of comfort can't cannibalise itself.

Benefit: A deliberately designed culture of candour and adaptability is what makes repeated self-disruption possible; it is the machine behind the strategy.
Tradeoff: High-performance cultures can be harsh and are not for everyone; the same intensity that enables change can burn people out or curdle into fear.
Tactic for operators: If your strategy depends on continual reinvention, design the culture that makes reinvention possible — candour, speed, high standards — and write it down. Strategy that the culture can't execute is just a slide.
Principle 9

Let data decide what to make, not just what to show

Netflix uses what it learns about viewing not only to recommend content but to inform which shows to commission and renew. It turns the data flywheel back onto the supply side, lowering the guesswork of a hit-driven business.

Benefit: Using demand data to guide what you produce reduces the risk of a hit-or-miss business and aligns your creation with proven appetite.
Tradeoff: Data can't fully predict creative success, and leaning on it too hard risks formulaic sameness and missing the original hit no data foresaw.
Tactic for operators: Feed what you learn about customer behaviour back into what you decide to create, not just how you present it. Data-informed creation lowers the odds of expensive misses — as long as it doesn't crowd out genuine originality.
Principle 10

Expect success to summon competition

Netflix's triumph provoked every major studio to launch a rival service, turning a market it once dominated into a crowded, costly war. Its very success created the competition it must now outlast.

Benefit: Anticipating that success breeds imitation lets you build durable advantages — scale, owned content, data, habit — before the rivals arrive, rather than after.
Tradeoff: You cannot prevent competition your success attracts; the costs of defending share and the ceiling on any single market are real.
Tactic for operators: Assume that if you prove a market is valuable, well-resourced competitors will follow. Use your head start to build the moats — scale, ownership, data, customer habit — that will let you outlast them when they do.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Netflix makes money

Netflix's model is simple at its core and powerful at scale: members pay a recurring monthly subscription for access to its library. The economics turn on three things — the number of members, the revenue per member (raised through price increases, tiers and now advertising), and the cost of the content that attracts and retains them. The strategic tension is permanent: content is both the main cost and the main reason anyone subscribes, so the whole business is a bet on spending on content efficiently enough to grow members faster than costs.

Competitive position and the moat

Netflix remains the largest and most global pure streaming service, but it no longer has the market to itself. Its moat is a compound: global scale that lets it amortise content across the world's largest subscriber base, a growing library of owned originals no rival can license away, a recommendation engine honed on enormous data, and the sheer habit of being the default place hundreds of millions of people go to watch. Against it stand deep-pocketed studios with their own libraries and franchises, and a finite amount of household entertainment spending and attention.

The flywheel

Netflix's flywheel
  1. More members fund a bigger content budget
  2. A bigger budget buys more and better original content
  3. Owned content no rival can license away attracts and retains members
  4. More members and their data improve recommendations
  5. Better recommendations raise engagement and retention
  6. Global scale spreads content cost across the largest base, improving economics
  7. Better economics fund still more content (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

The streaming wars — severity: high

By provoking every major studio into streaming, Netflix turned a market it once dominated into a crowded, expensive battlefield of rivals with deep libraries and franchises of their own.

Saturation and content cost — severity: high

In mature markets the easy subscriber growth is gone, forcing deeper monetisation, while the content spending that drives the business keeps rising and must be spent ever more efficiently.

Customer-goodwill moves — severity: medium

The password crackdown and the pivot to advertising restarted growth but tested the goodwill of members who were used to sharing and to an ad-free experience.

Hit-driven content risk — severity: medium

Original content is expensive and unpredictable; a run of weak releases raises churn, and the model depends on consistently commissioning content people must watch.

Why Netflix matters

Netflix matters because it is the clearest case study in a principle most companies know but almost none can execute: cannibalise your own successful business before a competitor does. Three times — DVD to streaming, distributor to studio, and ad-free to ad-supported and paid sharing — it disrupted itself rather than be disrupted, and in doing so reinvented global entertainment. For operators the lesson is both the courage it takes and the discipline it requires: the willingness to put a profitable business at risk for a bigger future, repeatedly, is one of the rarest and most valuable capabilities a company can build — and the streaming wars are the reminder that even a brilliant disruptor eventually summons the competition it must then outlast.

Frequently asked questions

What is Netflix's business model?

A recurring subscription for access to its library, funded content and now funds a massive slate of original content it owns. The economics turn on growing members and revenue-per-member faster than the content cost that both attracts and retains them.

Why did Netflix start making its own shows?

To escape dependence on studios that were becoming its competitors and could pull their content, and to own content outright that it could offer globally and that no rival could license away — reducing risk and differentiating itself, starting emphatically with House of Cards in 2013.

What is the lesson of Netflix for other companies?

Cannibalise your own successful business before a competitor does. Netflix disrupted itself three times — DVD to streaming, distributor to studio, ad-free to ad-supported — rather than be disrupted, which is one of the rarest and most valuable capabilities a company can build.

What are Netflix's biggest challenges now?

The streaming wars it triggered, slowing growth as streaming saturates mature markets, and the permanent tension of spending enough on content to retain members while keeping that spending efficient against deep-pocketed rivals.

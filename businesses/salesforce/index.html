Enterprise software (SaaS)

Salesforce

The company that declared 'the end of software' — and pioneered selling it as a subscription over the internet, turning enterprise software into the recurring-revenue machine the whole industry copied.

9 min read · Founded 1999

Contents

Part I — The story

The end of software

When Salesforce was founded in 1999, enterprise software worked one way: companies bought expensive software, installed it on their own computers and servers, and paid large sums up front, plus costly consultants to set it up and maintain it. It was slow, expensive, and painful. Marc Benioff's radical idea — captured in Salesforce's provocative early slogan, 'the end of software' — was that software should instead be delivered over the internet as a service, accessed through a web browser, and paid for by subscription, like a utility. No installation, no servers to buy, no huge upfront cost — just log in and use it, and pay a predictable fee each month. This was software as a service, or SaaS, and Salesforce pioneered it for the enterprise, beginning with software to manage customer relationships (CRM).

Salesforce is the definitive case study in the business model that came to dominate software: the shift from selling software as a one-off product to delivering it as a subscription service, and the 'land and expand' strategy that makes such businesses compound. The model Salesforce pioneered — recurring subscription revenue, delivered over the cloud, growing within each customer over time — became the template for a huge share of the modern software industry. Understanding Salesforce means understanding why SaaS is such a powerful business model, and why the whole industry followed.

Why subscription software is so powerful

The shift from selling software as a one-off product to delivering it as a subscription transformed the economics, and understanding why is central. Under the old model, a software company made a large sale, then had to find new customers to make the next one — revenue was lumpy and had to be won again and again. Under the subscription model, a customer pays a recurring fee for as long as they use the software, so each customer becomes a stream of revenue that continues month after month, year after year. This recurring revenue is more predictable, compounds as customers are added, and — crucially — grows more valuable the longer a customer stays. A software business built on subscriptions accumulates a base of recurring revenue that is far more stable and valuable than a series of one-off sales.

Delivering the software over the internet (the cloud) made this possible and added further advantages. Customers could start quickly without installation or hardware; Salesforce could update and improve the software continuously for everyone at once; and Salesforce, not the customer, ran the infrastructure. This lowered the barrier to adoption, made the software easier to sell and spread, and gave Salesforce a direct, ongoing relationship with every customer. The combination — subscription pricing plus cloud delivery — is the foundation of SaaS, and it is why the model proved so powerful that it reshaped the entire software industry.

The only constant in the technology industry is change.Marc Benioff

Land and expand

The strategic engine that makes SaaS businesses compound is 'land and expand', and Salesforce is its archetype. The idea is to 'land' inside a customer with a relatively small, easy-to-adopt initial sale — a single team or use case — proving the value quickly and at low risk to the customer. Then, once the software is in and trusted, 'expand': sell more seats to more teams, add more products and modules, and grow the account over time. A customer that starts with a small subscription for one sales team might, over years, expand to the whole company and many additional products, turning a small initial sale into a large, growing, recurring account.

This strategy is powerful because expanding within an existing, satisfied customer is far cheaper and surer than winning a new one, and because it means each customer's value grows over time rather than being fixed at the initial sale. It also deepens the integration and switching costs — the more of a customer's operations run on Salesforce, the harder it is to leave. Salesforce built a vast platform and ecosystem (additional products, an app marketplace, tools for customers to build on the platform) precisely to give itself more to expand into within each customer. Land and expand, compounding across many customers over many years, is how a SaaS business grows into something enormous, and Salesforce executed it as well as anyone.

The honest ledger: growth, complexity and competition

A fair account addresses the real tensions. Salesforce grew enormously, partly through a long series of large acquisitions that expanded its platform but also added complexity, integration challenges and cost — and at times raised questions from investors about discipline, profitability and whether the sprawling product range had become unwieldy. As a mature leader, Salesforce has faced pressure to balance growth with profitability, and its scale and acquisition-driven breadth are a double-edged sword: more to sell to each customer, but a more complex, expensive organisation to run. It also competes fiercely with other enterprise-software giants (some of which bundle competing products into broader suites) and with a constant stream of newer, more focused SaaS challengers attacking specific parts of its range.

And the model Salesforce pioneered is now universal — SaaS is simply how enterprise software works, so Salesforce's early-mover advantage has long since become table stakes, and it must compete on scale, integration, platform breadth and execution rather than on the novelty of the model. The next chapter, as across software, is artificial intelligence — embedding AI into its products to stay essential. The honest picture is of a company that pioneered and still leads a model it no longer has to itself, working to remain essential as the industry it shaped matures and the next technological wave arrives.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Sell software (or anything) as a subscription

Salesforce replaced the one-off software sale with a recurring subscription, so each customer became a continuing stream of revenue rather than a single transaction that had to be won and then re-won with the next customer.

Benefit: Recurring subscription revenue is more predictable, compounds as customers are added, and grows more valuable the longer customers stay — far stronger than a series of one-off sales.
Tradeoff: The subscription model earns less up front and only pays off if customers stay, so it demands relentless focus on retention and delivering ongoing value.
Tactic for operators: Consider turning one-off sales into recurring subscriptions, so each customer becomes a compounding stream of revenue rather than a single transaction. Recurring revenue is more predictable and more valuable — but it shifts the burden to keeping customers, so you must keep earning their renewal.
Principle 2

Deliver over the cloud to lower the barrier

By delivering software over the internet rather than requiring installation and servers, Salesforce let customers start quickly and cheaply, updated the product for everyone continuously, and kept a direct ongoing relationship with every customer.

Benefit: Delivering as a service over the cloud lowers the barrier to adoption, lets you improve the product for all customers at once, and gives you a direct, continuous relationship with each one.
Tradeoff: Running the infrastructure yourself is a major operational responsibility, and continuous delivery means any failure hits all customers at once.
Tactic for operators: Where you can, deliver your product as a service that customers access rather than own, lowering the barrier to starting and letting you improve it for everyone continuously. The direct, ongoing relationship this creates is as valuable as the easier adoption.
Principle 3

Land small to lower the customer's risk

Salesforce's land-and-expand strategy starts by 'landing' with a small, easy-to-adopt initial sale — one team or use case — proving value quickly at low risk to the customer, rather than demanding a huge commitment up front.

Benefit: Landing small lowers the customer's risk and the barrier to saying yes, getting you in the door to prove value before asking for a bigger commitment.
Tradeoff: Small initial deals mean small initial revenue, and you must actually deliver the value that justifies expansion or you never grow the account.
Tactic for operators: Make it easy and low-risk for a customer to start with you — land with a small, quick win rather than demanding a big upfront commitment. Getting in the door and proving value is what earns you the right to grow the account later.
Principle 4

Expand within the customer you've won

Once Salesforce is in and trusted, it expands — more seats, more teams, more products — turning a small initial sale into a large, growing account. Expanding a satisfied customer is far cheaper and surer than winning a new one.

Benefit: Expanding within existing, satisfied customers is cheaper and surer than acquiring new ones, and makes each customer's value grow over time rather than being fixed at the first sale.
Tradeoff: Expansion depends on genuinely delivering value and having more to sell, and over-aggressive upselling can damage the trust the expansion relies on.
Tactic for operators: Focus as much on growing the customers you've won as on winning new ones. Expanding a happy customer — more seats, more products, more use — is cheaper and more reliable than new acquisition, and it compounds each account's value over years.
Principle 5

Build a platform you can expand into

Salesforce built a vast platform and ecosystem — additional products, an app marketplace, tools for customers to build on it — precisely to give itself more to expand into within each customer, feeding the land-and-expand engine.

Benefit: Building a broad platform gives you more to sell into each customer, fuelling expansion, deepening integration, and raising switching costs as more of the customer's operations run on you.
Tradeoff: Platform breadth adds complexity and cost, can dilute focus, and must be integrated well or it becomes an unwieldy sprawl.
Tactic for operators: Build a platform with enough breadth that you always have more to offer an existing customer — new products, modules, tools. The more of their operations run on your platform, the more you can expand and the harder you are to leave; just keep the breadth coherent.
Principle 6

Make recurring revenue depend on retention

Salesforce's recurring revenue only compounds if customers stay, so retention — keeping customers satisfied enough to renew — is the heart of the model. Churn is the enemy of a subscription business.

Benefit: Treating retention as central keeps you focused on continuously delivering value, which is what makes recurring revenue actually compound rather than leak away.
Tradeoff: Relentless focus on retention requires ongoing investment in customer success and product value, and a subscription model punishes you immediately if you stop delivering.
Tactic for operators: In a recurring-revenue business, treat retention as the heart of the model — churn silently destroys the compounding that makes subscriptions valuable. Invest in customer success and continuous value, because keeping customers is what turns recurring revenue into a growing base.
Principle 7

Deepen integration to raise switching costs

The more of a customer's operations run on Salesforce — its whole sales process, its data, its workflows — the harder it is to leave. Deep integration into core processes creates high switching costs that protect the recurring revenue.

Benefit: Deepening integration into a customer's core processes raises switching costs, protecting your recurring revenue and making the relationship durable.
Tradeoff: Deep integration raises the stakes on your reliability (a failure hits the customer's core) and can make customers wary of depending so heavily on one vendor.
Tactic for operators: Aim to become integrated into your customer's core processes, so that leaving you means re-engineering how they work. Deep integration protects recurring revenue through high switching costs — but earn that position with the reliability such dependence demands.
Principle 8

Use a bold narrative to create a category

Benioff's provocative 'end of software' slogan and relentless marketing created a narrative that defined a new category — cloud software — and positioned Salesforce as its leader, winning attention and framing the shift on his terms.

Benefit: A bold, clear narrative can create and define a new category, winning attention and positioning you as its leader before rivals even recognise the shift.
Tradeoff: A bold narrative must be backed by a real product and shift, or it rings hollow, and defining a category also invites competitors into it.
Tactic for operators: When you're pioneering a genuine shift, use a bold, clear narrative to define the new category and claim leadership of it. Framing the change on your terms wins attention and positions you as the leader — but only if the substance behind the story is real.
Principle 9

Balance growth with discipline as you mature

Salesforce's growth through many large acquisitions added breadth but also complexity and cost, and as a mature leader it has faced pressure to balance growth with profitability and discipline. Scale and breadth are a double-edged sword.

Benefit: Balancing growth with discipline as you mature keeps a scaling company from drowning in the complexity and cost that unchecked expansion and acquisition bring.
Tradeoff: Discipline can slow growth and disappoint those who want expansion at all costs, while too little discipline lets complexity and cost overwhelm the business.
Tactic for operators: As you scale — especially through acquisitions — balance growth with the discipline to integrate, simplify and stay profitable. Breadth is a double-edged sword: it gives you more to sell but a more complex, costly organisation to run, so grow with discipline, not just ambition.
Principle 10

Keep reinventing as your model becomes universal

The SaaS model Salesforce pioneered is now how all software works, so its early-mover advantage became table stakes. It must now compete on scale, integration and execution, and reinvent around the next wave — AI embedded in its products.

Benefit: Recognising when your pioneering advantage has become table stakes pushes you to compete on execution and to reinvent around the next wave, rather than resting on a lead that no longer exists.
Tradeoff: Reinventing around each new wave is costly and uncertain, and a pioneer can struggle to stay ahead once its original innovation is universal.
Tactic for operators: When the model you pioneered becomes universal, accept that it's now table stakes and compete on scale, integration and execution — while reinventing around the next wave. The advantage of being first fades; staying essential means leading each new shift, not resting on the last one.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Salesforce makes money

Salesforce makes money from subscriptions to its cloud software — recurring fees customers pay for access, scaling with the number of users (seats) and the range of products they use. Its economics are driven by winning customers, retaining them (recurring revenue is only valuable if customers stay), and expanding within them over time (more seats, more products) — the land-and-expand engine. Because the software is delivered from the cloud and improved for everyone at once, the model combines predictable recurring revenue with the operating leverage of serving many customers from a shared platform.

Competitive position and the moat

Salesforce's moat is early leadership and scale in cloud software, deep integration into customers' core processes (high switching costs — a company's whole sales operation may run on it), and a vast platform and ecosystem that gives it more to sell into each account. It competes with other enterprise-software giants and a stream of focused SaaS challengers. The SaaS model it pioneered is now universal, so its advantage rests on scale, integration, breadth and execution rather than the novelty of the model, and the next battleground is embedding AI to stay essential.

The flywheel

Salesforce's land-and-expand flywheel
  1. Land inside a customer with a small, low-risk initial subscription
  2. Prove the value quickly, winning trust
  3. Expand: more seats, more teams, more products over time
  4. Deeper integration raises switching costs and retention
  5. A broad platform gives ever more to expand into
  6. Recurring revenue compounds across many growing accounts
  7. Scale and cash fund more products and acquisitions (loop repeats)

Why Salesforce matters

Salesforce matters because it pioneered software as a service — delivering software over the internet as a subscription — and the land-and-expand strategy, together the template that reshaped the entire software industry into a recurring-revenue business. For operators the lessons are why subscription revenue is so much more valuable than one-off sales (predictable, compounding, growing with each customer's tenure); why delivering over the cloud lowers barriers and deepens the customer relationship; and why 'land and expand' — entering small, proving value, then growing within the account — is one of the most powerful growth strategies there is, because expanding a happy customer beats winning a new one and compounds over years into something enormous.

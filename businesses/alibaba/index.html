E-commerce & technology

Alibaba

The Chinese giant that built e-commerce by connecting buyers and sellers rather than selling to them — and, by solving the trust problem of online payments, spawned a fintech revolution that reshaped how a nation pays.

10 min read · Founded 1999

Contents

Part I — The story

The marketplace that didn't sell anything

Alibaba, founded in 1999 by Jack Ma and a small group in a Hangzhou apartment, became the dominant force in Chinese e-commerce and one of the most important technology companies in the world — and it did so with a model that differs in a crucial way from the Western giant it is often compared to. Where Amazon is fundamentally a retailer that buys and sells goods (alongside its marketplace), Alibaba's core is a pure marketplace: it connects buyers and sellers and takes a cut, but it does not, in its central business, own the inventory or sell the goods itself. Its platforms — Taobao for consumer-to-consumer and small-business selling, Tmall for brands selling to consumers — are vast digital bazaars where millions of merchants reach hundreds of millions of buyers, and Alibaba provides the platform, the trust and the services, rather than the products. This asset-light, marketplace-not-retailer model is central to understanding Alibaba, and it is the definitive case study in building an e-commerce empire by connecting buyers and sellers rather than selling to them.

But Alibaba's story carries a second, equally profound lesson: how solving the problem of trust in online payments — getting strangers to transact safely in a market where trust was scarce — not only made its marketplace work but spawned a fintech revolution that transformed how an entire nation pays. The payment system Alibaba built to make its marketplace function, Alipay, grew into one of the world's largest payment and financial-services platforms, helping move China toward a largely cashless, mobile-payment economy. Understanding Alibaba means understanding the power of the marketplace model, the way solving trust unlocked both commerce and fintech, and the building of a sprawling ecosystem — commerce, payments, cloud — that became woven into Chinese economic life, along with the distinctive and significant role of the state in China's technology industry.

The marketplace model

The heart of Alibaba's commerce business is the marketplace model, and the distinction from a retailer is strategically crucial. A retailer like a traditional shop (or Amazon's first-party business) buys inventory and sells it, taking on the capital, the risk and the logistics of owning and moving goods. A marketplace, by contrast, owns no inventory; it provides the platform where independent sellers list their goods and buyers find them, and it makes money by taking a cut of transactions and, importantly, by selling advertising and services to the merchants who want visibility and tools. This asset-light model let Alibaba scale its 'supply' of goods enormously and cheaply — the inventory belonged to millions of independent merchants, not to Alibaba — and tap into the vast population of small businesses and entrepreneurs in China, giving them a platform to reach the whole nation. Alibaba's genius was recognising that in China's fragmented, fast-growing economy, the opportunity was to be the platform that connected a sea of small sellers with a sea of buyers.

This marketplace creates powerful network effects: more buyers attract more sellers (more customers to sell to), and more sellers attract more buyers (more choice and better prices), in a two-sided flywheel that, once spinning at scale, is extraordinarily hard for a rival to break into. Alibaba won this network effect in China at enormous scale, becoming the dominant marketplace where much of the nation's commerce happened. And because it monetises largely through advertising and services to merchants (who pay for visibility and tools on the platform) rather than through retail margins, its economics are those of a high-margin platform rather than a thin-margin retailer. The lesson is that the marketplace model — connecting buyers and sellers without owning the inventory — can be a powerful, asset-light, high-margin way to build an enormous business, especially in a market full of small sellers needing a platform to reach customers.

Solving trust, and spawning a fintech revolution

A marketplace connecting strangers only works if they trust each other enough to transact, and in the early days of Chinese e-commerce, trust was scarce — buyers feared paying for goods that would never arrive or would be fake; sellers feared shipping goods they'd never be paid for. Alibaba's crucial innovation was to solve this trust problem through a payment system, Alipay, built on the idea of escrow: the buyer's money is held by Alipay until the goods are received and confirmed, so neither side has to trust the other, only the platform. This removed the fear that would otherwise have stopped the transaction, and it made Alibaba's marketplace work. Solving trust was not a feature; it was the thing that made the entire marketplace possible, exactly as it was for Airbnb and PayPal.

But Alipay became far more than a tool for the marketplace. It grew into one of the world's largest payment platforms, and then into a sprawling financial-services ecosystem — mobile payments, lending, wealth management and more — that helped transform China into a largely cashless, mobile-payment society, where people pay for almost everything by phone. The payment system Alibaba built to make its marketplace function spawned a fintech giant that reshaped how an entire nation handles money. This is one of the most striking lessons of Alibaba: that solving a core problem for your main business (trust in payments) can open an opportunity vastly larger than the original business — that the capability you build to serve one need can become a revolution in its own right. The honest picture, though, must note what followed: the enormous scale and influence of Alibaba's fintech and commerce empire drew significant attention from the Chinese state, and regulatory action (including the dramatic halting of Alipay's parent's planned public offering and a broader tightening of oversight on China's technology giants) reshaped the company's trajectory — a reminder of the distinctive and powerful role of the state in China's technology industry.

The honest ledger: the state, competition and the ecosystem

A fair account centres the role of the Chinese state, which is a defining feature of Alibaba's story in a way that has no close Western parallel. Alibaba grew to enormous scale and influence, and in doing so became subject to the particular dynamics of operating as a dominant technology company in China — where the relationship between large technology firms and the government, and the government's willingness to intervene, regulate and reshape the technology sector, are powerful and distinctive forces. A wave of regulatory tightening on China's technology giants, including Alibaba, significantly affected the company and the sector, and the role of the state is an essential, non-ignorable part of understanding Alibaba. This is not a moral judgement but a description of a genuinely different operating environment, and a crucial counterpoint to the assumption that the dynamics of Western technology companies apply everywhere.

Beyond the state, Alibaba faces intense competition in China's fast-moving e-commerce and technology landscape — from rivals in commerce, payments and the newer forms of online retail (short-video and livestream commerce, and aggressive discount platforms) that have emerged — and the challenge of sustaining growth and dominance as the market matures and evolves. It has also built a broad ecosystem beyond commerce and payments, including a major cloud-computing business, pursuing the same platform logic across sectors. None of this diminishes the achievement — building the dominant marketplace of the world's largest e-commerce market, and spawning a fintech revolution, is extraordinary — but it clarifies that Alibaba's model operates in a distinctive national context, faces relentless competition, and rests on network effects and an ecosystem that must be continually defended. The honest picture is of one of the world's most important technology companies, whose story can only be understood alongside the particular role of the Chinese state and the dynamics of its home market.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Be the marketplace, not the retailer

Alibaba's core connects buyers and sellers and takes a cut, without owning the inventory — unlike a retailer that buys and sells goods. The asset-light marketplace let it scale 'supply' enormously and cheaply through millions of independent merchants.

Benefit: The marketplace model — connecting buyers and sellers without owning inventory — scales supply cheaply, avoids the capital and risk of owning goods, and yields high platform margins rather than thin retail ones.
Tradeoff: A marketplace must solve trust between strangers and reach critical mass on both sides, and it controls neither the quality nor the supply directly.
Tactic for operators: Consider whether you can be the marketplace that connects buyers and sellers rather than the retailer that owns and sells the goods. The asset-light platform scales supply cheaply and earns high margins — especially where many small sellers need a way to reach customers — if you can solve trust and reach both sides.
Principle 2

Monetise through merchant services, not retail margins

Alibaba makes money largely by selling advertising and services to the merchants who want visibility and tools on its platform, not through retail margins on goods. It earns like a high-margin platform, not a thin-margin retailer.

Benefit: Monetising through services and advertising to the sellers on your platform earns high platform margins and aligns your revenue with helping merchants succeed, rather than the thin margins of selling goods.
Tradeoff: It depends on having many merchants who value visibility and tools, and over-monetising them (too many ads, high fees) can drive them away.
Tactic for operators: If you run a marketplace, monetise by selling visibility, tools and services to the sellers who want to reach customers, rather than taking only a transaction cut or retail margin. Merchant services are a high-margin revenue stream aligned with helping sellers succeed — just don't over-monetise them into leaving.
Principle 3

Solve trust to make the market exist

Alibaba's escrow payment system — holding the buyer's money until goods arrive — solved the trust problem that would otherwise have stopped strangers transacting. Solving trust was what made the entire marketplace possible.

Benefit: Solving the trust problem that blocks strangers from transacting is what makes a marketplace exist at all — it's the precondition, not a feature, as it was for Airbnb and PayPal too.
Tradeoff: Solving trust is genuinely hard and ongoing, and a trust failure can damage the whole platform.
Tactic for operators: In any marketplace connecting strangers, treat solving trust as the precondition for the business existing, not a feature to add later. Alibaba's escrow payments made its marketplace possible; find the mechanism — escrow, reviews, verification — that removes the fear stopping your two sides from transacting.
Principle 4

Win the two-sided network effect at scale

Alibaba's marketplace creates a flywheel — more buyers attract more sellers, more sellers attract more buyers — that, won at scale in China, became extraordinarily hard for rivals to break into.

Benefit: A two-sided network effect won at scale is a powerful moat, because a rival starting from zero has neither side, and neither wants to join an empty platform.
Tradeoff: Getting the flywheel started is hard (the cold-start problem), and both sides must grow in balance or the marketplace stalls.
Tactic for operators: If you build a marketplace, focus on winning the two-sided network effect at scale — more buyers attracting more sellers and vice versa. Once that flywheel spins, it's a moat rivals struggle to break into; the hardest part is igniting it and keeping both sides in balance.
Principle 5

Let solving a core problem open a larger opportunity

Alipay, built to solve trust for Alibaba's marketplace, grew into one of the world's largest payment and financial-services platforms, transforming how China pays. Solving a core problem opened an opportunity vastly larger than the original business.

Benefit: Solving a core problem for your main business can create a capability that opens an opportunity far larger than the original — the tool you built to serve one need can become a revolution in its own right.
Tradeoff: Pursuing the larger opportunity can distract from the core, and it may enter areas (like finance) with their own risks and heavy regulation.
Tactic for operators: Watch whether solving a core problem for your business creates a capability with a far larger opportunity of its own. Alibaba's payment tool became a fintech giant — the thing you build to make your main business work can sometimes become bigger than the business itself.
Principle 6

Serve the underserved small businesses

Alibaba recognised the vast population of small Chinese businesses and entrepreneurs needing a platform to reach customers, and built marketplaces to empower them. Serving the underserved sellers was both the opportunity and the mission.

Benefit: Serving an underserved population (like small businesses lacking a platform) can unlock an enormous market and build loyalty and mission, where others overlook them.
Tradeoff: Serving small, fragmented sellers is operationally complex, and their fortunes and behaviour are harder to control than a few large partners.
Tactic for operators: Look for an underserved population — like the small businesses Alibaba empowered — that lacks a platform to reach customers or markets. Serving the overlooked can unlock an enormous market and build genuine loyalty and mission around giving them access they didn't have.
Principle 7

Build a reinforcing ecosystem across sectors

Alibaba extended its platform logic across commerce, payments, cloud and logistics — a reinforcing ecosystem where the businesses strengthen each other and deepen its hold on users and merchants.

Benefit: A reinforcing ecosystem across sectors deepens your hold on users, creates cross-business synergies, and spreads your platform logic into new high-growth areas.
Tradeoff: A broad ecosystem adds enormous complexity and many fronts to manage and defend, and can attract scrutiny over concentration of power.
Tactic for operators: Consider extending your platform logic into a reinforcing ecosystem across sectors — commerce, payments, cloud — where the businesses strengthen each other. Breadth deepens your hold and opens new markets, but manage the complexity and the scrutiny that a sprawling, powerful ecosystem brings.
Principle 8

Understand that your environment can be as defining as your strategy

Alibaba's trajectory was powerfully shaped by the role of the Chinese state — regulation, intervention, the distinctive relationship between technology giants and the government. The operating environment was as defining as any strategy.

Benefit: Understanding that your operating environment — the national context, the role of the state, the regulatory dynamics — can be as defining as your strategy lets you navigate it deliberately rather than assuming universal rules.
Tradeoff: The environment (especially state intervention) is largely outside your control, and misreading it — or assuming one country's dynamics apply in another — can be costly or fatal.
Tactic for operators: Recognise that your operating environment — the national context, the role of the state, the regulatory dynamics — can shape your fate as much as your strategy. Don't assume the dynamics of one country's industry apply everywhere; understand and navigate the specific environment you actually operate in.
Principle 9

Expect dominance to draw the state's attention

Alibaba's enormous scale and influence drew significant attention and intervention from the Chinese state, reshaping its trajectory. In China's context especially, dominance in a strategic sector invites the government's involvement.

Benefit: Anticipating that dominance in a strategic sector draws the state's attention — intensely so in some countries — lets you navigate the relationship with government deliberately rather than being blindsided by intervention.
Tradeoff: The state's attention and intervention are outside your control and can dramatically reshape your business, and the dynamics differ sharply by country.
Tactic for operators: Expect that dominance in a strategically important sector will draw the attention of the state — far more so in some countries than others. Navigate the relationship with government deliberately and understand its power in your context; in some environments, the state is a force that can reshape your business overnight.
Principle 10

Defend the network effect as the market evolves

Alibaba faces relentless competition from evolving e-commerce forms (livestream and short-video commerce, discount platforms) and must continually defend its network effect and ecosystem as China's fast-moving market changes.

Benefit: Actively defending your network effect and ecosystem as the market evolves keeps a dominant position from eroding to new forms of competition that your established model didn't anticipate.
Tradeoff: New forms of competition can attack from angles your model didn't foresee, and defending dominance against fast-evolving rivals requires continual reinvention.
Tactic for operators: Defend your network effect and ecosystem continually as your market evolves — new forms of competition (for Alibaba, livestream and discount commerce) can attack from angles your established model didn't anticipate. Dominance won is not dominance kept; keep reinventing to hold it.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Alibaba makes money

Alibaba makes money chiefly from its commerce marketplaces — not by selling goods itself but by taking transaction fees and, above all, by selling advertising and services to the millions of merchants who want visibility, traffic and tools on its platforms — plus its growing cloud-computing business, its logistics and other ventures, and (through its affiliated fintech business) the vast payments and financial-services ecosystem. Its economics are those of a high-margin platform: asset-light marketplaces monetised through merchant advertising and services, scaled across hundreds of millions of users, rather than the thin margins of owning and selling inventory.

Competitive position and the moat

Alibaba's moat is dominant marketplace network effects in China (more buyers attract more sellers and vice versa, hard for rivals to break into at scale), deep ecosystem integration across commerce, payments and cloud, enormous scale, and data. These are powerful within its market. The defining forces around it are the distinctive and significant role of the Chinese state (regulation and intervention), intense competition from evolving forms of e-commerce and rival platforms, and the challenge of defending dominance as the market matures.

Key risks and debates

The role of the Chinese state — severity: high

A defining, non-ignorable force: the relationship between China's technology giants and the government, and the state's willingness to regulate and reshape the sector, powerfully affect Alibaba's trajectory in ways without close Western parallel.

Intense evolving competition — severity: high

China's fast-moving e-commerce landscape brings relentless competition — rival marketplaces, short-video and livestream commerce, aggressive discount platforms — challenging Alibaba's dominance.

Market maturation — severity: medium

Sustaining growth and dominance as China's e-commerce market matures and evolves is an ongoing challenge.

Ecosystem complexity — severity: medium

A broad empire across commerce, payments, cloud and logistics offers reinforcement but also complexity and the challenge of managing and defending many fronts.

Why Alibaba matters

Alibaba matters because it is the definitive case study in building an e-commerce empire through the marketplace model — connecting buyers and sellers without owning the inventory — and in how solving the trust problem of online payments both made the marketplace work and spawned a fintech revolution that reshaped how a nation pays. For a Navigator reader it is also essential as a window into the distinctive dynamics of China's technology industry and the powerful role of the state. The lessons are that the asset-light marketplace can be a high-margin, scalable way to build an enormous business, especially where many small sellers need a platform; that solving trust can unlock both your core business and opportunities far larger than it; and that a company's operating environment — the national context, the role of the state — can be as defining as its strategy, so that the dynamics of one country's technology giants cannot be assumed to apply everywhere.

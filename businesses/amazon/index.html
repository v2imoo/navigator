Technology & retail

Amazon

Not really a retailer at all — a logistics, cloud, advertising and marketplace machine where every business quietly funds the next.

13 min read · Founded 1994

Contents

Part I — The story

The company that refused to make money

For most of its first two decades, Amazon was famous for one thing: not making a profit. Jeff Bezos absorbed losses that would have killed any ordinary business, pouring investor capital into lower prices, faster delivery and new bets, and treating reported profit almost as a failure of imagination — money that should have been reinvested. A hedge-fund critic once dismissed it as 'a charity being run for the benefit of consumers.' That jibe aged badly. By the mid-2020s Amazon was posting tens of billions of dollars of profit in a single quarter and generating annual revenue rivalling Walmart's — a scale that would have been unthinkable for the money-losing bookstore it began as.

The deeper point is that Amazon is not, in any useful sense, a retailer. It is a logistics network, a computing utility, an advertising platform, a media company and a marketplace that happens to sell things. The retail operation is the loss-leader that feeds everything else. This nesting-doll architecture — where each business funds, subsidises or generates demand for another — is what makes Amazon simultaneously the most admired and most feared company in the world, and nearly impossible to categorise, compete with, or regulate.

The regret-minimisation bet

The founding story is worn smooth by retelling, but it reveals the temperament behind the machine. In 1994 Bezos, a young vice-president at the quant hedge fund D. E. Shaw, read that web usage was growing 2,300% a year. He was not a technologist by training but a systems thinker with a speculator's nose for asymmetric bets — a decade on Wall Street had given him deep comfort with expected-value reasoning and a willingness to underwrite large losses for a shot at a convex payoff.

He framed the decision to leave a secure, lucrative job through what he called a 'regret-minimisation framework': imagine yourself at eighty, looking back, and choose the path you would least regret not taking. It sounds like a motivational poster, but it encoded a real bias — toward action under uncertainty, in a fast-moving window where the cost of doing nothing compounded faster than the cost of failing.

We've had three big ideas at Amazon that we've stuck with: put the customer first, invent, and be patient.Jeff Bezos

Books were never the point

Bezos chose books the way a general chooses terrain — for the advantage it gave his particular weapon. The US book market had over three million titles in print, far more than any physical store could stock; an online store could in principle list them all, the 'infinite shelf.' Books were also standardised, light to ship, and bought by the early internet crowd. But the deeper logic was data and habit. Every order revealed something about a customer's taste and trust, and Bezos grasped earlier than almost anyone that the internet did not merely change distribution — it created a persistent, personalised record of the relationship between seller and buyer. Reviews, recommendations and collaborative filtering were not features; they were the foundation of a data flywheel that would one day extend to every category imaginable.

Amazon went public in 1997 at $18 a share, a money-losing online bookstore by any conventional reading of its filings. What the numbers could not convey was the velocity of learning. Amazon was not scaling a bookstore; it was scaling an operating system for internet commerce whose unit economics improved with every order and every new category layered onto the same infrastructure. Music and video came in 1999; third-party sellers in 2000. Each looked like reckless diversification. Each was a calculated reuse of the same underlying assets: the customer relationship, the logistics network, the data layer.

The invention of the cloud

The popular story is that Amazon had spare servers and decided to rent them out. That is not what happened. In the early 2000s Amazon's own software was a tightly-coupled mess; teams waited months for central IT to provision resources. The breakthrough came not from surplus capacity but from a deficit of agility. Amazon broke its internal services — storage, compute, databases — into modular, self-service units so its own engineers could move faster. Once those services existed internally, offering them to the outside world was a conceptual leap, not a technical one.

S3 launched in 2006, EC2 soon after, with a radical pitch: anyone, even a student in a dorm room, could rent the same infrastructure as the largest enterprises, on demand, paying only for what they used — no capital outlay, no hardware to manage. Andy Jassy, who wrote the original vision and built the business, was neither an engineer nor a product manager by background but a strategist who absorbed Bezos's operating philosophy and applied it to a market that did not yet exist. Amazon kept AWS deliberately obscure for years, barely mentioning it in earnings calls, while it built an insurmountable head start. By the time Microsoft and Google grasped the opportunity, AWS led not just in technology but in organisational learning, customer relationships and the compounding advantages of scale.

Prime: the subscription that became a gravitational field

In 2005 Amazon launched Prime — unlimited two-day shipping for an annual fee. The finance models screamed red: shipping costs would balloon, members would order more often in smaller baskets. Bezos overruled them, reasoning not about the economics of shipping but the psychology of commitment. A customer who has pre-paid an annual fee stops comparison-shopping; their first instinct becomes 'is it on Amazon?' The fee converts a transactional relationship into a gravitational one.

The bet proved spectacularly right. Prime expanded far beyond shipping into video, music, Prime Day, grocery discounts and more, each addition raising the membership's perceived value and locking customers deeper in. The genius is that it turns the cost centre of logistics into a moat: every dollar spent on faster delivery makes Prime more valuable, driving more subscriptions, more volume, and justifying still more logistics investment — a flywheel that is self-reinforcing and, crucially, funded in advance by the customers themselves.

The marketplace paradox

Opening the platform to third-party sellers in 2000 was, in hindsight, the most consequential decision in Amazon's history — more than AWS, more than Prime. Why would a retailer invite competitors onto its own site, sometimes undercutting its own prices? Because the customer does not care who the seller is; they want the widest selection at the lowest price. By opening the marketplace, Amazon expanded its catalogue enormously without the capital burden of owning inventory. Sellers bore the risk; Amazon captured the transaction, the data and, increasingly, the fees.

Third-party sellers now account for more than 60% of units sold. Fulfilment by Amazon (FBA), launched in 2006, was especially clever: sellers who stored inventory in Amazon's network made their items Prime-eligible (more attractive to buyers) while raising the utilisation of Amazon's own warehouses (better unit economics). Everyone won — except the sellers' margins. This is also where the antitrust risk concentrates: regulators allege Amazon uses its marketplace dominance to enforce price parity and penalise sellers who go cheaper elsewhere, a structural-power argument that the old consumer-welfare standard, focused only on prices, struggles to capture.

The advertising colossus nobody predicted

For years Amazon's advertising was a rounding error, then a curiosity, then — around 2020 — the third-largest digital ad platform in the US behind only Google and Meta, and the fastest-growing. Because advertising is almost pure software, the marginal cost of one more sponsored listing is near zero, so the margins are extraordinary — likely even better than AWS. The structural advantage is almost unfair: Google and Meta sell ads on intent signals (what you search, who your friends are); Amazon sells on purchase signals (what you actually buy) and can close the loop from impression to checkout in a single session. The data is observed, not inferred.

Advertising completes a circle that would be near-impossible to build from scratch: a marketplace seller pays Amazon to list, pays again to advertise so customers see the listing, and pays again for fulfilment. At every layer Amazon takes a fee. The platform taxes discovery, transaction and logistics at once — something no competitor can match, because none controls all three layers.

The honest ledger: Alexa and groceries

No account is complete without the failures. Alexa, launched in 2014, was meant to be the next platform — the voice operating system for digital life. Amazon sold over 100 million devices, but the monetisation never came: people asked for the weather, set timers and played music; they did not shop by voice at the scale needed to justify the vast investment, and without a screen there was no advertising surface. Groceries tell a similar story — despite the $13.7B Whole Foods acquisition and years of effort, Amazon holds only a low-single-digit share of US grocery against Walmart's roughly 30%. Perishable, low-margin and dependent on physical proximity, groceries are where Amazon's digital-first model collides with the physics of the real world.

These are not footnotes; they are the limits of the flywheel. They show that the same willingness to subsidise a loss-leader for years — which built Prime and AWS — can also pour billions into bets that never achieve self-sustaining economics. The discipline the model demands is not just patience but the judgement to tell a patient bet from a sunk cost.

The Jassy era: operator replaces visionary

In 2021 Bezos stepped back to executive chairman and Andy Jassy, the builder of AWS, took over. Where Bezos was a visionary who tolerated years of losses for structural advantage, Jassy is an operator extracting margin from the empire Bezos built. Operating expenses grew far slower than revenue; the US fulfilment network was restructured into regional hubs that store inventory closer to customers, cutting both delivery times and transport costs for consecutive years. He also made the hard cuts — the largest layoffs in Amazon's history — and placed the company's biggest-ever bet on AI infrastructure, pledging roughly $100 billion of capital expenditure in a single year, 'the biggest technology shift since the internet,' aiming to repeat the AWS playbook: build the infrastructure layer, let others build on top, and collect rent on every transaction.

Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death. And that is why it is always Day 1.Jeff Bezos, 2016 shareholder letter

That line names the deepest risk, which is not regulation but calcification: whether a company of 1.5 million people, embedded in the infrastructure of daily life, can keep the urgency and the willingness to cannibalise itself that made it dominant. The AI bet is, in essence, a wager that it still can.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Subsidise the customer, tax the seller

Amazon's marketplace is really a three-sided extraction machine: customers get subsidised prices and speed, sellers get access to the customer base, and Amazon takes a fee at every layer — listing, advertising, fulfilment, returns. The customer's gain is funded by the seller's margin, and the gap is Amazon's profit.

Benefit: Enormous free cash flow with minimal inventory risk; Amazon captures upside on both sides of every transaction while sellers carry the product risk.
Tradeoff: The extraction rate has a ceiling. As fees rise and advertising becomes mandatory just to be seen, the platform's value to sellers erodes — which is exactly what the antitrust case targets.
Tactic for operators: If you run a marketplace, design fees so the customer always perceives net value even as the supply side funds that perception. The customer is loyal to the experience; the seller is loyal to the volume. Tax the volume.
Principle 2

Cannibalise yourself before someone else does

Amazon's history is a sequence of deliberate self-disruptions: the marketplace undercut its own first-party retail, Kindle threatened its physical book sales, AWS diverted engineers from the core, Prime Video turned a retailer into a media company. Each time the internal resistance was real and the short-term logic questionable.

Benefit: Self-cannibalisation buys strategic optionality: disrupt your own profitable business before an outsider does and you control the timing, the direction and the customer relationship through the transition.
Tradeoff: The internal politics are brutal, resources bleed from the cash cow to the experiment, and the new business may not replace the old one's revenue — as Alexa shows.
Tactic for operators: Ask which of your businesses a new model could make obsolete, then decide whether you'd rather be the one who obsoletes it. Build the new thing in a separate unit with its own metrics reporting straight to the top — never ask the incumbent business to cannibalise itself.
Principle 3

Productise your own pain

AWS was not conceived as a cloud business; it was the fix for Amazon's own infrastructure bottleneck, built so well that Amazon realised every other company had the same problem. The pattern repeats — FBA productised the logistics network, Amazon Advertising productised the search-ranking system. In each case the internal investment was justified on its own; the external revenue was found money.

Benefit: Products born from internal pain already have product-market fit with at least one demanding customer (you) and solve real, well-understood problems.
Tradeoff: Tools built for your own use may not generalise, and the internal team's incentives (stability) can clash with the external customer's (innovation).
Tactic for operators: Audit your internal tools. If your team built something to solve a problem your peers and competitors also face, you may be sitting on a product. The test: would someone outside your company pay for this?
Principle 4

Sort decisions into one-way and two-way doors

Bezos distinguished 'Type 1' decisions (irreversible, high-stakes, deserving deliberation) from 'Type 2' (reversible, lower-stakes, deserving speed). Most big companies treat every decision as Type 1 because a bad call is career-ending while a slow call is invisible — so everything crawls. Amazon inverts this, pushing reversible decisions down to teams who can move fast and fail without permission, reserving the heavy process for the genuinely irreversible.

Benefit: Speed compounds: a company that makes reversible decisions far faster than rivals runs more experiments per unit of time and learns faster.
Tradeoff: It requires real organisational trust — accepting that some fast decisions will be wrong without punishing the people who made them, a cultural muscle that is hard to build.
Tactic for operators: Explicitly label each decision your leadership makes as one-way or two-way. If more than a fifth are 'one-way', you are almost certainly over-deliberating and moving slower than you need to.
Principle 5

Win on selection, not on taste

Amazon's retail strategy is breadth, not curation — the infinite shelf that physical stores cannot match. It bets that the customer, armed with search, reviews and recommendations, can find what they want in an ocean of options. The same logic runs through AWS's 200+ services and the Kindle's self-published long tail.

Benefit: Breadth creates gravity: the wider the selection, the more customers default to you for any purchase, attracting more sellers and widening selection further.
Tradeoff: Breadth dilutes brand. Amazon is where you go when you know what you want, not for discovery or taste — a vulnerability in fashion, luxury and anywhere curation matters.
Tactic for operators: In a platform business, default to breadth and invest in the tools that help customers choose well. Resist the institutional urge to curate; it doesn't scale and it substitutes your judgement for the customer's.
Principle 6

Build the tollbooth, not the car

Amazon's most profitable businesses — AWS, advertising, seller services — are all infrastructure that others run on top of. Amazon need not win any particular category; it needs to be the platform the winners operate on. Netflix, a streaming rival, runs on AWS. The marketplace takes a cut whichever seller wins. At every layer Amazon captures rent on other companies' activity.

Benefit: Tollbooth businesses generate durable recurring revenue insulated from any single market's competitive swings, plus a data advantage from seeing all the traffic.
Tradeoff: Tollbooth operators eventually hit the trust problem: once customers realise you see all their data and also compete with them, they start building alternatives — the dynamic behind Walmart's and Shopify's marketplace growth and the antitrust case.
Tactic for operators: If you can position your business as infrastructure others build on, do it aggressively — but be honest that the moment you compete with your own platform's customers, you create a structural conflict that will draw regulators, rival coalitions, or both.
Principle 7

Use the long term as a weapon

Most companies optimise for the quarter; Amazon optimised for decade-long structural advantages, reinvesting nearly all its cash flow for almost twenty years and reporting minimal profit. The 1997 shareholder letter ('It's all about the long term'), reprinted every year, functioned as a filter — attracting investors with a matching horizon and repelling those who demanded quarterly optimisation.

Benefit: Long-term thinking is a genuine edge precisely because so few companies can practise it against public-market pressure, board impatience and short incentive cycles — letting you build moats that take years to construct.
Tradeoff: Long-term thinking can decay into long-term excuse-making; not every losing business is a future winner, and the line between patient investment and sunk cost is only clear in hindsight.
Tactic for operators: Split your investor communication into two tracks — the operating business (judged on efficiency and margin) and the investment portfolio (judged on optionality) — and be explicit about the time horizon of each. People can tolerate uncertainty; they cannot tolerate confusion about what you're doing and why.
Principle 8

Design culture as a product

Amazon treats its culture the way most firms treat a flagship product — obsessive design, relentless iteration, zero tolerance for drift. The Leadership Principles are a decision-making operating system that governs hiring and planning; the six-page memo (read in silence at the start of meetings) forces complete, logical argument where slides permit vagueness; 'working backwards' from a press release forces teams to name the customer benefit before writing code.

Benefit: A codified culture scales: across 1.5 million people and dozens of countries, a shared decision-making framework is the only thing that maintains coherence without central control.
Tradeoff: Codification invites legalism — principles become ammunition in internal politics, invoked to win arguments rather than serve customers, as staff did when they weaponised the principles against a return-to-office mandate.
Tactic for operators: Write your operating principles down early and make them specific enough to act on (not 'we value innovation' but 'we write memos because clear writing forces clear thinking'). Hire and evaluate against them — but build in a mechanism to amend them, because principles that can't evolve become dogma.
Principle 9

Make the loss-leader fund itself

Prime is the paradigm: an economically irrational free-shipping offer whose subscription fee offset the cost, whose higher purchase frequency generated more revenue, whose larger base drew more sellers, whose fees funded more logistics — until the whole ecosystem earned more than the shipping it was built to subsidise. FBA and Kindle followed the same arc.

Benefit: Loss-leaders that eventually fund themselves build compounding advantages rivals can't copy without enduring the same period of subsidised losses — the willingness to invest through the trough is the barrier to entry.
Tradeoff: Not every loss-leader funds itself — Alexa, the Fire Phone and grocery are long-running subsidised bets that never reached self-sustaining economics, so the strategy demands the discipline to kill the ones that don't work.
Tactic for operators: When you launch a loss-leader, define in advance the metrics that would prove it's working (lifetime value, cross-sell, retention) and the deadline by which they must appear. A loss-leader without a thesis is just a loss.
Principle 10

Own the last mile

Amazon's most defensible advantage is not software or data but the physical network connecting its warehouses to your door — regional fulfilment hubs, a proprietary delivery fleet, air cargo. These are tens of billions of dollars and years of construction that no startup can replicate and no software company can disrupt.

Benefit: Physical infrastructure is the ultimate moat: unlike software advantages that sufficient engineering talent can copy, a nationwide logistics network compounds over years of capital and operational expertise.
Tradeoff: It is capital-intensive and operationally heavy, and it creates vast labour obligations — hundreds of thousands of warehouse and delivery workers, making the company a perpetual target for organising and regulation.
Tactic for operators: If your business involves physical delivery, judge whether owning the last mile creates a customer-experience advantage worth the capital. For most companies the answer is no — outsource. But if delivery speed and reliability are your core differentiator, ownership may be the only way to protect it at scale.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Amazon makes money

Amazon reports several revenue lines, but the strategic picture is clearer as four business models on shared infrastructure. The critical fact is that its revenue mix is shifting steadily toward its highest-margin businesses: cloud, advertising and seller services are roughly half of revenue but the large majority of operating profit. That margin-mix shift, not top-line growth, is what drove Amazon's profit transformation from near-zero in the mid-2010s to tens of billions a year.

Competitive position and the moat

Amazon competes in different markets with different rivals and different moat strengths. It is dominant in US e-commerce and cloud, a fast-rising number three in digital advertising, moderate in streaming, and genuinely weak in grocery. The moat is not one thing but a stack: marketplace network effects, AWS switching costs, logistics scale economies, purchase-data advantage in advertising, and Prime loyalty.

A moat map is only honest when it names its own gaps.

The flywheel

Amazon's flywheel is the most-discussed business diagram of the internet era because it accurately describes a self-reinforcing system — and it has evolved from a retail-only loop into a multi-business machine with cross-subsidy.

The Amazon flywheel
  1. Lower prices and faster delivery attract more customers
  2. More customers attract more third-party sellers
  3. More sellers widen selection, raising customer value
  4. More traffic lifts advertising revenue (sellers pay to be seen)
  5. Ad revenue and seller fees fund logistics and technology
  6. Better logistics lowers cost per unit and speeds delivery (loop closes)
  7. Prime locks in behaviour, raising lifetime value
  8. AWS underpins all of it and throws off profit that funds the next bet
  9. AI investment improves personalisation and logistics, feeding every node

Key risks and debates

Antitrust — severity: high

Regulators allege Amazon uses marketplace dominance to suppress competition and enforce price parity, seeking structural relief up to forced divestiture. Even a legal win won't reverse the shifted political climate for platform power. This directly threatens the integrated model.

AI capex returns — severity: medium-high

The ~$100B-a-year AI infrastructure bet assumes demand keeps growing exponentially. If it disappoints — cheaper models, slower adoption — Amazon faces huge outlays with thin returns, while Microsoft and Google invest at comparable scale.

Grocery & the physical frontier — severity: medium

Groceries are the highest-frequency purchase and the best driver of lifetime value, yet Amazon holds only a few percent of US grocery. Continued failure caps its ambition to be the true 'everything store.'

Labour & scale — severity: medium

At 1.5 million employees, warehouse conditions, unionisation and wage pressure are a persistent operational and reputational drag on the most labour-intensive parts of the machine.

Why Amazon matters

Amazon is the most complete expression of a single thesis about the internet: that whoever controls the infrastructure layer of commerce, computing and information captures an outsized share of the value created on top of it. Over thirty years it built the tollbooths through which a growing share of economic activity flows — the marketplace, the cloud, the logistics network, the ad platform. For operators the lessons cut both ways: relentless customer focus, patient capital, self-cannibalisation and culture-as-product can compound into decades of advantage — and the very same logic breeds regulatory backlash, cultural calcification and the hubris of believing the flywheel is perpetual.

Frequently asked questions

What is Amazon's business model?

Not one model but four on shared infrastructure: first-party retail (thin-margin, a traffic driver), third-party marketplace and seller services (fees, no inventory risk), AWS cloud (the profit engine), and advertising (the highest-margin stream). Retail subsidises customer loyalty; the fees, cloud and ads pay for it and generate the profit.

How does Amazon make most of its profit?

Not from selling goods. AWS and advertising — together a minority of revenue — generate the large majority of operating profit, because both are near-pure software with very high margins. Retail is close to break-even and functions as the customer-acquisition and habit layer.

What is the Amazon flywheel?

Lower prices and faster delivery attract more customers, who attract more sellers, who widen selection, which improves the experience and enables scale economies that lower prices further — now extended so that AWS profit and ad revenue fund the logistics and technology that power every other loop.

What is the biggest risk to Amazon?

Two: antitrust action targeting the integrated marketplace model, and the internal risk of bureaucratic calcification at 1.5 million employees — losing the 'Day 1' urgency and willingness to cannibalise itself that made it dominant.

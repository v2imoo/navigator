Enterprise software

Oracle

The database giant that understood enterprise software's deepest truth — that once a company's critical data runs on your system, it can almost never leave — and built a famously aggressive, high-margin empire on that lock-in.

9 min read · Founded 1977

Contents

Part I — The story

The company built on the stickiest software there is

Oracle, founded in 1977 by Larry Ellison and partners, built one of the most profitable and durable enterprise-software businesses in the world on a profound understanding of a single idea: that the software running a company's most critical data is the stickiest, hardest-to-replace software there is. Oracle's foundation was the database — the system that stores and manages the core data on which a large organisation runs: its customers, its transactions, its finances, its operations. Once a company's mission-critical data lives in an Oracle database, deeply integrated into everything the business does, switching to a rival becomes extraordinarily difficult, risky and expensive. You cannot casually move the data that runs your company. Oracle understood this better than anyone, and built an empire on the lock-in it creates.

Oracle is the definitive case study in switching costs and lock-in as a moat, in the economics of mission-critical software, and in a famously aggressive approach to sales, contracting and competition. It grew from databases into a broad enterprise-software giant — selling the applications, as well as the databases, that run large organisations — and later into cloud computing, always anchored on the deep, sticky relationships it holds with the world's large enterprises. Understanding Oracle means understanding why mission-critical software is such a powerful business, why switching costs can be one of the strongest moats there is, and how Oracle's aggressive culture turned that moat into enormous, high-margin profits.

Why mission-critical software is the stickiest business

The heart of Oracle's genius is understanding the power of mission-critical software. Not all software is equally sticky. A tool a company uses occasionally can be switched with little pain; but the system that runs a company's core data and operations — its database, the software its whole business depends on — is a different thing entirely. That data is the lifeblood of the organisation, deeply integrated into countless processes, applications and workflows, accumulated over years. Moving it to a rival system means an enormous, risky, expensive migration — rewriting integrations, retraining staff, and, terrifyingly, risking the integrity and availability of the data the company runs on. The cost and risk of switching are so high that companies very rarely do it, even if a rival is cheaper or better. The data, once in, stays in.

This creates one of the strongest moats in business: switching costs so high that customers are, in effect, locked in for the long term. Oracle recognised that if it could get its database and software embedded as the system of record for a large organisation, it would hold that customer for years or decades, able to sell them more software, support and upgrades, and to raise prices, because leaving was almost unthinkable. The lesson, which applies far beyond databases, is that the stickiness of software (or any product) depends enormously on how mission-critical and deeply integrated it is — and that building your product into the critical core of your customer's operations, where switching is prohibitively costly, can be one of the most durable advantages there is.

Once the customer's data is in your database, they are never leaving. That is the whole game.(the logic at the heart of Oracle's strategy)

The aggressive machine

Oracle turned its structural advantage into enormous profit through a famously aggressive approach to sales, contracting and competition — a culture shaped by Larry Ellison's combative, win-at-all-costs intensity. Oracle became known for a hard-nosed, relentless sales operation, for complex licensing and contracts, for aggressive auditing of customers' software usage to extract more revenue, and for a fiercely competitive stance toward rivals. This aggression, built on the foundation of lock-in, let Oracle extract substantial value from its embedded customers — high prices, expensive support contracts, and more. Customers often resented Oracle's tactics and its pricing, but the switching costs meant they frequently paid anyway, because leaving was so painful. The combination of a deep structural moat and an aggressive machine to monetise it made Oracle extraordinarily profitable.

Oracle also grew through acquisition, buying up other enterprise-software companies to expand the range of mission-critical software it could sell into its locked-in customer base and to consolidate the industry. And it worked, under Ellison's intense competitive drive, to make the technical and commercial bets — including a later, hard-fought transition into cloud computing — needed to stay essential as enterprise software evolved. The honest picture includes the real cost of this approach: Oracle's aggressive tactics and pricing have made it, for many customers, a vendor they depend on but resent, and its lock-in-and-extract model, while hugely profitable, depends on a dynamic that newer, more open or cloud-native competitors (and customer frustration) continually probe and challenge.

The honest ledger: resentment, the cloud transition, and the limits of lock-in

A fair account addresses the tensions. Oracle's lock-in-and-monetise model, for all its profitability, rests on a relationship that many customers find extractive — high prices, aggressive audits, hard contracts — which creates a persistent undercurrent of customer resentment and a desire, where possible, to reduce dependence on Oracle. This matters because the shift to cloud computing opened a genuine challenge: cloud-native and more open alternatives, and the broader move to the cloud (where Oracle was initially slower than rivals like Amazon and Microsoft), gave some customers new options and leverage. Oracle fought hard to make its own cloud transition and to keep its database and applications central in the cloud era, with meaningful success — but the transition tested whether a model built on on-premise lock-in could carry into a more fluid, competitive cloud world.

The deeper question is the durability of lock-in itself. Switching costs are a powerful moat, but they are not infinite: if a vendor pushes customers too hard, or if technology shifts enough to lower the cost of switching, even deeply embedded customers may eventually move. Oracle's challenge is to keep its software mission-critical and essential — to keep earning its lock-in through genuine value — rather than relying purely on the pain of leaving, which breeds the resentment that motivates customers to escape when they can. The honest picture is of an extraordinarily profitable company with one of the strongest switching-cost moats in business, navigating the cloud transition and the perennial tension between extracting value from lock-in and keeping customers who don't feel merely trapped.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Embed yourself in the mission-critical core

Oracle built its empire by getting its database and software embedded as the system of record for a company's most critical data — the lifeblood of the organisation, integrated into everything. Mission-critical embedding is the source of the lock-in.

Benefit: Embedding your product into your customer's mission-critical core — the data and systems their whole business depends on — creates switching costs so high the relationship becomes extraordinarily durable.
Tradeoff: Becoming mission-critical raises the stakes on your reliability (a failure hits the customer's core), and customers may resist depending so heavily on one vendor.
Tactic for operators: Aim to embed your product into your customer's mission-critical core — the systems and data they can't run without. The deeper and more essential the integration, the more durable the relationship; just earn that position with the reliability such dependence demands.
Principle 2

Understand that stickiness depends on how critical you are

Oracle grasped that not all software is equally sticky — a tool used occasionally can be switched easily, but the system running a company's core data can almost never leave. Stickiness depends on how mission-critical and integrated you are.

Benefit: Understanding that your stickiness depends on how mission-critical and deeply integrated you are lets you deliberately build toward the critical core, where switching costs are highest.
Tradeoff: Becoming more critical means taking on more responsibility and risk, and not every product can credibly become mission-critical.
Tactic for operators: Assess how mission-critical and deeply integrated your product is to your customer — that, more than features or price, determines how sticky you are. Deliberately move toward the critical core where you can; the systems a customer can't risk moving are the ones that hold them longest.
Principle 3

Build switching costs into a durable moat

Oracle's switching costs — the enormous cost and risk of migrating mission-critical data and integrations — are so high that customers rarely leave even for a cheaper or better rival. Switching costs became one of the strongest moats there is.

Benefit: High switching costs are one of the most durable moats in business, holding customers for years even against superior or cheaper rivals, because the cost and risk of leaving outweigh the gain.
Tradeoff: Switching costs can breed complacency and resentment, and they're not infinite — technology or your own overreach can lower them.
Tactic for operators: Where you can build genuine switching costs — through integration, data, accumulated value — do so, because they're among the most durable moats there is. But don't rely on them alone: high switching costs hold customers against better rivals, yet resentment and shifting technology can still erode them.
Principle 4

Monetise the embedded base with more to sell

Oracle grew the range of mission-critical software it could sell — databases, applications, and more through acquisition — so it could continually sell more into its locked-in customer base, where the relationship was already deep.

Benefit: Having more to sell into an embedded, locked-in base lets you grow each relationship with far less friction than winning new customers, since the customer already depends on and trusts you.
Tradeoff: Selling ever more into a captive base can tip into extraction that breeds resentment, and expansion through acquisition adds complexity.
Tactic for operators: Once you hold deep, embedded customer relationships, build more to sell into them — expanding the mission-critical software or services you offer. Selling to a customer who already depends on you is far easier than winning new ones; just don't let it tip into extraction that breeds resentment.
Principle 5

Pair the moat with a machine to monetise it

Oracle turned its lock-in into enormous profit through an aggressive, relentless sales-and-contracting machine — hard-nosed sales, complex licensing, usage audits — built on the foundation of switching costs. The moat plus the machine produced the profits.

Benefit: Pairing a structural moat with an effective machine to monetise it (sales, pricing, contracting) turns the advantage into actual profit, where the moat alone would leave value uncaptured.
Tradeoff: An aggressive monetisation machine breeds customer resentment that motivates escape, and over-extraction can damage the relationship the moat depends on.
Tactic for operators: A moat alone doesn't capture value — pair it with an effective machine to monetise it (sales, pricing, contracting). But calibrate the aggression: extracting too hard from locked-in customers breeds the resentment that drives them to escape the moment they can, so monetise without poisoning the relationship.
Principle 6

Grow through acquisition to consolidate and sell in

Oracle bought up other enterprise-software companies to expand what it could sell into its locked-in base and to consolidate the industry. Acquisition extended its mission-critical footprint and its hold on customers.

Benefit: Acquiring companies that add mission-critical software to sell into your embedded base extends your footprint and hold on customers, and can consolidate an industry on your terms.
Tradeoff: Acquisitions are expensive and add integration complexity, and a roll-up strategy can accumulate products and resentment faster than value.
Tactic for operators: Consider acquisition to add mission-critical products you can sell into your embedded base and to consolidate your industry. Buying your way to a broader footprint within locked-in customers can be powerful — if you integrate well and don't just accumulate products and customer resentment.
Principle 7

Fight to stay essential through technology shifts

Oracle worked hard — under Ellison's intense drive — to make the technical and commercial bets, including a late but determined cloud transition, needed to keep its software essential as enterprise technology evolved. Staying essential through shifts protects the moat.

Benefit: Fighting to stay essential through technology shifts keeps your mission-critical position (and its lock-in) intact as the world changes, rather than letting a shift erode it.
Tradeoff: Technology transitions are costly and uncertain, and an incumbent can be slow to them (as Oracle initially was with cloud), risking its position.
Tactic for operators: When technology shifts, fight to keep your product essential in the new era rather than clinging to the old — your mission-critical position and its lock-in only survive if you carry them through the transition. Incumbents are often slow to shifts; don't let one erode the essential role that is your moat.
Principle 8

Keep earning the lock-in, don't just rely on the pain of leaving

Oracle's challenge is to keep its software genuinely essential and valuable rather than relying purely on the pain of switching — because a model that merely traps customers breeds the resentment that motivates escape when switching costs fall.

Benefit: Keeping customers through genuine ongoing value, not just the pain of leaving, builds a durable relationship that survives even when switching costs fall, where mere entrapment invites escape.
Tradeoff: Continually earning the lock-in through value costs more than simply relying on switching costs, and the temptation to coast on lock-in is strong.
Tactic for operators: Earn your lock-in through genuine ongoing value, not just the pain of leaving. Customers who stay because you're truly essential are durable; those who stay only because escape is costly will leave the moment it isn't — so keep deserving the position, don't just exploit it.
Principle 9

Watch that aggression doesn't become your vulnerability

Oracle's aggressive tactics and pricing made it, for many customers, a vendor they depend on but resent — and that resentment is a vulnerability, motivating customers to reduce dependence and escape where new options (like the cloud) allow.

Benefit: Recognising that aggressive extraction breeds resentment — a vulnerability that motivates escape — lets you balance monetisation against the goodwill that keeps customers willingly, not just grudgingly.
Tradeoff: Balancing aggressive monetisation against goodwill means forgoing some extractable value, and the pressure to maximise short-term revenue works against it.
Tactic for operators: Watch that the aggression which monetises your moat doesn't become your vulnerability — resented customers escape the moment they can. Balance extracting value against keeping customers who stay willingly rather than grudgingly; goodwill is a cheaper lock-in than pain, and more durable.
Principle 10

Remember that no moat, even lock-in, is infinite

Oracle's switching costs are powerful but not infinite — push customers too hard, or let technology lower the cost of switching, and even deeply embedded customers may eventually move. No moat lasts forever.

Benefit: Remembering that even a strong lock-in moat isn't infinite keeps you earning your position and watching for the shifts (and your own overreach) that can erode it, rather than assuming it's permanent.
Tradeoff: Acting on this means not fully exploiting the moat while it's strong, which sacrifices some short-term value, and the erosion can be slow and easy to ignore.
Tactic for operators: Never assume even a strong lock-in is permanent — switching costs can fall as technology shifts, and pushing customers too hard accelerates their search for the exit. Keep earning your position and watch for erosion; the strongest moats still require you to deserve them, not just defend them.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Oracle makes money

Oracle makes money by selling mission-critical enterprise software — databases and the applications that run large organisations — and, increasingly, cloud services, monetised through software licences, highly profitable ongoing support and maintenance contracts, and cloud subscriptions. Its economics are driven by the deep, sticky relationships it holds with large enterprises (whose switching costs keep them paying), the high margins on support and licences, and its ability to sell more software and services into its embedded base. The transition to cloud revenue is central to its modern economics. The lock-in is what underpins the pricing power and the profitability.

Competitive position and the moat

Oracle's moat is one of the strongest switching-cost positions in business: mission-critical data and software so deeply embedded that leaving is prohibitively costly and risky, deep enterprise integration, scale, and an aggressive sales-and-contracting machine to monetise it. This lock-in is extraordinarily durable. The pressures are customer resentment of its extractive tactics (which motivates escape where possible), the cloud transition and the more open, competitive options it brought, and the perennial need to keep its software genuinely essential rather than relying purely on the pain of switching.

The flywheel

Oracle's lock-in flywheel
  1. Get the database and software embedded as the system of record
  2. Mission-critical data deeply integrated into everything the business does
  3. Switching becomes prohibitively costly and risky — the customer stays
  4. Sell more software, support and services into the locked-in base
  5. An aggressive sales-and-contracting machine extracts high margins
  6. Acquisitions add more mission-critical software to sell in
  7. Profits fund staying essential through transitions like the cloud (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Customer resentment of extraction — severity: medium-high

Oracle's high prices, aggressive audits and hard contracts breed resentment that motivates customers to reduce their dependence and escape the lock-in where they can.

The cloud transition — severity: medium-high

The shift to cloud (where Oracle was initially slower) brought more open, cloud-native alternatives and gave customers new options and leverage — testing whether on-premise lock-in carries into the cloud.

Limits of lock-in — severity: medium

Switching costs are powerful but not infinite; pushing customers too hard, or technology lowering switching costs, can eventually drive even embedded customers to move.

Relying on pain, not value — severity: medium

A model that relies on the pain of leaving rather than genuine ongoing value is vulnerable to customers escaping the moment the cost of switching falls.

Why Oracle matters

Oracle matters because it is the definitive case study in switching costs and lock-in as a moat — in understanding that the software running a company's mission-critical data is the stickiest, hardest-to-replace there is, and building an enormously profitable empire on the near-impossibility of leaving. For operators the lessons are that the stickiness of a product depends enormously on how mission-critical and deeply integrated it is; that building your product into the critical core of your customer's operations, where switching is prohibitively costly, can be one of the most durable advantages in business; but also that lock-in breeds resentment, that a model relying on the pain of leaving rather than genuine value is vulnerable when switching costs fall, and that the strongest moat is to keep earning your essential position, not merely to trap the customer in it.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Oracle's business so sticky?

Because it runs on mission-critical software — above all the database that stores a company's core data. Once that data is deeply integrated into everything the business does, switching to a rival means an enormous, risky, expensive migration. The cost and risk are so high that companies rarely leave, even for a cheaper or better option.

What is Oracle's moat?

Switching costs and lock-in. By getting its database and software embedded as the system of record for large organisations, Oracle holds customers for years or decades, because leaving is prohibitively costly and risky — one of the strongest moats in business, which Oracle monetises through high prices, support contracts and selling more into the base.

Why do some customers resent Oracle?

Because its lock-in-and-monetise model, while hugely profitable, can feel extractive — high prices, aggressive usage audits, hard contracts. Customers depend on Oracle but often resent its tactics, which creates an undercurrent of desire to reduce dependence and escape the lock-in where new options (like the cloud) allow.

What is the main lesson of Oracle?

That the stickiness of a product depends enormously on how mission-critical and deeply integrated it is — and that building your product into the critical core of your customer's operations, where switching is prohibitively costly, can be one of the most durable advantages in business. But lock-in breeds resentment, so the strongest position is to keep earning it, not just trap the customer.

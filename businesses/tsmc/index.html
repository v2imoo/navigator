Semiconductors

TSMC

The most important company most people have never heard of — the neutral foundry that manufactures the world's most advanced chips for everyone and competes with no one.

8 min read · Founded 1987

Contents

Part I — The story

The company that makes everyone else's chips

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company is, by most measures, one of the most strategically important companies on the planet, and one of the least understood by the public. It does not design chips or sell gadgets. It manufactures the semiconductors that other companies design — the processors in your phone, the graphics chips training artificial intelligence, the silicon inside cars, data centres and weapons — and it manufactures the most advanced of them better than anyone else in the world. When you hear that a company like Apple or Nvidia makes a cutting-edge chip, in a very real sense TSMC makes it for them.

That position — the indispensable, neutral manufacturer at the centre of the digital economy — was not an accident. It was the deliberate creation of one idea, conceived by one man, that split an entire industry in two and made TSMC the quiet kingmaker of the technology age.

The idea that split the industry

For the first decades of the semiconductor age, chip companies were 'integrated device manufacturers' — they both designed their chips and built them in their own factories (fabs). Building a leading-edge fab costs billions and demands relentless reinvestment, a barrier that kept chipmaking the preserve of giants. Morris Chang, a veteran semiconductor executive, founded TSMC in 1987 on a radical proposition: a company that would only manufacture, building chips designed by others and never designing or selling chips of its own.

This 'pure-play foundry' model did something profound: it decoupled chip design from chip manufacturing. Suddenly a startup with a brilliant design but no capital to build a fab could bring a chip to market by having TSMC make it. An entire generation of 'fabless' design companies — the Nvidias, Qualcomms and, eventually, Apple's own silicon teams — became possible because TSMC existed to manufacture for them. Chang had not just founded a company; he had created the conditions for a whole new industry structure.

In TSMC's business model, we never compete with our customers. Without trust, there is no foundry business.Morris Chang

Why trust is the product

The genius of the pure-play model is as much strategic as technical. A fabless company like Nvidia hands TSMC its most valuable secrets — the design of its next-generation chip. It can do so only because it trusts that TSMC will manufacture that design brilliantly and will never use the knowledge to build a competing product of its own. The moment TSMC designed its own chips, every customer would become a rival, and the trust that makes the whole model work would collapse.

By doing only manufacturing, and refusing to compete, TSMC made itself the partner everyone could rely on. This neutrality is a competitive weapon: it allows TSMC to serve fierce rivals simultaneously — Apple, Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm all depend on it — because none fears that its chipmaker is secretly its competitor. Trust, deliberately protected, is the foundation of the business.

The treadmill of the leading edge

TSMC's deepest moat is the leading edge — the most advanced manufacturing processes, measured in ever-smaller nanometre nodes, that pack more transistors onto a chip. Staying at the front requires staggering, continuous capital investment (tens of billions of dollars a year) and the accumulation of process know-how that cannot be bought, only learned through years of painstaking iteration. Each new node is harder, more expensive and more physically extreme than the last, and the number of companies able to afford and master it has dwindled to a tiny handful.

This creates a brutal, self-reinforcing advantage. The leading-edge customers — those building the most advanced phone and AI chips — need the best process, so they go to TSMC; their enormous volume funds TSMC's investment in the next node; that investment widens TSMC's lead; which attracts the next generation of leading-edge customers. Competitors who fall behind find the gap compounding against them, because they lack the volume to fund the catch-up. TSMC's dominance of the leading edge is one of the most lopsided positions in modern industry.

The most important — and most exposed — company in the world

TSMC's centrality carries a geopolitical weight almost unique in business. Because so much of the world's most advanced chip manufacturing is concentrated in Taiwan, and because those chips are essential to everything from consumer electronics to artificial intelligence to defence, TSMC sits at the heart of the US–China technology rivalry and the question of Taiwan's security. The concentration is so acute that TSMC's fabs have been described as a 'silicon shield' — and, simultaneously, as a dangerous single point of failure for the global economy.

In response, and under pressure from governments, TSMC has begun building fabs in the United States, Japan and Europe to diversify geographically — an expensive, complex undertaking that dilutes the efficiency of its concentrated Taiwan operations but reduces the systemic risk. This tension, between the efficiency of concentration and the resilience of diversification, is one of the defining strategic stories of the age, and TSMC is at its centre.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Never compete with your customers

TSMC's founding rule is that it only manufactures and never designs chips of its own, so its customers can hand over their most valuable secrets without fear. The neutrality is the product: it lets TSMC serve fierce rivals simultaneously because none sees it as a competitor.

Benefit: Being the neutral enabler lets you serve an entire industry at once, including rivals who would never trust a competitor, making you indispensable rather than threatening.
Tradeoff: You forfeit the higher margins of the end product and tie your fate to your customers' success; you must resist the constant temptation to move up the value chain into their business.
Tactic for operators: If you're building a platform or supplier business, decide early and credibly whether you will ever compete with your customers. Choosing not to can unlock trust — and a market — that a competitor could never access.
Principle 2

Win through monastic focus

TSMC does one thing — advanced chip manufacturing — and does it better than anyone. It did not diversify into design, products or adjacent businesses; it poured everything into mastering the single hardest problem in its field.

Benefit: Deep focus on one hard thing builds a level of mastery and scale that diversified competitors, splitting their attention and capital, cannot match.
Tradeoff: Focus concentrates risk: if your one thing is disrupted or your lead is lost, you have no fallback, and you may miss adjacent opportunities.
Tactic for operators: Identify the single hardest, most valuable thing in your domain and consider pouring everything into mastering it, rather than spreading across many. The depth that focus buys can be a deeper moat than breadth.
Principle 3

Build an advantage from compounding know-how

TSMC's lead at the leading edge rests on process know-how accumulated over decades of iteration — knowledge that cannot be bought or copied from a blueprint, only learned slowly through doing. Each node mastered makes the next one more achievable, and widens the gap against anyone behind.

Benefit: Know-how that compounds through experience is among the most durable moats there is, because rivals can't shortcut the years of learning even with unlimited capital.
Tradeoff: It takes years to build and can be lost through a few missed generations or a brain drain; it also makes you dependent on retaining the people who hold the knowledge.
Tactic for operators: Invest in the kind of capability that only accumulates through doing — process mastery, hard-won operational knowledge — because an advantage your rivals can only earn through years of their own learning is one they can't simply buy their way past.
Principle 4

Let volume fund the next leap

TSMC's leading-edge customers provide the enormous volume that funds its investment in the next, even more advanced node — which widens its lead and attracts the next wave of customers. Volume is not just revenue; it is the fuel for staying ahead.

Benefit: A high-volume position in a capital-intensive business becomes self-reinforcing: scale funds the investment that protects the scale.
Tradeoff: It is a treadmill — you must keep winning the volume and keep investing, because the moment you can't fund the next leap, the flywheel runs in reverse against you.
Tactic for operators: In a capital-intensive business, treat volume as strategic fuel, not just income: the scale that lets you out-invest rivals on the next generation is often what secures your position in it.
Principle 5

Make your customers' success your success

Because TSMC only wins when the chips it makes succeed, its interests are perfectly aligned with its customers': it obsesses over yield, reliability and helping customers get their designs to market. It grows only by making others grow.

Benefit: Perfect alignment with your customers' success builds deep, durable relationships and turns customers into long-term partners rather than counterparties.
Tradeoff: Your fortunes rise and fall with your customers'; a downturn in their markets hits you directly, and you depend on their continued innovation.
Tactic for operators: Structure your business so you win only when your customer clearly wins. Alignment of incentives is one of the strongest foundations for a lasting relationship — and one of the hardest for a rival to disrupt.
Principle 6

Price for the value at the frontier

TSMC's newest, most advanced nodes — where it has the least competition and the most value to offer — command premium prices and the fattest margins. It captures the value of being the only one who can do the hardest thing.

Benefit: Charging for the scarcity and value of your most advanced capability funds the investment to extend it and rewards the risk of staying at the frontier.
Tradeoff: Premium pricing invites customers to seek alternatives and attracts competitors to the lucrative frontier; it works only as long as your lead is real.
Tactic for operators: Price your most advanced, hardest-to-replicate capabilities for the value they deliver, not just cost-plus. The premium at the frontier is what funds your ability to stay at the frontier.
Principle 7

Diversify to reduce systemic risk, even at a cost

TSMC's concentration in Taiwan is efficient but exposes it — and the world — to geopolitical and single-point-of-failure risk. Under pressure it has begun building fabs abroad, accepting lower efficiency and higher cost to reduce that systemic fragility.

Benefit: Geographic and structural diversification reduces catastrophic, correlated risk and can satisfy the governments and customers who depend on you.
Tradeoff: Diversification dilutes the efficiency of concentration — duplicated operations, higher costs, harder coordination — a real and immediate price for resilience against a risk that may never materialise.
Tactic for operators: When your business becomes a systemic single point of failure, weigh the efficiency of concentration against the resilience of spreading out. Sometimes accepting a cost to reduce catastrophic risk is the mature, necessary choice — for you and your ecosystem.
Principle 8

Create the conditions for an industry, then own the keystone

By pioneering the foundry model, TSMC didn't just build a company — it made the fabless design industry possible, and then became the indispensable keystone that industry depends on. It enabled an ecosystem and positioned itself at its most valuable node.

Benefit: Enabling a whole ecosystem and occupying its keystone position creates enormous, durable value and makes you structurally essential rather than merely competitive.
Tradeoff: Your fate becomes tied to the ecosystem's health, and being the keystone draws scrutiny, dependency and the ambitions of those who would rather not depend on you.
Tactic for operators: Look for the move that doesn't just build your business but enables a whole ecosystem to form around it — then make sure you occupy its most essential, hardest-to-replace position.
Principle 9

Say no to moving up the value chain

The constant temptation for a successful supplier is to capture more value by moving into the higher-margin end product — to start designing and selling chips. TSMC's discipline in refusing that temptation, decade after decade, is what preserves the trust its whole model depends on.

Benefit: Refusing to move up the value chain preserves the neutrality and trust that may be your core asset, and keeps every customer's door open.
Tradeoff: You permanently cede the richer margins of the end product to your customers, which can look like leaving money on the table and may frustrate ambitious insiders.
Tactic for operators: If trust and neutrality are your advantage, guard them by resisting the pull to capture more of the value chain. The margin you'd gain by competing with your customers is rarely worth the trust you'd lose.
Principle 10

Treat capital discipline as a weapon

Mastering the leading edge means spending tens of billions a year wisely — building the right capacity at the right time, neither over- nor under-investing through brutal demand cycles. TSMC's ability to deploy colossal capital with discipline is itself a competitive advantage few can match.

Benefit: Disciplined deployment of large-scale capital lets you out-invest rivals through cycles without over-extending, turning your balance sheet into a strategic weapon.
Tradeoff: Get the timing wrong — too much capacity into a downturn, too little into a boom — and the sheer scale of the spending can wound you badly.
Tactic for operators: In a capital-intensive business, treat capital allocation as a core strategic skill, not a finance function. The discipline to invest heavily and well through cycles is often what separates the enduring leader from the rest.

Part III — Business breakdown

How TSMC makes money

TSMC earns by manufacturing chips to its customers' designs, charging for the wafers it produces. Its economics are driven by two things above all: the mix of leading-edge versus older processes (the newest, most advanced nodes command far higher prices and margins), and the utilisation of its enormously expensive fabs (a fab must run near capacity to justify its cost). The advanced nodes, where TSMC has the least competition, are where the profit concentrates.

Competitive position and the moat

At the leading edge, TSMC's position approaches a monopoly, with only one or two rivals able to attempt the most advanced nodes at all, and neither consistently matching TSMC's yield and scale. In older, 'trailing-edge' processes the competition is broader and the advantage thinner. The moat is a compound of process know-how (learned, not bought), capital scale (few can fund it), and customer trust (the refusal to compete) — a combination extraordinarily hard to replicate.

The flywheel

TSMC's leading-edge flywheel
  1. Leading-edge customers need the best process, so they come to TSMC
  2. Their enormous volume funds TSMC's investment in the next node
  3. That investment widens TSMC's process lead
  4. A wider lead attracts the next generation of leading-edge customers
  5. Refusing to compete with customers keeps all of them willing to come
  6. Scale and yield lower cost per chip, reinforcing the advantage

Key risks and debates

Geopolitical concentration — severity: high

So much advanced chipmaking is concentrated in Taiwan that TSMC sits at the centre of the US–China rivalry and questions over Taiwan's security — a systemic risk for the whole world economy, not just the company.

Capital intensity — severity: medium-high

Staying at the leading edge demands tens of billions a year; a stumble in funding or in mastering the next node could let a rival close the gap that the flywheel otherwise keeps widening.

Geographic diversification cost — severity: medium

Building fabs in the US, Japan and Europe reduces systemic risk but dilutes the efficiency of TSMC's concentrated Taiwan operations and raises costs.

Demand cyclicality — severity: medium

Chip demand swings with the electronics and AI cycles; fabs that must run near full to pay for themselves make TSMC sensitive to downturns.

Why TSMC matters

TSMC matters because a single strategic idea — manufacture for others and never compete with them — reshaped an entire industry and made one company indispensable to the digital world. It is the clearest case study of a business model that creates value by being the neutral enabler of everyone else: the foundry on which the fabless revolution, and ultimately the AI era, was built. For operators the lesson is that choosing what not to do — refusing to compete with your customers, focusing with monastic discipline on one hard thing — can be the most powerful strategic choice of all, and that an advantage built on compounding know-how and trust can become nearly unassailable.

Frequently asked questions

What does TSMC actually do?

It manufactures semiconductors — the chips in phones, computers, cars and AI systems — to the designs of other companies, and it makes the most advanced of them better than anyone else. It does not design or sell chips of its own.

What is the 'pure-play foundry' model?

Morris Chang's innovation of a company that only manufactures chips others design and never competes with its customers. It decoupled chip design from manufacturing, enabling the whole 'fabless' industry of companies like Nvidia and Qualcomm to exist.

Why is TSMC so hard to compete with?

Its moat compounds three things: process know-how learned over decades (not buyable), capital scale few can fund (tens of billions a year), and customer trust from never competing with them. At the leading edge this makes its position close to a monopoly.

Why is TSMC geopolitically important?

Because so much of the world's most advanced chip manufacturing is concentrated in Taiwan, and those chips are essential to modern technology and defence, TSMC sits at the centre of the US–China technology rivalry — described as both a 'silicon shield' and a global single point of failure.

Starbucks

The company that didn't sell coffee so much as a place to be — turning a cheap commodity into a premium experience and a 'third place' between home and work.

9 min read · Founded 1971

Contents

Part I — The story

Selling the experience, not the coffee

Coffee is a commodity — cheap, abundant, and available almost anywhere. Starbucks built one of the world's most valuable consumer brands by understanding that it was not really selling coffee at all; it was selling an experience, an environment, and a feeling, with the coffee as the vehicle. The company began in Seattle in 1971 as a seller of coffee beans, but its transformation came when Howard Schultz, inspired by the coffee-house culture of Italy, reimagined it as a place — somewhere you would want to be, between home and work, where the atmosphere, the ritual and the sense of belonging were as much the product as the drink. Starbucks is the definitive case study in taking a commodity and, through brand and experience, turning it into a premium product people happily pay far more for.

The core insight is that people will pay a large premium not for coffee but for what surrounds it — the comfortable space, the consistent quality, the ritual, the name written on the cup, the feeling of a small affordable luxury in the day. Starbucks is a case study in branding, in the deliberate design of customer experience, and in Schultz's concept of the 'third place' — a space that is neither home nor work but a welcoming third place where people can linger. Understanding Starbucks means understanding that a commodity, wrapped in a strong enough experience and brand, becomes something people will pay a premium for and return to out of habit and loyalty.

The third place

The strategic idea at the heart of Starbucks is Howard Schultz's concept of the 'third place' — the notion that people need, besides home (the first place) and work (the second place), a third place to go: a comfortable, welcoming public space where they can relax, meet, work or simply be. Schultz designed Starbucks stores to be that third place — with comfortable seating, pleasant atmosphere, music, the aroma of coffee, and a sense that you could linger without pressure. This reframed what Starbucks was selling. It was not a transaction (buy coffee, leave) but a destination and an environment, and people would pay a premium, and return daily, for access to it.

This idea is why Starbucks could charge far more than the cost of the coffee. The premium was for the experience and the place, not the beans. And because the experience was an environment people wanted to spend time in, Starbucks became woven into people's daily routines — the morning stop, the afternoon break, the place to meet a friend or do some work. That habit and routine is a powerful source of loyalty: a daily ritual is far stickier than an occasional purchase. The lesson is that designing an experience and an environment people want to be in — not just a product they buy — can command a premium and build a loyalty that a mere product never could.

We are not in the coffee business serving people. We are in the people business serving coffee.Howard Schultz

Consistency and scale

Having defined the experience, Starbucks scaled it across the world, and doing so required the same discipline of consistency that powers other great chains: a customer should get recognisably the same Starbucks experience — the same drinks, the same quality, a familiar environment — whether in Seattle, London or Shanghai. This consistency builds trust and makes the brand portable: a traveller knows what they'll get at any Starbucks. Achieving it at scale required systems, training and standards to deliver the experience reliably across thousands of company-run stores, while adapting modestly to local tastes and cultures. The scale, in turn, gave Starbucks real-estate density (a store on every busy corner), purchasing power, and a ubiquity that reinforces the brand and the habit.

Starbucks also, importantly, chose largely to own and run its stores rather than franchise them (especially in its core markets), unlike McDonald's — because the experience is the product, and the company wanted direct control over delivering it consistently. This gave it tighter control over the experience at the cost of the capital and complexity of operating the stores itself. The combination — a premium experience, delivered consistently, at enormous scale, through company-run stores on dense real estate — turned a commodity into a global brand and a daily habit for millions.

The honest ledger: commoditisation, saturation and the experience under strain

A fair account addresses the real tensions, several of them ironic given Starbucks' origins. The very scale and efficiency that drove growth can erode the experience that is the product: as stores optimise for speed, mobile ordering and throughput, the 'third place' of comfortable lingering can give way to a transactional coffee-to-go operation, which risks undermining the premium experience the brand is built on — a genuine, much-debated tension that recent leadership has openly tried to address by recommitting to the in-store experience. Starbucks also faces saturation in mature markets (how many stores can a city hold?), intense competition from both cheaper chains and premium independents, the challenge of maintaining a premium in economic downturns, and, in some markets, labour tensions. And coffee itself remains a commodity others can sell, so Starbucks must continually justify its premium through the experience, the brand and convenience rather than the drink.

None of this negates the achievement; it clarifies the model's central tension. Starbucks' advantage is the experience and the brand around a commodity — and its permanent challenge is protecting that experience against the commoditising, transactional pressures that scale, efficiency and convenience create. The honest story of Starbucks is the ongoing effort to keep selling an experience in a business that constantly pulls toward just selling coffee.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Sell the experience, not the commodity

Starbucks realised it wasn't selling coffee — a cheap commodity — but an experience, an environment and a feeling, with coffee as the vehicle. People happily pay a large premium for what surrounds the product, not the product itself.

Benefit: Selling the experience around a commodity lets you command a premium a commodity never could, and differentiates you in a market where the product itself is undifferentiated.
Tradeoff: An experience is harder and more expensive to deliver consistently than a product, and the premium only holds as long as the experience genuinely justifies it.
Tactic for operators: Ask whether you're really selling a product or an experience around it. A commodity wrapped in a strong enough experience commands a premium the commodity alone never could — but you must actually deliver the experience that justifies the price.
Principle 2

Create a destination, not a transaction

Schultz designed Starbucks as a 'third place' — a welcoming space between home and work where people linger — reframing it from a transaction (buy coffee, leave) into a destination people want to spend time in and return to.

Benefit: Making your business a destination people want to be in, rather than a transaction to complete, drives repeat visits, longer engagement and a loyalty that transactions don't create.
Tradeoff: A destination requires investment in space and atmosphere and sacrifices the pure throughput efficiency of a transactional model.
Tactic for operators: Consider whether you could make your business a destination people want to spend time in, not just a transaction to complete. An environment people choose to be part of builds a stickier relationship — though it costs more to create than a pure transaction.
Principle 3

Turn purchase into daily ritual

Because the experience was somewhere people wanted to be, Starbucks became woven into daily routines — the morning stop, the afternoon break. A daily ritual is far stickier than an occasional purchase, and that habit is a powerful source of loyalty.

Benefit: Turning your product into a daily ritual creates habitual, repeated custom that is far more durable and valuable than occasional purchases.
Tradeoff: Becoming a ritual requires consistent presence and convenience, and a ritual once broken (by a lapse in experience or convenience) can be hard to rebuild.
Tactic for operators: Look for ways your product can become part of customers' daily routine, not an occasional buy. A ritual embeds you in their life in a way that's hard to dislodge — habit is one of the stickiest forms of loyalty there is.
Principle 4

Deliver the experience consistently at scale

Starbucks scaled its experience worldwide with the discipline of consistency — the same recognisable experience whether in Seattle or Shanghai — so the brand became trusted and portable, with customers knowing what they'd get anywhere.

Benefit: Delivering a consistent experience at scale makes your brand trusted and portable, so customers choose you anywhere out of confidence in what they'll get.
Tradeoff: Consistency at scale requires heavy investment in systems, training and standards, and the discipline to hold the experience steady across thousands of locations.
Tactic for operators: If your advantage is an experience, invest in delivering it consistently everywhere you operate. A trusted, portable experience lets customers choose you anywhere with confidence — but consistency at scale takes real systems and discipline to maintain.
Principle 5

Own the experience where it's the product

Unlike McDonald's, Starbucks largely owns and runs its stores rather than franchising, especially in core markets — because the experience is the product and it wanted direct control over delivering it consistently, accepting more capital and complexity for tighter control.

Benefit: Owning your operations where the experience is the product gives you tight control over delivering it consistently, protecting the thing that justifies your premium.
Tradeoff: Owning rather than franchising means carrying the capital and operational complexity yourself, growing slower and heavier than an asset-light model.
Tactic for operators: Where the experience is your product, consider owning and running your operations directly rather than franchising, to control the experience tightly. The capital cost is higher, but direct control protects the consistency and quality that are your real product.
Principle 6

Charge a premium justified by the whole

Starbucks charges far more than the cost of the coffee because the premium is for the experience, the place, the ritual and the brand — the whole, not the drink. The premium is justified by everything around the product.

Benefit: Charging a premium justified by the whole experience — not just the product — captures the value of everything you've built around it, far beyond what the product alone could command.
Tradeoff: The premium must be continually justified by the experience, or customers notice they're overpaying for a commodity, and it's vulnerable when budgets tighten.
Tactic for operators: Price for the whole experience you deliver, not just the product at its centre — the environment, the ritual, the brand, the convenience. A premium justified by everything around the product captures value the product alone never could, as long as you keep delivering the whole.
Principle 7

Use scale for density and ubiquity

Starbucks' scale gave it real-estate density — a store on every busy corner — purchasing power and a ubiquity that reinforces the brand and the daily habit. Being everywhere makes the ritual easy and the brand ever-present.

Benefit: Scale that creates density and ubiquity reinforces habit (you're always conveniently there) and the brand (you're always visible), compounding the loyalty advantage.
Tradeoff: Density can tip into saturation and cannibalisation, and ubiquity can erode the premium, special feeling that scarcity preserves.
Tactic for operators: Use scale to build density and ubiquity that make your product the convenient, ever-present default — reinforcing habit and brand. But watch for saturation, and for the point where being everywhere starts to erode the premium feeling that made you special.
Principle 8

Adapt locally without losing the core

Starbucks keeps its core experience and brand consistent worldwide while adapting modestly to local tastes and cultures — holding the recognisable Starbucks experience while flexing enough to fit each market.

Benefit: Adapting at the edges to local tastes while holding the core experience consistent wins local customers without diluting the trusted, portable brand.
Tradeoff: Too much local adaptation erodes the consistency that makes the brand portable; too little alienates local customers — a delicate balance.
Tactic for operators: Hold your core experience and brand consistent while adapting modestly to local markets. The discipline is to flex enough to fit local tastes without diluting the recognisable experience that makes your brand trusted and portable everywhere.
Principle 9

Protect the experience from your own efficiency

Starbucks' central tension is that the scale, speed and mobile ordering that drive growth can erode the 'third place' experience that justifies its premium — commoditising its own differentiator. Protecting the experience against efficiency pressure is the permanent challenge.

Benefit: Consciously protecting the experience that is your differentiator from the commoditising pressures of efficiency and convenience keeps you from undermining the very thing that commands your premium.
Tradeoff: Protecting experience can mean sacrificing some efficiency and throughput, and customers increasingly want the convenience that pulls against the experience — a genuine, unresolved tension.
Tactic for operators: If your advantage is an experience, guard it against the efficiency, speed and convenience pressures that scale creates — they can quietly commoditise your own differentiator. The hardest part of an experience business is protecting the experience as you optimise for throughput.
Principle 10

Recommit to the core when you've drifted

When efficiency and transactional pressures eroded the 'third place' experience, Starbucks' leadership openly recommitted to the in-store experience — recognising it had drifted from the core and returning to what made it special.

Benefit: Recognising when you've drifted from the core experience and recommitting to it restores the differentiator that drove your success, before the drift becomes decline.
Tradeoff: Recommitting means sacrificing some of the efficiency gains that caused the drift, and admitting you lost your way, which is organisationally hard.
Tactic for operators: When growth and efficiency have caused you to drift from the core experience that made you special, recognise it honestly and recommit to that core. Returning to what differentiated you — even at some cost to efficiency — can restore the advantage that the drift was quietly eroding.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Starbucks makes money

Starbucks makes money by selling coffee, food and drinks at a premium through its stores, with its economics driven by the number of stores, the sales and traffic per store, and the premium its brand and experience command over the commodity cost of coffee. Scale brings purchasing power and real-estate density; its loyalty programme and mobile ordering drive habit, repeat visits and valuable customer data. The core logic is to charge a premium justified by experience, brand and convenience, and to multiply that across thousands of company-run stores and millions of daily rituals.

Competitive position and the moat

Starbucks' moat is a brand built on experience and consistency, enormous scale and real-estate density, and the customer habit and loyalty a daily ritual creates. Competitors can sell coffee — it's a commodity — but replicating the brand, the scale, the ubiquity and the ingrained habit is far harder. The central vulnerability is that the experience, which justifies the premium, is under constant pressure from the efficiency, speed and convenience that scale and modern ordering push toward — the risk of commoditising its own differentiator.

The flywheel

Starbucks' experience flywheel
  1. Design a 'third place' experience people want to be in
  2. The experience justifies a premium far above commodity coffee
  3. Consistency makes the brand trusted and portable everywhere
  4. A daily ritual turns customers into loyal, habitual visitors
  5. Scale brings real-estate density, purchasing power and ubiquity
  6. Ubiquity and loyalty programmes reinforce the habit
  7. Premium margins at scale fund more stores and experience (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Experience vs. efficiency — severity: high

The scale, speed, mobile ordering and throughput that drive growth can erode the 'third place' experience that justifies the premium — commoditising Starbucks' own differentiator, a tension recent leadership has openly tried to address.

Saturation — severity: medium

Mature markets face the question of how many stores a city can hold, limiting the store-growth that has driven the business.

Commodity competition — severity: medium

Coffee is a commodity others sell more cheaply or more artisanally, so Starbucks must continually justify its premium through experience, brand and convenience.

Premium in downturns & labour — severity: medium

An affordable luxury is pressured when budgets tighten, and the company has faced labour tensions in some markets.

Why Starbucks matters

Starbucks matters because it proved that a commodity, wrapped in a strong enough experience and brand, becomes a premium product people happily pay far more for and return to out of habit — and because Howard Schultz's 'third place' idea showed that designing an environment people want to be in, not just a product they buy, can command a premium and build a loyalty a mere product never could. For operators the lessons are that you may be selling an experience more than a product; that designing a space or ritual people want to be part of builds a stickier loyalty than any transaction; and that the central challenge of an experience business is protecting that experience against the commoditising pressures of scale, efficiency and convenience.

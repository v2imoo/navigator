Goldman Sachs

The investment bank whose real assets walk out of the door every night — a business of elite talent, networks, reputation and information, where the people and the relationships are the firm.

10 min read · Founded 1869

Contents

Part I — The story

The firm whose assets walk out the door every night

Goldman Sachs, founded in 1869, is one of the most prestigious, powerful and scrutinised investment banks in the world — a firm at the centre of global finance, advising governments and the world's largest companies, trading in markets, investing, and managing enormous sums of money. But to understand Goldman is to understand something distinctive about the business it is in: unlike a factory or a retailer, an investment bank owns little in the way of physical assets or products. Its real assets are its people — the elite, highly talented individuals it recruits, trains and deploys — along with its relationships, its reputation, and the information and insight it commands. As the old saying about such firms goes, their most valuable assets walk out of the door every evening and (one hopes) walk back in every morning. Goldman is the definitive case study in the business of talent, networks, reputation and information — a business where the people and the relationships are, quite literally, the firm.

This makes Goldman a profoundly instructive case, because it illuminates a kind of business whose logic differs from product or asset businesses: the professional-services and talent business, where competitive advantage is embodied not in factories or patents but in human capital, relationships and trust. How do you build a durable advantage when your key assets can quit and join a rival? How do you compete when your product is advice, judgement and access rather than a tangible good? Goldman's answers — an intensely selective, high-performance talent culture; deep, long-cultivated relationships and networks; a powerful reputation; and scale and information advantages — are a masterclass in building and sustaining a people-based business. It is also, inescapably, a case study in the power, the controversy and the ethical scrutiny that come with sitting at the center of global finance.

Talent as the business

At the core of Goldman is talent. The firm is famous for an intensely selective recruitment and an intensely demanding, high-performance culture: it hires a tiny fraction of those who apply, works them extraordinarily hard, develops them intensively, and promotes a culture of excellence, competitiveness and (in its own telling) client service and teamwork. Because its product is the judgement, skill and relationships of its people, the quality of those people is the business, and Goldman invests enormously in attracting, developing and retaining the best. The partnership culture (historically, the aspiration to 'make partner' and share in the firm's profits) created powerful incentives for talented people to commit their careers to the firm and to act, at least in principle, in its long-term interest. Talent, recruited and cultivated obsessively, is the first pillar of Goldman's advantage.

The challenge of a talent business, though, is precisely that the talent is mobile — your best people can leave and join a rival, or start their own firm, taking their skills and relationships with them. Goldman's answer is a combination of compensation (paying enough to retain the best), culture and prestige (making Goldman a place the ambitious want to be, where being a Goldman person carries status), and the fact that much of an individual's value is bound up with the firm's platform, relationships, information and brand — so that a star performer is more valuable inside Goldman than alone. The lesson, for any people-based business, is that your advantage lives in human capital, and that building the culture, incentives and platform to attract, develop and retain the best — and to make them more valuable with you than without you — is the central strategic task.

Our assets are our people, capital and reputation. If any of these is ever diminished, the last is the most difficult to restore.Goldman Sachs business principles

Relationships, reputation and information

Beyond talent, Goldman's advantage rests on three intertwined, intangible assets. The first is relationships and networks: the firm has spent over 150 years cultivating deep relationships with the world's most important companies, institutions, governments and investors — relationships that bring it the mandates to advise on the biggest deals, the access to the best opportunities, and the trust that such sensitive work requires. These relationships, built over decades, are hard for a rival to replicate quickly. The second is reputation: in finance, where clients entrust a firm with enormous sums and sensitive decisions, a reputation for capability, discretion and (the firm would say) integrity is enormously valuable — and, as Goldman's own principles note, the most difficult asset to restore if damaged. The third is information and insight: a firm at the center of global markets and deals commands information, expertise and a vantage point that inform better judgement and access to opportunity.

These three — relationships, reputation, information — combine with talent and scale into a formidable, if intangible, moat. A new entrant cannot simply hire some bankers and compete; it lacks the decades of relationships, the established reputation, the information advantages of being at the center, and the deep bench of talent. The honest picture, though, must hold the controversies alongside the capabilities. Goldman's power and its position at the center of finance have made it a frequent focus of criticism and scrutiny — over its role in financial crises, over conflicts of interest inherent in a firm that advises, trades and invests at once, over its culture and compensation, and over specific scandals. These are genuine and serious, and they bear directly on the reputation that Goldman itself identifies as its hardest-to-restore asset. A firm whose moat includes reputation is uniquely exposed to the damage that ethical lapses and scandals inflict.

The honest ledger: cycles, conflicts and reputation

A fair account addresses the real tensions of Goldman's business. Finance is deeply cyclical — investment banking revenues swing enormously with markets, deal activity and the economy, so Goldman's fortunes rise and fall with cycles far more than a steady consumer business. The firm is exposed to the risks of the markets it trades in and invests in, and to the possibility of large losses. More fundamentally, a firm that simultaneously advises clients, trades in markets, and invests its own capital faces inherent conflicts of interest — situations where its different roles and interests may collide with each other or with its clients' — and managing (and being seen to manage) those conflicts is a permanent challenge that has repeatedly drawn criticism. And, as noted, Goldman's reliance on reputation makes it acutely vulnerable to the scandals and scrutiny that its power and its conflicts invite: the very asset that is hardest to restore is the one most exposed.

None of this diminishes what Goldman is — one of the world's foremost financial institutions, a masterclass in building a durable business out of talent, relationships, reputation and information rather than physical assets — but it clarifies that a people-and-reputation business carries distinctive risks: the mobility of its talent, the cyclicality of its markets, the conflicts inherent in its multiple roles, and the fragility of the reputation on which so much depends. The honest picture is of a formidable, prestigious and genuinely powerful firm whose advantages are intangible and human, and whose greatest vulnerabilities flow from the same source — that its most valuable assets are people and reputation, which can leave, lapse, or be damaged in ways that factories and patents cannot.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Recognise when your people are the business

Goldman owns little physically; its real assets are its people, relationships, reputation and information — assets that walk out the door each night. In a talent business, the quality of your people is the business itself.

Benefit: Recognising that your people are the business focuses your strategy on attracting, developing and retaining talent, which is where the advantage actually lives in a professional-services or talent-driven firm.
Tradeoff: People-based advantages are mobile and can leave, and they're harder to protect and scale than physical assets or patents.
Tactic for operators: If your product is judgement, skill, advice or access, recognise that your people — not any asset — are the business. The central strategic task becomes attracting, developing and retaining the best, because in a talent business the quality of your people is your competitive advantage.
Principle 2

Recruit elite talent obsessively selectively

Goldman hires a tiny fraction of applicants and invests enormously in recruiting the best, because the quality of its people is its product. Obsessive selectivity in recruiting is the foundation of a talent business.

Benefit: Obsessive selectivity in recruiting ensures the quality of your people — your actual product in a talent business — stays exceptionally high, compounding into reputation and capability.
Tradeoff: Intense selectivity is costly and slow, can create a narrow or homogeneous culture, and risks missing talent that doesn't fit the mould.
Tactic for operators: In a talent business, recruit with obsessive selectivity — the quality of your people is your product, so who you hire is your most consequential decision. Invest heavily in attracting and selecting the best; a talent business can only be as good as the people in it.
Principle 3

Build a culture that develops and retains the best

Goldman pairs selective hiring with an intense, high-performance culture that develops talent and, through compensation, prestige and the partnership aspiration, retains it. The culture is how talent is cultivated and kept.

Benefit: A strong culture that develops talent and gives the ambitious reasons to stay — pay, prestige, growth, partnership — retains the people who are your advantage against rivals who would hire them away.
Tradeoff: Intense high-performance cultures can burn people out or turn toxic, and retention through pay and prestige is expensive and never guaranteed.
Tactic for operators: Build a culture that both develops your talent and gives the best reasons to stay — compensation, prestige, growth, a path to ownership. In a talent business, retaining the people who embody your advantage is as important as hiring them; culture and incentives are how you do it.
Principle 4

Make talent more valuable with you than alone

Much of a Goldman performer's value is bound up with the firm's platform, relationships, information and brand — so a star is more valuable inside Goldman than alone. Making talent more valuable with you than without you is how you retain mobile assets.

Benefit: Making your talent more valuable with you than without — through your platform, relationships, brand and information — is the deepest way to retain mobile human assets who could otherwise leave.
Tradeoff: If individuals can replicate your platform's value alone or elsewhere, the retention breaks down, and some stars will always believe they're bigger than the firm.
Tactic for operators: Build a platform — relationships, brand, information, scale — that makes your talent more valuable with you than they'd be alone or at a rival. When your people achieve more through your platform than without it, you retain mobile talent not by trapping them but by genuinely amplifying them.
Principle 5

Cultivate relationships as a decades-long moat

Goldman spent over 150 years cultivating deep relationships with the world's most important companies, governments and investors — relationships that bring the best mandates and access, and that a rival can't replicate quickly.

Benefit: Relationships and networks built over decades are a genuine moat, bringing access, opportunity and trust that a new entrant can't replicate quickly however much it hires.
Tradeoff: Relationships take a long time to build and depend on individuals who could leave, and they must be continually maintained.
Tactic for operators: Cultivate deep relationships and networks over the long term — they bring access, opportunity and trust that rivals can't quickly replicate. In a relationship business, the decades of cultivated trust are a moat; invest in them patiently, and guard the continuity when key people move on.
Principle 6

Guard reputation as your hardest-to-restore asset

Goldman's own principles state that of its people, capital and reputation, reputation is the hardest to restore if diminished. In a trust business, reputation is the most valuable and most fragile asset.

Benefit: Guarding reputation as your most valuable and fragile asset protects the trust that a finance or professional-services business depends on utterly, and that is hardest to rebuild once lost.
Tradeoff: Guarding reputation means forgoing gains that would compromise it and maintaining discipline across the firm, where any part could damage the whole.
Tactic for operators: In a trust-dependent business, guard your reputation as the hardest asset to restore — more carefully even than capital. A single serious lapse can damage what took decades to build, and unlike a factory, reputation can't simply be rebuilt; protect it above short-term gain.
Principle 7

Use information and a central vantage as advantage

A firm at the center of global markets and deals commands information, expertise and a vantage point that inform better judgement and access to opportunity. Being at the center is itself an information advantage.

Benefit: An information and vantage advantage from being central to your market informs better judgement and access to opportunity that peripheral players lack.
Tradeoff: Information advantages raise conflict-of-interest and ethical concerns (especially in finance), and must be used within legal and ethical limits.
Tactic for operators: Where your position gives you information and a central vantage on your market, use it as an advantage in judgement and access — being at the center is itself valuable. But handle information advantages within clear ethical and legal limits, because they also create conflicts and scrutiny.
Principle 8

Manage the conflicts your multiple roles create

Goldman advises, trades and invests at once, creating inherent conflicts of interest that must be managed and seen to be managed. Handling the conflicts your multiple roles create is a permanent discipline.

Benefit: Deliberately managing — and being seen to manage — the conflicts your multiple roles create protects client trust and reputation, and pre-empts the criticism and scandal that unmanaged conflicts invite.
Tradeoff: Conflicts are inherent and can't be eliminated, only managed, and the perception of conflict can damage you even when handled well.
Tactic for operators: If your business has multiple roles that can conflict — advising, trading, investing — manage those conflicts deliberately and transparently, because unmanaged conflicts breed the scandals and criticism that threaten your reputation. Being seen to manage them is as important as managing them.
Principle 9

Prepare for the cycles your business rides

Finance is deeply cyclical, and Goldman's revenues swing enormously with markets and the economy. A firm whose fortunes ride the cycle must prepare for the downturns as well as the booms.

Benefit: Preparing for the cycles your business rides — building resilience, managing risk, diversifying revenue — lets you survive the downturns that sink less-prepared rivals and emerge stronger.
Tradeoff: Cyclicality is inherent and can't be escaped, only managed, and the pressure to maximise during booms works against preparing for busts.
Tactic for operators: If your business is cyclical, prepare for the downturns during the booms — build resilience, manage risk, diversify toward steadier revenue. The firms that endure cyclical businesses are those that don't assume the good times last; prepare for the cycle you ride.
Principle 10

Accept that power invites scrutiny of a reputation business

Goldman's power and central position invite frequent criticism and scrutiny — over crises, conflicts, culture and scandals — which bear directly on the reputation it calls its hardest-to-restore asset. Power and scrutiny come together in a reputation business.

Benefit: Accepting that power invites scrutiny — and that scrutiny threatens the reputation a trust business depends on — keeps you vigilant about conduct and perception, not just performance.
Tradeoff: Scrutiny is the price of power and can't be avoided, and a reputation business is perpetually exposed to the damage that lapses and even perceptions inflict.
Tactic for operators: If your power and position invite scrutiny, and your business depends on reputation, treat conduct and perception as seriously as performance — because the scrutiny that power attracts threatens the very reputation your moat depends on. In a reputation business, how you're seen is part of what you are.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Goldman makes money

Goldman makes money across several lines: advising companies, governments and institutions on major transactions (mergers, acquisitions, raising capital) for fees; trading in financial markets (for clients and, within limits, its own account); investing its own and clients' capital; and managing money (asset and wealth management) for fees. Its economics are driven by the volume and value of deals and trading (which swing cyclically with markets and the economy), the fees its advice and management command (underpinned by its talent, relationships and reputation), and the returns on its investing — all resting on the human capital, relationships and reputation that are its real assets.

Competitive position and the moat

Goldman's moat is intangible and human: an elite, obsessively cultivated talent pool and culture; deep relationships and networks built over 150 years; a powerful reputation (its self-identified hardest-to-restore asset); and scale and information advantages from sitting at the center of global finance. These are hard for a new entrant to replicate quickly. The distinctive vulnerabilities flow from the same source — the mobility of talent, the cyclicality of finance, the conflicts inherent in its multiple roles, and the fragility of the reputation on which it depends.

The flywheel

Goldman's talent-and-reputation flywheel
  1. Recruit elite talent intensely selectively
  2. A high-performance culture develops and deploys that talent
  3. Talent plus the firm's platform wins deals and relationships
  4. Deep relationships and scale bring the best mandates and information
  5. Success and access build a powerful reputation
  6. Reputation and prestige attract the next generation of elite talent
  7. The platform makes each person more valuable inside than alone (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Cyclicality — severity: medium-high

Conflicts of interest — severity: medium-high

Reputation fragility — severity: high

Why Goldman matters

Goldman Sachs matters because it is the definitive case study in the business of talent, networks, reputation and information — a people-based business where competitive advantage is embodied not in factories or patents but in human capital, relationships and trust, and whose most valuable assets walk out of the door every night. For operators, especially in professional services and any talent-driven business, the lessons are deep: that when your product is judgement, skill and access, the quality of your people is the business, so attracting, developing and retaining the best is the central strategic task; that relationships and reputation built over decades are a genuine, hard-to-replicate moat; that you must make your talent more valuable with you than without you; and that a business built on reputation is uniquely exposed, because the asset that is hardest to restore is the one most threatened by the ethical lapses and conflicts that power invites.

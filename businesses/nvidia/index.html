Semiconductors & AI

Nvidia

The company that spent decades building the chips for video games — and discovered it had accidentally built the engine of the artificial-intelligence revolution.

9 min read · Founded 1993

Contents

Part I — The story

The accidental engine of AI

Nvidia, founded in 1993, spent most of its history as a company that made graphics chips — GPUs (graphics processing units) — primarily to render the increasingly realistic images in video games. For years it was a successful but specialised chip company, known to gamers and largely invisible to everyone else. Then, in one of the great strategic turns in business history, the specialised hardware Nvidia had spent decades perfecting turned out to be almost perfectly suited to a completely different and far larger purpose: training and running artificial intelligence. The parallel-processing power that made GPUs good at rendering graphics made them extraordinarily good at the mathematics behind modern AI, and Nvidia found itself holding the single most important hardware of the AI revolution.

But to tell the story as pure luck is to miss the real lesson, and the real genius. Nvidia did not merely stumble into AI; its leader, Jensen Huang, made long, patient, sometimes-doubted bets — on the GPU as a general computing engine, and above all on building the software that would make its chips the foundation of a new kind of computing. Nvidia is the definitive case study in positioning yourself for a wave you can't precisely predict, and in the extraordinary power of building a software ecosystem around your hardware to turn a performance lead into a near-unassailable moat.

From graphics to general computing

The pivotal strategic insight was to see the GPU as more than a graphics chip. A GPU's architecture is designed to do many calculations in parallel — ideal for the millions of simultaneous computations needed to render a 3D scene. Jensen Huang and Nvidia recognised, well before it was obvious, that this parallel-processing power could be harnessed for general computing tasks far beyond graphics — any problem that could be broken into many parallel calculations. This was a bet that the GPU could become a general engine for a new kind of high-performance computing, not just a component for games. It was not an obvious bet, and for years its biggest payoff was uncertain.

When modern AI — particularly the neural networks behind the deep-learning boom — turned out to require exactly that kind of massive parallel computation, Nvidia's long bet paid off spectacularly. Its GPUs were the best available tool for training AI models, and as AI exploded, demand for Nvidia's chips exploded with it. The lesson is not that Nvidia got lucky but that it had spent years building a capability whose full value it could not precisely foresee, positioning itself so that when a massive wave arrived, it was the company holding the essential tool. Positioning for an unpredictable future — building a capability that could matter enormously — is a strategy, not an accident.

The more you buy, the more you save.Jensen Huang (on the economics of accelerated computing)

CUDA: the software moat

The single most important and underappreciated part of Nvidia's dominance is not its chips but its software — specifically CUDA, the software platform it built to let developers harness its GPUs for general computing. Years before the AI boom, Nvidia invested heavily in CUDA, giving programmers the tools, libraries and framework to write software that runs on Nvidia GPUs. This was a long, patient investment with no guaranteed payoff, and it is the heart of Nvidia's moat. Over years, a vast ecosystem of AI researchers, developers and software grew up around CUDA — the tools, the tutorials, the accumulated code, the expertise. The entire modern AI software stack was, to a large degree, built on and optimised for Nvidia's platform.

This ecosystem is why Nvidia's lead is so durable and so hard to challenge. A rival could, in principle, build a chip as fast as Nvidia's — but the AI world's software, skills and tools are all built around CUDA, so switching to a rival chip means rewriting software, retraining people, and leaving the rich ecosystem behind. The hardware advantage can be chased; the software ecosystem and the lock-in it creates cannot easily be. This is the deepest lesson of Nvidia: that building the software ecosystem around your hardware — making your platform the default that everyone's tools and skills depend on — can turn a performance lead into a moat far stronger than the hardware alone. Nvidia sells chips, but its moat is the ecosystem.

The honest ledger: concentration, competition and the cycle

A fair account addresses the real questions hanging over Nvidia's extraordinary position. Its dominance and profitability in AI chips have been staggering, but that very success attracts intense efforts to challenge it: rivals building competing chips, Nvidia's own largest customers (the big technology companies) designing their own AI silicon to reduce dependence on it, and sustained efforts to build software alternatives to CUDA's lock-in. There is also the question of cyclicality and concentration — a great deal of Nvidia's recent growth depends on enormous AI-infrastructure spending by a relatively small number of large customers, and if that spending slows or proves to have over-built, demand could fall sharply. And Nvidia's position sits atop a complex global supply chain (it designs chips but relies on others, notably TSMC, to manufacture them) and amid geopolitical tensions over advanced chips.

None of this has yet dislodged Nvidia — its performance lead and CUDA ecosystem remain formidable — but it frames the honest question about a company riding an extraordinary wave: whether the dominance is durable or partly a function of a particular, possibly cyclical, moment of massive AI investment. The lessons from Nvidia's rise are real regardless; the durability of its peak is the open question.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Position for a wave you can't precisely predict

Nvidia spent years building the GPU into a general computing engine and investing in CUDA, bets whose biggest payoff — AI — it couldn't precisely foresee. When the AI wave arrived, Nvidia held the essential tool because it had positioned for a future it couldn't predict in detail.

Benefit: Building a capability whose full value you can't yet foresee positions you to ride a massive wave when it arrives, rather than scrambling to catch up after it's obvious.
Tradeoff: Betting on uncertain futures ties up resources in capabilities that may never pay off, and requires conviction through years of doubt before any payoff appears.
Tactic for operators: Make long bets that position you for possible large futures, not just optimise the present. You can't predict exactly which wave will arrive, but building a capability that could matter enormously means that when a wave comes, you're the one holding the essential tool.
Principle 2

See your product as more than its first use

Nvidia saw the GPU as more than a graphics chip — as a general parallel-computing engine — well before that was obvious. Reframing what its product fundamentally was opened a vastly larger opportunity than its original market.

Benefit: Seeing your product as more than its initial use case can reveal far larger markets and opportunities that competitors fixated on the original use miss entirely.
Tradeoff: Pursuing broader uses can distract from the core market and may not pay off, and the broader potential is often unclear and doubted early on.
Tactic for operators: Ask what your product fundamentally is and does, beyond its first use case. The capability you built for one market may be far more valuable in another you haven't yet seen — reframing what you really make can open opportunities many times larger.
Principle 3

Build the software ecosystem around your hardware

Years before AI, Nvidia invested heavily in CUDA — the software platform letting developers harness its GPUs. The AI world's software, skills and tools grew up around CUDA, making Nvidia the default. The ecosystem, not the chip, is the deepest moat.

Benefit: Building a software ecosystem around your product turns a performance lead into a durable moat, because switching away means leaving behind all the tools, skills and code built around your platform.
Tradeoff: Building an ecosystem is a long, patient investment with no guaranteed payoff, and it requires getting developers to adopt your platform before the ecosystem exists.
Tactic for operators: Build the software, tools and ecosystem around your product so that others' work depends on your platform. A performance lead can be chased, but an ecosystem of tools, skills and code built around you is a far deeper moat — invest in it long before the payoff is obvious.
Principle 4

Make your platform the default

The entire modern AI software stack was built on and optimised for Nvidia's platform, making CUDA the default that everyone's tools and skills depend on. Being the default means a rival must overcome not just your product but the whole world built around it.

Benefit: Becoming the default platform that an industry's tools, skills and software depend on creates switching costs so high that rivals must dislodge an entire ecosystem, not just beat your product.
Tradeoff: Becoming the default requires early, sustained investment before the ecosystem exists, and dominance as the default invites efforts (and regulation) to break the lock-in.
Tactic for operators: Strive to make your platform the default that an industry's tools and skills are built around. Being the standard everyone depends on is a moat far stronger than being merely the best product — rivals then have to move a whole ecosystem, not just win a feature comparison.
Principle 5

Invest patiently before the payoff is visible

Nvidia's bets on general-purpose GPUs and on CUDA were long, patient investments with no guaranteed payoff, sometimes doubted for years before AI made them look prescient. The patience to invest ahead of the payoff was essential.

Benefit: Investing patiently in a capability before its payoff is visible positions you to lead when the opportunity arrives, ahead of rivals who waited for certainty.
Tradeoff: Long investment before payoff ties up resources, tests investors' and the organisation's patience, and risks backing a future that never comes.
Tactic for operators: Be willing to invest patiently in capabilities whose payoff isn't yet visible, through years of doubt. The companies that lead a new wave are usually those that built the capability before it was obviously valuable — but it demands conviction and the resources to wait.
Principle 6

Design the high-value part, let others fabricate

Nvidia designs its chips and the software but relies on partners (notably TSMC) to manufacture them — keeping the high-value, high-margin design and ecosystem while others carry the capital-heavy fabrication. It owns the profitable, defensible part of the chain.

Benefit: Keeping the high-value design and software while letting partners handle capital-heavy manufacturing gives you high margins and asset-light focus on where you add most value.
Tradeoff: Relying on others to manufacture creates dependence on partners and supply-chain risk, and cedes the manufacturing capability (and its leverage) to them.
Tactic for operators: Focus on the high-value, defensible part of your value chain — design, software, the ecosystem — and let partners handle the capital-heavy rest where it makes sense. Owning where you add the most value, asset-light, can yield better margins and focus than owning everything.
Principle 7

Turn a performance lead into lock-in before it's matched

A rival could eventually match Nvidia's chip speed, but by building CUDA and the ecosystem, Nvidia converted a potentially temporary hardware lead into durable lock-in. It used the window of its lead to build a moat that outlasts the lead itself.

Benefit: Converting a temporary performance lead into ecosystem lock-in, while you still have the lead, builds a moat that endures even after rivals catch up on raw performance.
Tradeoff: Building the lock-in takes investment and time you must spend while ahead, and if you wait until the lead erodes, the window to convert it may close.
Tactic for operators: Use the window of a performance lead to build switching costs and ecosystem lock-in before rivals catch up. A hardware or feature lead is often temporary — convert it into a moat (ecosystem, lock-in, standards) that outlasts the lead itself while you still can.
Principle 8

Let the ecosystem create its own momentum

As developers, researchers and tools accumulated around CUDA, the ecosystem gained self-reinforcing momentum — more users attracted more tools, which attracted more users. Nvidia seeded a flywheel that grows on its own.

Benefit: An ecosystem with self-reinforcing momentum grows on its own as more users attract more tools and more tools attract more users, compounding your advantage without equal ongoing effort.
Tradeoff: Igniting such momentum is hard and uncertain early on, and a momentum that builds can also, in principle, shift to a rival ecosystem if neglected.
Tactic for operators: Design your ecosystem so that growth reinforces itself — more users bring more tools, which bring more users. Once that flywheel spins, it compounds your advantage with less effort — but you must seed it carefully and keep feeding it so the momentum doesn't shift elsewhere.
Principle 9

Stay paranoid at the peak

Nvidia's dominance attracts intense challenge — rivals' chips, customers building their own silicon, efforts to break CUDA's lock-in — and its growth rests partly on a possibly cyclical AI-spending wave. Sustaining success requires treating the peak as precarious, not permanent.

Benefit: Treating a peak as precarious rather than permanent keeps you investing and adapting against the challengers and cycles that threaten even dominant positions.
Tradeoff: Paranoia at the peak can tip into over-caution, and it's hard to maintain urgency when results are extraordinary and everyone is celebrating.
Tactic for operators: When you're dominant and the results are extraordinary, stay paranoid — the challengers, the customers building alternatives, the possibility that your boom is cyclical. Success at the peak is precarious; the companies that endure treat their dominance as something to defend, not take for granted.
Principle 10

Depend on partners, but know the dependence

Nvidia designs chips but depends on TSMC to make them, and sits amid geopolitical tensions over advanced chips. Its asset-light model is powerful but leaves it dependent on a complex supply chain it doesn't fully control.

Benefit: Knowing exactly where you depend on partners and external factors lets you manage and hedge those dependencies rather than being blindsided by them.
Tradeoff: Some critical dependencies (like advanced chip fabrication) can't be easily brought in-house, and geopolitical risks are outside your control.
Tactic for operators: Map clearly where your asset-light or partner-dependent model leaves you exposed — on a key supplier, a supply chain, or geopolitics — and manage those dependencies deliberately. The focus that makes you strong also concentrates your risk where you rely on others.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Nvidia makes money

Nvidia makes money by designing and selling high-performance chips — above all the GPUs and related systems used to train and run AI, now its dominant business, alongside chips for gaming, professional visualisation and other uses. Its economics are driven by the enormous demand for AI computing, the premium its performance and ecosystem command, and the fact that it designs rather than manufactures (relying on partners like TSMC), keeping the high-value design and software while others carry the capital-heavy fabrication. The CUDA ecosystem underpins the pricing power by making Nvidia's chips the default.

Competitive position and the moat

Nvidia's moat is a commanding performance lead in AI chips and, above all, the CUDA software ecosystem that makes its hardware the default for AI — around which the world's AI software, skills and tools are built. Switching away means leaving that ecosystem behind, which is far harder than matching the chip. The real pressures are rivals and customers building competing chips and software alternatives, and the concentration and possible cyclicality of the AI-spending wave its recent growth rests on.

  4. That ecosystem makes Nvidia the default and raises switching costs

Key risks and debates

Customer concentration and cyclicality — severity: high

Much of Nvidia's recent growth rests on enormous AI-infrastructure spending by a few large customers; if that spending slows or proves over-built, demand could fall sharply.

Customers building their own chips — severity: medium-high

Nvidia's largest customers are designing their own AI silicon to reduce dependence on it, and rivals are building competing chips.

Challenges to CUDA lock-in — severity: medium

Sustained efforts to build software alternatives to CUDA aim to erode the ecosystem moat that is Nvidia's deepest advantage.

Supply chain and geopolitics — severity: medium

Nvidia designs but doesn't manufacture its chips (relying on TSMC), and sits amid geopolitical tensions and export controls over advanced AI chips.

Why Nvidia matters

Nvidia matters because it is the definitive case study in positioning yourself for a wave you can't precisely predict — building, over years, a capability (the GPU as a general computing engine) whose full value could not be foreseen — and in the extraordinary power of a software ecosystem to turn a hardware lead into a durable moat. For operators the lessons are to make long bets that position you for possible futures rather than only optimising the present; to build the software and ecosystem around your product so that switching away means leaving a whole world behind; and to recognise that the deepest moat is often not the core product but the ecosystem of tools, skills and dependencies built around it.

Frequently asked questions

Why did Nvidia become so important for AI?

Its GPUs, built to render graphics by doing many calculations in parallel, turned out to be ideal for the massive parallel computation behind modern AI. As AI exploded, Nvidia held the best available tool for training and running it — a payoff of years of betting on the GPU as a general computing engine.

What is CUDA and why does it matter?

CUDA is the software platform Nvidia built, years before the AI boom, to let developers harness its GPUs for general computing. The AI world's software, skills and tools grew up around CUDA, making Nvidia the default — and that ecosystem, not the chip itself, is Nvidia's deepest and most durable moat.

Did Nvidia just get lucky with AI?

Partly it was positioned by fortune, but the real genius was positioning for a wave it couldn't precisely predict: Jensen Huang made long, doubted bets on the GPU as a general computing engine and on building CUDA. When AI arrived needing exactly that, Nvidia held the essential tool because it had invested for years ahead of the payoff.

What are the biggest risks to Nvidia?

Customer concentration and possible cyclicality (much growth rests on a few big customers' enormous AI spending), its largest customers designing their own chips to reduce dependence, efforts to build software alternatives to CUDA's lock-in, and reliance on partners (notably TSMC) amid geopolitical tensions over advanced chips.

Samsung

The Korean giant that went from cheap fast-follower to making the components inside its rivals' products — and turned vertical integration across the whole electronics stack into a formidable, if complex, advantage.

9 min read · Founded 1938

Contents

Part I — The story

From cheap imitator to the components inside everything

Samsung, founded in 1938 as a Korean trading company, became one of the most important and instructive companies in global electronics — and its story is a masterclass in transformation. For much of its earlier history, Samsung was known as a maker of cheap, lower-quality electronics, a fast-follower that imitated the products of leading Japanese and Western firms. Its rise to global leadership came through a deliberate, relentless transformation — famously driven by chairman Lee Kun-hee, who in the 1990s demanded that the company abandon its reputation for cheapness and compete on quality and innovation at the highest level. Samsung climbed from imitator to leader, becoming a top maker of smartphones, televisions and a wide range of devices, and — most strategically important — the world's leading maker of some of the most critical components in electronics, above all memory chips and displays.

This is the key to understanding Samsung: it is not just a maker of finished products but a vertically integrated giant that makes both the devices and the essential components inside them — and inside its rivals' products too. Samsung is the definitive case study in deep vertical integration across the entire electronics stack, in the transformation from fast-follower to leader, and in the strategic power (and the inherent tensions) of being both a component supplier to your competitors and their rival in finished goods. Understanding Samsung means understanding how controlling the whole stack, from the chip to the phone, can be a formidable advantage — and why it is also so complex to manage.

The transformation: from cheap to quality

Samsung's rise was not inevitable; it was the result of a deliberate and painful transformation. For years Samsung competed on price as a maker of inexpensive, unremarkable electronics. Chairman Lee Kun-hee concluded this was a dead end — that competing forever on cheapness against lower-cost rivals was a losing game, and that Samsung had to compete on quality, design and innovation to become a true leader. His famous exhortation to 'change everything except your wife and children' captured the depth of the cultural shift he demanded. Samsung invested massively in quality, in design, in research and development, and in the manufacturing excellence needed to make genuinely competitive, high-quality products. Over years, it succeeded, shedding its image as an imitator and becoming a leader in multiple categories.

The deeper lesson is about the danger of being stuck competing on price as a follower, and the difficulty and necessity of moving up to compete on quality and innovation. Many companies that start as cheap fast-followers never make the leap — they remain trapped at the low end, squeezed by even cheaper rivals. Samsung's achievement was to make that leap deliberately, through sustained investment and a wrenching cultural change driven from the top. It is a case study in the possibility of transformation, and in the leadership conviction required to force a successful company to abandon the formula that was working in order to reach a higher level before being trapped at the low end.

Change everything except your wife and children.Lee Kun-hee (on the depth of transformation he demanded)

Owning the whole stack

Samsung's most distinctive and powerful strategic feature is its deep vertical integration across the entire electronics stack. Unlike a company that only makes finished devices and buys its components from others, Samsung makes many of the most critical and valuable components itself — above all memory chips and advanced displays, where it is a world leader — as well as the finished products (phones, TVs) that use them. This integration gives Samsung several advantages: it can supply its own devices with cutting-edge components, control quality and supply across the chain, capture the value at multiple stages, and move fast by coordinating component and product development together. In an industry where the best components can make or break a product, controlling the supply of the best components is a formidable edge.

Even more striking, Samsung sells those critical components — memory chips, displays — to its rivals, the very companies it competes against in finished devices. This is a remarkable and double-edged position: Samsung profits from the whole industry's growth (everyone needs memory and displays, and Samsung makes them), gaining a diversified, economy-wide source of profit, while also competing against its own customers in the products those components go into. It means a rival's successful phone may still enrich Samsung, which supplied its memory or screen. This dual role — supplier to and competitor of the same companies — is strategically powerful but inherently tense, requiring Samsung to manage the conflict of selling to the rivals it is trying to beat. It is one of the most distinctive strategic positions in business, and a core reason Samsung is studied.

The honest ledger: complexity, governance and the squeeze

A fair account addresses the genuine complexities. Samsung's vast, vertically integrated, diversified structure (it is part of a larger Korean conglomerate, a 'chaebol', with interests far beyond electronics) is a source of strength but also of enormous complexity and of governance concerns. Korean chaebols, Samsung included, have faced sustained scrutiny and legal issues over governance, family control, opaque cross-holdings between group companies, and the concentration of economic power — genuine, serious issues that are part of Samsung's story and the broader debate about the chaebol model. The dual role of supplier-and-competitor, while powerful, is a permanent management tension. And in finished devices, Samsung faces a strategic squeeze common to its position: competing at the premium end against Apple's integrated ecosystem and brand, while being pressed at the lower end by cheaper rivals (notably Chinese manufacturers) — the classic difficulty of holding the middle and premium ground against both a differentiated leader above and cheaper challengers below.

None of this diminishes the achievement — transforming from cheap imitator to world leader, and building leadership in the critical components the whole industry depends on, is extraordinary — but it clarifies that Samsung's integrated, diversified model carries real tensions: the complexity and governance concerns of the chaebol structure, the inherent conflict of its dual role, and the competitive squeeze in finished goods. The honest picture is of one of the world's most formidable and distinctive electronics companies, whose vertical integration is both its greatest strength and the root of its greatest complexities.

Part II — The playbook

Principle 1

Escape the fast-follower trap before it closes

Samsung, long a cheap imitator, concluded that competing forever on price as a follower was a dead end, and transformed — through massive investment and cultural change — to compete on quality and innovation. Many followers never make that leap and stay trapped at the low end.

Benefit: Moving up from price-competing follower to quality-and-innovation leader, before cheaper rivals squeeze you out, is the escape from the fast-follower trap that few manage.
Tradeoff: The transformation is wrenching and expensive, requires abandoning a formula that's working, and faces fierce internal resistance and real risk of failure.
Tactic for operators: If you're competing as a cheap fast-follower, recognise it as a trap and plan the leap to competing on quality and innovation before even cheaper rivals squeeze you out. The move up is wrenching and risky — but staying at the low end, where you're always undercut, is often worse.
Principle 2

Drive transformation from the top with conviction

Samsung's transformation was driven by chairman Lee Kun-hee's uncompromising conviction — 'change everything except your wife and children' — forcing a successful company through a painful cultural shift it would not have made on its own.

Benefit: A deep transformation of a working company usually requires uncompromising conviction and force from the top, because the organisation won't abandon a successful formula on its own.
Tradeoff: Forcing wrenching change on a company that's succeeding is enormously difficult, risks disruption and resistance, and depends on leadership judgement that could be wrong.
Tactic for operators: Deep transformation of a company that's still succeeding usually has to be driven from the top with uncompromising conviction — organisations don't abandon a working formula willingly. If the change is truly necessary, lead it forcefully; half-measures and consensus rarely achieve a wrenching shift.
Principle 3

Own the critical components your product depends on

Samsung makes the critical, high-value components — memory chips, displays — inside its devices, rather than buying them from others. Controlling the supply of the best components, where they can make or break a product, is a formidable edge.

Benefit: Owning the critical components your product depends on gives you cost advantage, quality control, supply security, and the speed of developing component and product together.
Tradeoff: Building component manufacturing is enormously capital-intensive and hard, and it only pays where the component genuinely differentiates or constrains your product.
Tactic for operators: Where a critical component can make or break your product, consider making it yourself rather than depending on others. Controlling the supply of the best components — for cost, quality, supply and speed — can be a formidable edge; just weigh the enormous investment it requires.
Principle 4

Integrate across the whole stack for speed and value

Samsung controls the whole electronics stack from chip to finished device, letting it capture value at multiple stages, coordinate component and product development, and move fast. Owning the stack is a distinctive source of advantage.

Benefit: Deep vertical integration across the stack captures value at every stage, enables fast coordinated development, and gives control over cost and quality that an assembler of others' parts lacks.
Tradeoff: Owning the whole stack is capital-intensive and enormously complex to manage, and it reduces the flexibility of buying the best from specialists.
Tactic for operators: Consider integrating across more of your stack where coordination and control genuinely create advantage — capturing value at each stage and developing the pieces together can be powerful. But weigh it against the capital and the sheer complexity of managing the whole chain.
Principle 5

Supply your rivals to profit from the whole industry

Samsung sells its critical components — memory, displays — to the very rivals it competes against in devices, so a competitor's successful phone may still enrich Samsung. It profits from the whole industry's growth, not just its own sales.

Benefit: Supplying critical components to your rivals lets you profit from the entire industry's growth — a diversified, economy-wide source of profit — not just your own finished-product sales.
Tradeoff: Being both supplier and competitor to the same companies is an inherent, permanent conflict that must be managed, and rivals may seek to reduce their dependence on you.
Tactic for operators: If you make a critical component, consider supplying even your rivals — you profit from the whole industry's growth, not just your own product. It's a powerful, diversified position, but managing the conflict of selling to the companies you're trying to beat is a permanent tension.
Principle 6

Turn component leadership into a diversified profit base

Because everyone needs memory and displays, Samsung's component leadership gives it a profit base tied to the whole electronics economy, diversifying beyond the fortunes of its own devices — though the commodity component cycle brings its own volatility.

Benefit: A leadership position in a critical, universally needed component gives you a diversified profit base tied to a whole industry, insulating you from the fortunes of any single product.
Tradeoff: Commodity components (like memory) are often cyclical, swinging your profits with the cycle, and leadership requires continuous heavy investment to maintain.
Tactic for operators: A leadership position in a component the whole industry needs can diversify your profits across an entire economy, beyond your own products. It's a resilient base — though commodity components are often cyclical, so expect the volatility that comes with it.
Principle 7

Fund relentless investment to hold component leadership

Samsung's component and device profits fund the massive, continuous R&D and manufacturing investment needed to stay ahead in memory and displays — leadership that must be constantly re-earned through investment.

Benefit: Funding relentless investment to maintain component leadership keeps rivals from catching up in a field where the lead must be continuously re-earned at the technological frontier.
Tradeoff: The investment is enormous and never-ending, and a stumble or a cyclical downturn can starve it, letting rivals close the gap.
Tactic for operators: In a frontier component business, treat relentless investment as the price of leadership — the lead must be continuously re-earned, and underinvesting (especially in a downturn) lets rivals catch up. Fund the frontier from your profits, because standing still means falling behind.
Principle 8

Manage the squeeze between premium and cheap

In devices, Samsung is pressed between Apple's differentiated premium ecosystem above and cheaper Chinese rivals below — the classic difficulty of holding the middle and premium ground against both a differentiated leader and cheaper challengers.

Benefit: Recognising the squeeze between a differentiated premium leader and cheaper challengers lets you defend deliberately — through your own differentiation, scale and integration — rather than being ground between them.
Tradeoff: Holding the middle and premium ground against both ends is genuinely hard, and you can be out-differentiated above and undercut below simultaneously.
Tactic for operators: If you're squeezed between a differentiated premium leader and cheaper challengers, defend deliberately with your own sources of advantage — integration, scale, specific differentiation — rather than trying to beat the premium brand at its game and the cheap rivals at theirs. Know which ground you can actually hold.
Principle 9

Build diversification, but manage its complexity and governance

Samsung's vast, diversified chaebol structure is a source of strength but also of enormous complexity and serious governance concerns — over family control, opaque cross-holdings and concentrated power. The diversification and its risks come together.

Benefit: Diversification across many businesses provides resilience and a funding engine, but its complexity and governance challenges must be managed as seriously as the advantages, or they undermine the whole.
Tradeoff: Vast diversified structures breed complexity, opacity and governance concerns that can grow beyond control and attract serious legal and reputational scrutiny.
Tactic for operators: If you diversify broadly, manage the resulting complexity and governance as seriously as you pursue the growth. Breadth offers resilience and a funding engine, but opacity, concentrated control and governance concerns are real risks that, unmanaged, can become legal and reputational crises.
Principle 10

Make transformation a repeatable capability

Samsung's history of moving from trading company to electronics, from cheap to quality, and from device-maker to component leader shows transformation as a repeatable capability — the ability to remake itself as the opportunity and the threat shift.

Benefit: Building transformation into a repeatable organisational capability lets you remake yourself as opportunities and threats shift, rather than being locked into one model until it fails.
Tradeoff: Repeated transformation is exhausting and risky, and the capability to change can tip into restless, unfocused reinvention if undisciplined.
Tactic for operators: Treat the ability to transform — to remake your model as opportunities and threats shift — as a capability to build, not a one-time event. Companies that can reinvent themselves repeatedly endure where single-model companies don't; just keep the reinvention disciplined and purposeful.

Part III — Business breakdown

How Samsung makes money

Samsung (Electronics) makes money from two broad engines: finished devices (smartphones, televisions, appliances and more) and — crucially — components, above all its world-leading memory-chip business and its advanced displays, which it sells both into its own products and to rivals. Its economics are driven by the scale and efficiency of its manufacturing, its leadership in the high-value component businesses (memory chips in particular are a major, if cyclical, profit engine), and the competitiveness of its devices. The component businesses let Samsung profit from the whole industry's growth, not just its own device sales.

Competitive position and the moat

Samsung's moat is deep vertical integration, world-leading manufacturing of critical components (memory chips, displays), enormous scale, and the ability to move fast across the whole stack — plus the diversified, economy-wide profit of supplying components to the entire industry. In components, especially memory, its position is dominant. In finished devices it is a leader but squeezed between a differentiated premium rival (Apple) above and cheaper challengers below. The complexities are the chaebol structure's governance concerns and the inherent tension of its supplier-and-competitor dual role.

The flywheel

Samsung's integration flywheel
  1. Make the critical components (memory, displays) at world-leading scale
  2. Supply its own devices with cutting-edge components, fast
  3. Sell those components to rivals too — profiting from the whole industry
  4. Component and device profits fund massive R&D and manufacturing investment
  5. That investment extends component leadership and device quality
  6. Controlling the whole stack lowers cost and speeds development
  7. Scale and integration compound the advantage (loop repeats)

Key risks and debates

Chaebol governance — severity: medium-high

Samsung's vast, family-controlled, diversified structure has faced sustained scrutiny and legal issues over governance, opaque cross-holdings and concentration of power — part of the broader chaebol debate.

Supplier-and-competitor tension — severity: medium

Selling critical components to the rivals it competes with in devices is powerful but an inherent, permanent management conflict.

Device squeeze — severity: medium

In finished devices Samsung is pressed between Apple's premium ecosystem above and cheaper Chinese rivals below — the difficulty of holding the middle and premium ground.

Memory cyclicality — severity: medium

The memory-chip business, a major profit engine, is notoriously cyclical, swinging Samsung's profits with the commodity-chip cycle.

Why Samsung matters

Samsung matters because it is the definitive case study in deep vertical integration across an entire industry stack — making both the finished devices and the critical components inside them and inside rivals' products — and in the transformation from cheap fast-follower to genuine world leader. For operators the lessons are that being stuck competing on price as a follower is a trap, and moving up to compete on quality and innovation, though wrenching, may be necessary before you're squeezed out at the low end; that controlling the critical components your product depends on can be a formidable edge; that supplying your rivals can let you profit from a whole industry's growth, at the cost of an inherent conflict; and that deep integration and diversification bring complexity and governance challenges that must be managed as seriously as the advantages they create.

Frequently asked questions

What makes Samsung distinctive among electronics companies?

Its deep vertical integration across the entire stack: Samsung makes both finished devices (phones, TVs) and the critical components inside them — above all memory chips and displays, where it leads the world — and sells those components to its rivals too. It controls the whole stack from chip to device, a distinctive and powerful position.

How did Samsung transform from a cheap imitator?

Through a deliberate, painful transformation driven by chairman Lee Kun-hee, who concluded that competing forever on price was a dead end and demanded Samsung compete on quality and innovation — 'change everything except your wife and children'. Massive investment in quality, design and R&D turned it from imitator into a world leader.

Why does Samsung sell components to its competitors?

Because it profits from the whole industry's growth, not just its own devices. Everyone needs memory chips and displays, and Samsung makes the best — so a rival's successful phone can still enrich Samsung, which supplied its memory or screen. It's a powerful, diversified position, though being supplier and competitor at once is an inherent tension.

What are the main challenges Samsung faces?

The governance concerns of its vast family-controlled chaebol structure; the permanent tension of being both supplier to and competitor of the same companies; the squeeze in devices between Apple's premium ecosystem above and cheaper Chinese rivals below; and the cyclicality of the memory-chip business that is a major profit engine.

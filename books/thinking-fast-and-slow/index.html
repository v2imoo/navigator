Thinking, Fast and Slow

The two systems of the mind — and the biases that make smart people think badly.

by Daniel Kahneman · 2011

Contents

Summary

Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman distils decades of research into a single frame: the mind runs on two systems. System 1 is fast, intuitive and emotional; System 2 is slow, deliberate and effortful. Most errors come from System 1's shortcuts operating where careful thought was needed.

The book is a comprehensive tour of the biases — anchoring, availability, loss aversion, overconfidence — that systematically distort judgement, and a sobering account of how hard they are to overcome even when you know them.

Big ideas

Two systems

Fast intuition (System 1) and slow reasoning (System 2) shape every judgement.

Cognitive biases

Predictable errors like anchoring and availability distort our thinking.

Loss aversion

Losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.

Overconfidence

We're systematically too sure of our judgements and forecasts.

Key lessons

  • Know when a decision needs slow, deliberate thought.
  • Learn the standard biases to blunt their effect.
  • Loss aversion distorts risk decisions.
  • Distrust your confidence, especially in forecasts.

Connections

  • Loss aversion
  • Availability heuristic
  • Confirmation bias

Frequently asked questions

What are System 1 and System 2?

System 1 is fast, automatic intuition; System 2 is slow, effortful reasoning. Errors arise when intuition handles what needed deliberate thought.

Can knowing the biases fix them?

Only partly — Kahneman is candid that awareness helps surprisingly little on its own; structure and slow thinking help more.

Nikola Tesla

The visionary who invented the electrical future — and the cautionary tale of genius without commercial control.

4 min read · 1882–1943

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Nikola Tesla was one of history's most brilliant inventors, whose alternating-current system became the foundation of the modern electrical grid, defeating Edison's direct current in the 'War of the Currents'. His induction motor and AC designs power the world to this day. Yet Tesla died nearly penniless — a genius who repeatedly failed to capture the commercial value of his own inventions.

Tesla's mind ran to grand visions — wireless power, global communication — often decades ahead of what could be built or funded. He lacked the business discipline and control that let rivals like Edison and Westinghouse turn invention into fortune. His life is both an inspiration and a warning: brilliance alone doesn't build an enduring enterprise.

Tesla's story is the classic lesson that invention and commercialisation are different skills. The inventor who cannot build a business, protect ownership, or focus on what can be delivered often watches others profit from his genius.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Pair invention with commercialisation

Tesla's genius wasn't matched by business control — and he lost the value.

Do this: Ensure someone captures the commercial value of an invention, or others will.
Principle 2

Focus on what can be delivered

His grandest visions outran what could be funded or built.

Do this: Balance visionary ambition with what you can actually deliver and sell now.
Principle 3

Protect and own your work

Tesla repeatedly failed to secure the value of his inventions.

Do this: Guard ownership and economics, not just the breakthrough itself.

Frequently asked questions

What did Nikola Tesla invent?

The alternating-current (AC) electrical system and the induction motor that power the modern grid, along with many other pioneering electrical and wireless technologies.

Why did Tesla die poor despite his genius?

Because invention and commercialisation are different skills — he lacked the business discipline and ownership control to capture the enormous value his inventions created, which others profited from instead.

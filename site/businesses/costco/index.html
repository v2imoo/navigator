Costco

The warehouse club that makes most of its profit from membership fees rather than the products on its shelves — a business model so disciplined that it turns low prices into a durable moat.

Story

Costco's model looks almost too simple to be a fortress. It sells a deliberately limited selection of goods in bulk, at margins so thin they barely cover the cost of running the stores. The profit comes from somewhere else: the annual membership fee. That single design choice reorganizes the entire business around the member's interest. Because Costco doesn't need to make money on the markup, it can pass savings through relentlessly — and the low prices are what make the membership worth renewing, year after year, at renewal rates most subscription businesses would envy.

The discipline shows up everywhere. Costco caps the margin it will take on any item, even when it could charge more. It carries a small number of stock-keeping units, which gives it enormous buying power per item and keeps operations simple. It treats employees unusually well for retail, which lowers turnover and keeps stores running smoothly. And it grows patiently, protecting the model rather than chasing every expansion.

The result is a flywheel: membership funds low prices, low prices drive loyalty and volume, volume drives buying power, buying power funds even lower prices. Each turn makes the next competitor's job harder.

The playbook — how it really works

  • Sell the membership, not the markup. Profit comes from fees, which aligns the whole company with the customer getting a genuinely good deal.
  • Self-imposed margin caps. Refusing to raise prices even when it could is what keeps the loyalty engine running.
  • Fewer SKUs, more power. A limited selection concentrates buying power and simplifies everything downstream.
  • Treat the staff well. Low turnover is a quiet cost advantage and a service advantage.
  • The renewal is the real metric. High membership renewal is the proof the flywheel is intact.

The Navigator take

Costco is the textbook case of a business where the pricing model is the moat. By making its money on membership, it can do the one thing competitors on a markup model can't sustainably match — sell at cost — and that turns low prices from a promotion into a structural advantage. Study it with the Moats framework and the Pricing-Model Chooser: the shape of how you charge can be worth more than the price itself.

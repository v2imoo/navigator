LVMH

The world's largest luxury group — a house of houses that proved luxury brands, run as a disciplined portfolio, can be one of the most durable and profitable businesses in the world.

Story

LVMH is built on a paradox: luxury depends on scarcity and heritage, yet LVMH is enormous and modern. Its answer is structure. Rather than one brand, it is a portfolio of dozens — fashion, leather goods, wines and spirits, watches, jewelry, beauty, retail — each run to protect its own identity, heritage, and sense of rarity, while the group provides capital, real estate, distribution, and management underneath.

The genius is in what the center does and doesn't touch. It supplies the unglamorous advantages of scale — prime store locations, supply chains, marketing muscle, the ability to invest patiently in a brand for decades. But it protects the thing that makes each brand valuable: the creative identity and the carefully managed scarcity that let a handbag or a bottle command extraordinary prices. Growth comes from acquiring heritage brands and nurturing them, and from the reliable, high-margin engine of luxury demand that tends to hold up even when the broader economy wobbles.

The result is a business with genuine pricing power at the brand level and genuine scale advantages at the group level — a combination that's very hard for any single-brand competitor to match.

The playbook — how it really works

  • A portfolio of moats. Each brand is its own branding moat; the group diversifies across many so no single trend can sink it.
  • Scale below, scarcity above. Centralize the boring advantages (real estate, supply chain, capital); protect the creative identity and rarity that create the premium.
  • Buy heritage, don't manufacture it. Acquire brands with real history, then invest patiently rather than diluting them.
  • Vertical control of the experience. Owning stores and distribution protects the brand and captures more margin.
  • Patience as pricing power. Willingness to invest in a brand for decades is itself an advantage competitors can't easily copy.

The Navigator take

LVMH shows that "luxury" is not one business but a repeatable architecture: brand-level scarcity stacked on group-level scale. Study it with Coco Chanel and the Moats framework to see both halves — how a single brand earns pricing power, and how a portfolio turns that into a durable, diversified machine.

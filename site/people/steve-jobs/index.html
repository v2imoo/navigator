People

Steve Jobs

Co-founder of Apple, who twice reshaped entire industries — personal computing, music, and the phone — by fusing technology with design and insisting that products should be a delight to use, not merely functional.

Story

Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak started Apple in a garage in 1976, and by the early 1980s Jobs was a young multimillionaire evangelizing the personal computer. The Macintosh, launched in 1984, carried his obsessions — typography, the graphical interface, the feel of the thing — but the company faltered, and in 1985 Jobs was pushed out of the company he had founded. The wilderness years that followed were, in hindsight, his real education. He built NeXT, whose software would later become the foundation of the modern Mac, and he bought a small graphics group from Lucasfilm that became Pixar, which reinvented animated film with Toy Story.

When Apple, near bankruptcy, bought NeXT in 1997, Jobs returned. What followed is one of the great corporate turnarounds. He slashed a bloated product line to a simple grid — consumer and pro, desktop and portable — and bet the company on a series of products that each redefined a category: the iMac, the iPod and iTunes, and finally the iPhone in 2007, which folded the phone, the iPod, and the internet into one device and remade the whole industry. Under Jobs, Apple became not just profitable but the most valuable company in the world, defined by an unusual insistence that hardware, software, and services be designed together as one seamless experience.

Jobs was a difficult, polarizing leader — demanding to the point of cruelty, prone to a "reality distortion field" that bent people toward the impossible. But he paired that intensity with genuine taste and a refusal to ship anything that wasn't right. He died in 2011, having built the template for the modern technology company.

The playbook — what to study

  • Design is how it works, not just how it looks. Jobs treated the whole experience — packaging, setup, the first ten seconds — as the product. Sweat the parts customers feel.

  • Focus is saying no. His famous move on returning was killing most of the roadmap. A short list done superbly beats a long list done adequately.

  • Integrate the whole stack. Owning hardware, software, and services let Apple control the experience end to end — a durable advantage rivals couldn't copy.

  • Taste as strategy. He hired for and defended aesthetic judgment when the industry treated it as an afterthought.

  • Reinvent before you're forced to. The iPhone cannibalized the iPod. Jobs would rather Apple obsolete its own hit than let a competitor do it.

  • Tell a story. He understood that how a product is presented is part of the product — the launch, the demo, the narrative.

In his own words

"Stay hungry. Stay foolish." — Closing his 2005 Stanford commencement address.

"Focus is about saying no." — On the discipline behind Apple's small product line.

The Navigator take

Jobs matters because he proved that taste and integration are strategy, not decoration. He built a company that competed on the total experience — and defended that experience with ruthless focus and a willingness to cannibalize its own successes. Study him for the marriage of craft and business: the insistence that the details customers feel are exactly where durable advantage is built.

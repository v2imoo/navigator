Warren Buffett

The most successful investor of the modern era, who turned a failing textile mill called Berkshire Hathaway into one of the world's most valuable companies — and taught a generation to think about businesses, not stock tickers.

Story

Warren Buffett bought his first stock at eleven and filed his first tax return at thirteen. But the intellectual turn that made him came at Columbia, where he studied under Benjamin Graham and absorbed the idea that a share of stock is a fractional ownership of a real business with an intrinsic value that may differ, sometimes wildly, from its price. The job of the investor was simply to buy when price was far below value, and to wait.

For the first stretch of his career Buffett practiced Graham's "cigar-butt" investing — buying cheap, mediocre businesses for less than they were worth and taking a last puff of profit. Berkshire Hathaway itself was one of those cigar butts: a declining New England textile maker he began buying in the 1960s and later called one of his worst mistakes, even as its name became the shell for everything that followed. The upgrade in his thinking came from his partner, Charlie Munger, who pushed him toward a better idea: it is far better to buy a wonderful business at a fair price than a fair business at a wonderful price. That shift — from cheapness to quality, from statistics to durable competitive advantage — produced the purchases that defined Berkshire: See's Candies, Coca-Cola, GEICO, and later Apple.

Buffett built Berkshire into a machine with a peculiar engine: insurance. Premiums collected today but paid out years later gave him a large, low-cost pool of "float" to invest, and he compounded it patiently across decades, avoiding leverage, ignoring forecasts, and mostly sitting still. He ran a sprawling conglomerate from a small office in Omaha with no layers, trusting managers he rarely second-guessed. The results made him one of the richest people alive — and he pledged to give almost all of it away.

The playbook — what to study

  • Buy businesses, not tickers. Every stock is a piece of a company. If you wouldn't be happy owning it with the market closed for ten years, don't own it for ten minutes.

  • Circle of competence. Buffett stays inside what he understands and is untroubled by opportunities he passes. Knowing the edge of your competence matters more than its size.

  • Margin of safety. Buy well below your estimate of value, so that being wrong still leaves you whole. Graham's core lesson, kept for life.

  • Moats over momentum. He hunts for durable competitive advantages — brands, low-cost positions, switching costs — that protect returns for decades. (See the 7 Powers.)

  • Temperament beats IQ. The decisive edge is emotional: greed when others are fearful, patience when there's nothing to do, and the discipline to avoid the crowd.

  • Reputation as an asset. He manages Berkshire's good name as carefully as its balance sheet — because trust compounds too.

In his own words

"Price is what you pay; value is what you get." — On the difference between a stock's quote and a business's worth.

"Our favorite holding period is forever." — On investing in businesses you never want to sell.

The Navigator take

Buffett's genius is less about any single pick than about temperament married to a simple, durable framework applied for seventy years. He removed the two things that ruin most investors — leverage and impatience — and let compounding do the rest. Study him for the mindset: businesses over tickers, quality over cheapness, and the rare discipline to do nothing when there's nothing worth doing.

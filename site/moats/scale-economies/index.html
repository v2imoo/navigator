Moat

Scale Economies

A business enjoys scale economies when its per-unit cost falls as it gets bigger. The leader's cost advantage is one a smaller rival simply cannot match without first winning the share it doesn't have — a chicken-and-egg trap that protects the incumbent.

The core idea

Some costs don't rise in step with volume. A fixed investment — a factory, a distribution network, a research budget, a software platform — gets spread over more and more units as a company grows, so each additional unit costs less. When a business has a large enough share of its market, that spreading drives its unit costs below what any smaller competitor can achieve. The competitor faces a bind: to match the leader's costs it needs the leader's scale, but to win that scale it would have to undercut on price while carrying higher costs — bleeding money the whole way. That asymmetry is the moat.

Why it's durable

The power comes from the gap between the leader and the challenger, and that gap defends itself. A challenger who tries to buy share with low prices loses money faster than the incumbent, because its costs are higher. The rational incumbent can hold price, keep its margin, and let the challenger exhaust itself. The advantage is strongest where fixed costs are large relative to variable costs, and where the market isn't so enormous that many players can all reach efficient scale.

Where it shows up

Retail and distribution (spreading warehouse and logistics costs), heavy manufacturing, and any business with a big fixed R&D or infrastructure base. Costco and Walmart are canonical: enormous purchasing and logistics scale lets them sell at prices rivals can't sustain.

How to build and test it

  • Find the fixed cost. Identify the large, share-sensitive cost in your business — the thing that gets cheaper per unit as you grow.
  • Win a defensible slice first. Scale economies reward density and focus before sprawl; dominate a segment or region before expanding.
  • Test it honestly: if a competitor doubled in size, would their unit costs fall meaningfully below yours? If not, you may have size without a scale advantage.

The limit

Scale economies eventually flatten — past a certain size, being bigger stops making you cheaper, and several players can all reach efficient scale. And scale in one thing doesn't automatically transfer to another. Size alone is not a moat; the cost gap is.

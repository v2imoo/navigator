Moat

Counter-Positioning

A newcomer counter-positions when it adopts a new, superior business model that the incumbent cannot copy — not because it's technically hard, but because copying would damage the incumbent's existing business. The leader's own success becomes its trap.

The core idea

Sometimes the challenger's advantage is a model the incumbent won't match, even though it easily could. Imagine a profitable incumbent built on high margins and an expensive sales channel. A newcomer arrives with a low-margin, direct model that customers love. The incumbent sees it — and freezes. Matching the newcomer would cannibalize its lucrative existing revenue, upset its channel, and shrink its own profits. So it rationalizes inaction ("that segment is small," "our customers are different") until the newcomer is too big to stop.

Why it's durable

The moat is the incumbent's rational reluctance. The bigger and more profitable the incumbent's legacy business, the more painful it is to cannibalize it — so the stronger the newcomer's protection. This is why counter-positioning so often lets small companies beat much larger, better-resourced ones: the giant's strengths are exactly what stop it from responding.

Where it shows up

Discount and direct-to-consumer models attacking full-price incumbents; streaming attacking physical media and licensing; index funds attacking active management; anything where the new model has structurally lower margins the incumbent can't stomach.

How to build and test it

  • Find the incumbent's golden goose. Identify the profitable thing your model would force them to give up.
  • Make your model genuinely better for customers — not just cheaper, but structurally aligned with what they want.
  • Test it: ask honestly, "if the incumbent copied us tomorrow, what would it cost them?" If the answer is "nothing," you don't have counter-positioning — you're just first, and they'll follow.

The limit

Counter-positioning is temporary by nature. Once the new model is clearly winning, the incumbent will eventually accept the pain and respond, or decline. The window is real but finite — the newcomer must convert it into a more durable power (scale, network, brand) before it closes.

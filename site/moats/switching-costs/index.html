Moat

Switching Costs

A business has switching costs when a customer would lose value by moving to a competitor for their next purchase. The rival must be better by more than the cost of switching just to break even — so the incumbent can hold the customer, and often a price premium, without being the best option on paper.

The core idea

Once a customer is embedded, leaving isn't free. They may have to migrate data, retrain staff, rebuild integrations, re-learn a workflow, or risk something breaking. Those costs — financial, procedural, and psychological — mean a competitor has to be better by more than the switching cost before it's worth moving. That gap is the moat: the incumbent can under-deliver slightly, or charge slightly more, and still keep the customer.

Why it's durable

Switching costs compound over time. The longer a customer uses a product, the more data, habits, customizations, and integrations accumulate around it — and the higher the wall to leaving becomes. This is why entrenched software, banks, and platforms retain customers who grumble but never leave. The advantage is captured at each repeat purchase, so it's strongest in businesses with frequent re-buying or ongoing subscriptions.

The three kinds

  • Financial: money lost by switching — deposits, loyalty status, sunk investment in equipment or licenses.
  • Procedural: time and effort to learn, migrate, and re-integrate a new option.
  • Relational: lost relationships, habits, and comfort — the human cost of change.

How to build and test it

  • Let value accumulate inside your product. Data, history, configurations, and integrations that live with you raise the cost of leaving.
  • Earn depth, don't trap. The healthiest switching costs come from genuine embedded value, not artificial lock-in that breeds resentment.
  • Test it: if a customer wanted to leave for a better rival, what exactly would they lose or have to redo? If the honest answer is "nothing," you don't have this power.

The limit

Switching costs protect existing customers but do nothing to win new ones — and they can invert against you when you try to acquire a rival's entrenched customers. Push lock-in too far and you invite resentment, regulation, and a competitor who makes switching effortless.

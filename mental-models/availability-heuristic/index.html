Cognitive bias

Availability Heuristic

We judge likelihood by how easily examples come to mind, not by actual frequency.

Contents

Overview

The availability heuristic is our tendency to estimate how common or likely something is by how easily examples spring to mind. Vivid, recent, or emotional events are more memorable, so we overrate their frequency — which is why people fear dramatic-but-rare risks (plane crashes, shark attacks) more than common-but-mundane ones (car crashes, heart disease).

The model warns that memorability is not frequency. What's easy to recall feels probable, but ease of recall is shaped by drama and recency, not by the real odds.

When to use it

Judging how likely or common something is — especially vivid or recent events.

How to apply it

Step 1

Notice the vivid example

Catch when a dramatic or recent case is driving your sense of likelihood.

Step 2

Ask for the actual frequency

Seek the real base rate rather than trusting what comes to mind.

Step 3

Discount drama and recency

Adjust down for events that are memorable mainly because they're vivid.

Step 4

Decide on data, not recall

Base the judgement on statistics, not on how easily examples appear.

Common pitfalls

  • Overreacting to rare, dramatic risks while ignoring common, dull ones.
  • Letting recent news distort your sense of what's normal.
  • Confusing 'I can easily think of examples' with 'this is common'.

Frequently asked questions

What is the availability heuristic?

A mental shortcut where we judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind — which overweights vivid, recent, and emotional events.

How do I counter it?

Look up the actual frequency or base rate instead of trusting your gut sense, and consciously discount drama and recency.

Margin of Safety

Leave room for error, because your estimate will sometimes be wrong.

Contents

Overview

A margin of safety is the buffer you build between your estimate and the point of failure, so that even if you're wrong, you don't get hurt. Engineers build bridges to hold far more than the expected load; investors buy well below their estimate of value. The principle is the same: assume your judgement is imperfect and plan for it.

The model is really about humility encoded into decisions. You don't know the future precisely, so you build in slack that absorbs the errors you can't foresee.

When to use it

Any decision under uncertainty where being wrong is costly — investing, engineering, planning.

How to apply it

Step 1

Estimate the value or limit

Make your best judgement of the true value, capacity, or timeline.

Step 2

Assume you're partly wrong

Accept that your estimate carries error you can't fully see.

Step 3

Build in a buffer

Act with a cushion — pay well below value, over-build capacity, pad the schedule.

Step 4

Size the margin to the stakes

The worse the consequence of failure, the bigger the buffer.

Common pitfalls

  • No margin at all — planning as if your estimate is exactly right.
  • So large a margin that you never act, or the project becomes uneconomic.
  • Adding margin to a fundamentally flawed estimate instead of fixing the estimate.

Frequently asked questions

How big should the margin be?

Proportional to the stakes and your uncertainty — larger when failure is catastrophic or your estimate is shaky.

Isn't a margin of safety just being cautious?

It's structured caution — a deliberate buffer sized to the risk, not vague timidity.

Hanlon's Razor

Never attribute to malice what is adequately explained by carelessness.

Contents

Overview

Hanlon's Razor advises that when someone does something that harms or annoys you, incompetence, oversight, or simple mistake is usually a likelier cause than deliberate malice. People are busy, distracted and error-prone far more often than they are scheming against you.

The model defuses conflict and improves judgement. Assuming malice triggers defensiveness and escalation; assuming a mistake keeps you calm and usually turns out to be correct.

When to use it

Interpreting others' harmful or annoying behaviour.

How to apply it

Step 1

Notice the assumption of malice

Catch yourself concluding someone acted against you on purpose.

Step 2

Ask what carelessness would look like

Consider how oversight, overload, or error could produce the same result.

Step 3

Default to the innocent explanation

Assume mistake first, absent real evidence of intent.

Step 4

Respond to the likely cause

Address a mistake with clarity, not a fight over imagined motives.

Common pitfalls

  • Applying it so rigidly you ignore genuine, repeated evidence of bad intent.
  • Using it to excuse harmful negligence that should still be addressed.
  • Forgetting that the same charity applies to your own mistakes.

Frequently asked questions

Does Hanlon's Razor mean people are never malicious?

No — it means mistake is the better default. If clear evidence of intent accumulates, update accordingly.

Why is it useful?

Assuming malice escalates conflict and is usually wrong; assuming carelessness keeps you calm and is usually right.

Part I — The story

The story

Homer is the name given to the poet (or tradition of poets) behind the Iliad and the Odyssey, the two foundational epics of Western literature, composed around the 8th century BC. The Iliad tells of rage and mortality in the Trojan War; the Odyssey of Odysseus's long, trial-filled journey home. Emerging from an oral tradition and later written down, they established narrative patterns — the hero's journey, the flawed warrior, the long road home — that storytellers have drawn on ever since.

Whether Homer was one person is debated, but the works' influence is beyond question: they shaped Greek identity, education and art, and through them all of Western literature. He is a study in the enduring architecture of story, in how the deepest human themes — war, pride, loss, homecoming — were set down once and echo forever, and in the power of narrative to carry a civilisation's values.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Build on enduring human themes

Homer's power lies in war, pride, loss and homecoming.

Do this: Anchor your work in the deep, permanent human concerns; they resonate across every age and culture.
Principle 2

Master the architecture of story

His epics set narrative patterns still used today.

Do this: Learn the underlying structures of story — journey, trial, return; they are tools that outlast any single telling.
Principle 3

Carry values through narrative

The epics transmitted Greek identity and ideals.

Do this: Stories are how cultures pass on what they value; a great narrative does more than entertain — it shapes a people.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Homer?

The poet (or poetic tradition) credited with the Iliad and the Odyssey, the two foundational epics of Western literature, composed around the 8th century BC from an oral tradition.

Why are Homer's epics so important?

They established narrative patterns — the hero's journey, the flawed warrior, the long road home — that have shaped Western storytelling for nearly three thousand years, and they formed the bedrock of Greek identity and art.

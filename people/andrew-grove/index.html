Andrew Grove

A refugee who survived the Nazis and the Soviets, then ran Intel on one creed: only the paranoid survive.

Part I — The story

The story

Andrew Grove escaped Communist Hungary as a young refugee, arrived in America barely speaking English, and became employee number three at Intel, which he eventually led as CEO. He drove Intel's pivotal, painful decision to abandon memory chips and bet the company on microprocessors — a 'strategic inflection point' he navigated with ruthless clarity — making Intel the dominant force of the PC era. He was also a hugely influential management thinker.

Grove's books High Output Management and Only the Paranoid Survive taught generations of managers about output-focused management, the OKR approach that spread across Silicon Valley, and the need to constantly watch for the inflection points that can destroy or remake a business. He is a study in decisive strategic pivots, in disciplined, measurable management, and in productive paranoia as a survival skill.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Watch for inflection points

Grove built Intel's survival on spotting strategic shifts early.

Do this: Constantly scan for the '10x' changes that can remake or destroy your business; see the inflection before it hits.
Principle 2

Make the hard pivot decisively

He abandoned Intel's original business to seize the future.

Do this: When the data says your core business is dying, pivot hard and fast rather than clinging to what made you.
Principle 3

Manage for measurable output

His management method focused on results and OKRs.

Do this: Define clear objectives and measurable key results; manage the output, not the activity.

In their words

Only the paranoid survive.
Success breeds complacency. Complacency breeds failure.
Bad companies are destroyed by crises; good companies survive them; great companies are improved by them.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Andrew Grove?

A Hungarian refugee who became Intel's third employee and later its CEO, driving its pivotal shift from memory chips to microprocessors and becoming one of history's most influential management thinkers.

What is a 'strategic inflection point'?

Grove's term for a major shift — often a 10x change in technology or competition — that can destroy or remake a business; his core management discipline was spotting and navigating these decisively.

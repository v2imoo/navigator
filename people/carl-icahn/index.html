Carl Icahn

The original corporate raider who forces change by buying in and demanding it.

4 min read · 1968–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Carl Icahn built one of the great fortunes in finance by pioneering activist investing: buying large stakes in companies he believes are badly run or undervalued, then using that ownership to force change — replacing management, breaking up conglomerates, returning cash to shareholders, or pushing for a sale. In the 1980s he was the archetypal 'corporate raider'; today the gentler term is 'activist investor', but the method is the same.

Icahn's core belief is that many companies destroy value through complacent or entrenched management, and that a determined outside shareholder can unlock it. His campaigns are combative and public, and his track record made him a figure both feared and courted in boardrooms for decades.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Find value trapped by bad management

Icahn targets undervalued firms with poor or entrenched leadership.

Do this: Look for value hidden by complacent management that a push could unlock.
Principle 2

Use ownership as leverage

He buys enough to force change, not just to profit passively.

Do this: Where you have a stake and a case, use ownership to drive the change you want.
Principle 3

Be willing to fight publicly

Icahn's combative, public campaigns pressure boards.

Do this: Sometimes public pressure is the tool that moves an entrenched organisation.

Frequently asked questions

What is activist investing, in Icahn's style?

Buying a large stake in a company you believe is undervalued or badly run, then using that ownership to force change — new management, breakups, cash returns, or a sale — to unlock value.

Was Carl Icahn a 'corporate raider'?

Yes — in the 1980s he was the archetypal raider; the modern, gentler term is 'activist investor', but the method of buying in and forcing change is the same.

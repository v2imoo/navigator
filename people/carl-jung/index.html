Carl Jung

He mapped the hidden territory of the psyche — the shadow, the archetypes, the collective unconscious.

4 min read · 1900s–1961

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Carl Jung, once Freud's heir apparent, broke away to found his own school of analytical psychology and to map the deeper structures of the mind. He proposed the 'collective unconscious' — a layer of the psyche shared across humanity, populated by 'archetypes', universal symbolic patterns like the hero, the mother and the shadow. He coined concepts now woven into everyday language: introversion and extraversion, the complex, synchronicity, and individuation — the lifelong process of becoming whole.

Central to Jung's work is the 'shadow', the disowned parts of ourselves that we must confront and integrate rather than repress. He is a study in exploring the parts of the self we'd rather ignore, in the idea that wholeness comes from integration rather than perfection, and in a view of the mind as a rich, symbolic inner world.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Face your shadow

Jung said we must integrate, not repress, our disowned parts.

Do this: Confront the parts of yourself you'd rather deny; what you refuse to see runs you from the dark.
Principle 2

Pursue wholeness, not perfection

Individuation is about integration, not flawlessness.

Do this: Aim to become whole — to integrate your contradictions — rather than to be perfect or one-sided.
Principle 3

Read the symbols

He took dreams, myths and symbols as meaningful data.

Do this: Pay attention to the recurring patterns and symbols in your life; they often carry information reason overlooks.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

Who was Carl Jung?

A Swiss psychiatrist, once Freud's heir apparent, who founded analytical psychology and introduced the collective unconscious, archetypes, the shadow, introversion/extraversion, and individuation.

What is the 'shadow' in Jungian psychology?

The disowned, repressed parts of ourselves; Jung held that psychological wholeness requires confronting and integrating the shadow rather than denying it.

Salman Rushdie

Part I — The story

The story

Salman Rushdie won the Booker Prize for Midnight's Children, a dazzling, magical-realist saga tying the life of a boy born at the moment of India's independence to the fate of the nation — a landmark of postcolonial literature. His later novel The Satanic Verses provoked outrage in parts of the Muslim world and a 1989 fatwa calling for his death, forcing him into years of hiding and making him, against his will, a global symbol of the fight for free expression.

Rushdie survived a serious knife attack in 2022 and wrote about it in his 2024 memoir Knife, continuing to write and defend artistic freedom. He is a study in fusing history, politics and fable into exuberant fiction, in the stakes of free expression, and in resilience and defiance in the face of threat.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Fuse history and fable

Rushdie braided real history with magical storytelling.

Do this: Blend the factual and the fantastical to tell a bigger truth; the mix can illuminate history better than either alone.
Principle 2

Defend the freedom to speak

He became a symbol of free expression under threat.

Do this: The right to say difficult things is foundational; defend it even, and especially, when it carries real cost.
Principle 3

Persist with defiance

He kept writing through decades of danger.

Do this: Threat and adversity test conviction; continuing the work itself can be the most powerful form of defiance.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Salman Rushdie significant?

For Midnight's Children, a landmark of postcolonial magical-realist fiction, and for becoming a global symbol of free expression after The Satanic Verses provoked a 1989 fatwa that forced him into years of hiding.

What happened to Rushdie in 2022?

He survived a serious knife attack on stage in New York and later wrote about the experience and his recovery in his 2024 memoir Knife, continuing to write and defend artistic freedom.

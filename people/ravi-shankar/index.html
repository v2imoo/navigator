Ravi Shankar

He made the sitar a global instrument — bridging Indian classical tradition and the West without diluting either.

4 min read · 1940s–2012

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Ravi Shankar was the sitar virtuoso who introduced Indian classical music to the wider world. A master of the demanding North Indian classical tradition, he collaborated with Western figures including George Harrison of the Beatles and the violinist Yehudi Menuhin, performed at Woodstock and the Monterey Pop Festival, and composed for film and orchestra — becoming, for much of the world, the face of Indian music. Crucially, he carried the tradition to new audiences without diluting its depth and discipline.

He insisted that the music be understood on its own terms, educating listeners rather than merely entertaining them. He is a study in carrying a deep tradition to the world on its own terms, in bridging cultures through mastery, and in the discipline beneath apparent spontaneity.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Carry the tradition on its own terms

Shankar globalised Indian music without diluting it.

Do this: You can bring a deep tradition to new audiences without watering it down; respect its integrity as you share it.
Principle 2

Bridge cultures through mastery

His depth earned him collaborators across the world.

Do this: Genuine mastery of your own tradition is what lets you bridge to others as an equal, not an imitator.
Principle 3

Educate, don't just entertain

He taught audiences to understand the music.

Do this: Bring your audience up to the work rather than dumbing the work down; respect deepens their engagement.

In their words

Our music is, in a way, the sound of the infinite.
Tradition is not the worship of ashes, but the preservation of fire.

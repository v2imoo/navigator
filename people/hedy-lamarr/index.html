Hedy Lamarr

A screen icon by day, she co-invented the frequency-hopping idea that underpins Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.

4 min read · 1930s–1950s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Hedy Lamarr was one of Hollywood's biggest stars of the 1930s and 40s — but off screen she was an inventor. During World War II, with composer George Antheil, she co-developed a 'frequency-hopping' system to stop enemies jamming radio-guided torpedoes, by rapidly switching transmission frequencies. The military shelved it at the time, but the underlying idea — spread-spectrum communication — later became foundational to Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and GPS.

For decades her scientific contribution was ignored, overshadowed by her fame as a beauty; she was recognised only late in life. She is a study in talent that refuses a single box, in ideas ahead of their time, and in the overdue correction of credit for a woman dismissed as merely glamorous.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Refuse a single box

Lamarr was both a film star and a serious inventor.

Do this: Don't let others' idea of who you are confine your talents; pursue your capabilities across every domain they reach.
Principle 2

Pursue the idea ahead of its time

Her invention mattered decades later.

Do this: An idea the world isn't ready for isn't wrong — it may be early; the value can arrive long after the insight.
Principle 3

Claim your contribution

Her credit came only after long neglect.

Do this: Document and assert your real contributions; recognition delayed by prejudice is still worth securing.

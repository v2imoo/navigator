Garry Kasparov

He ruled chess for 20 years — and his loss to a computer marked a turning point for human and machine.

4 min read · 1980s–2005

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Garry Kasparov became the youngest undisputed World Chess Champion in 1985 and dominated the game for two decades, widely regarded as one of the greatest players ever, famed for ferocious aggression, deep opening preparation and relentless competitive intensity. In 1997 he played IBM's Deep Blue supercomputer and lost a match — a watershed moment in the story of artificial intelligence, the first time a reigning world champion lost to a machine under tournament conditions.

Kasparov later became a writer and thinker on decision-making, human-machine collaboration and politics. He is a study in sustained dominance through intensity and preparation, in confronting the rise of the machines in your own domain, and in turning a career's mastery into broader lessons on how humans and computers can work together.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Prepare deeper than anyone

Kasparov's opening preparation was legendary.

Do this: Out-prepare the field; depth of preparation lets you dictate the game before it even begins.
Principle 2

Compete with relentless intensity

His aggression and will wore opponents down.

Do this: Bring sustained competitive intensity; many contests are won by the one who simply refuses to ease off.
Principle 3

Learn from the machine, don't just fear it

He turned the Deep Blue loss into insight on human-AI teaming.

Do this: When machines enter your field, study how to collaborate with them rather than only competing against them.

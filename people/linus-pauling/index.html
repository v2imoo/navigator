Linus Pauling

He explained how atoms bond, then won a second, unshared Nobel — for peace — making him uniquely decorated.

4 min read · 1930s–1990s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Linus Pauling was one of the most influential chemists of the twentieth century, applying quantum mechanics to explain the nature of the chemical bond — how and why atoms join to form molecules — work for which he won the 1954 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. He also made major contributions to molecular biology and the understanding of protein structure, and came close to solving the structure of DNA.

Alarmed by nuclear weapons testing, he became a prominent peace activist, winning the 1962 Nobel Peace Prize — making him the only person ever to receive two unshared Nobel Prizes. (His later strong advocacy of large doses of vitamin C was controversial and not supported by evidence.) He is a study in applying a deep theory across chemistry and biology, in a scientist using their authority for public causes, and in the mixed record even great minds can have outside their core expertise.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Apply deep theory broadly

Pauling took quantum mechanics into chemistry and biology.

Do this: A powerful theory mastered in one field can unlock problems in adjacent ones; carry your deepest tool across borders.
Principle 2

Use your standing for public good

He campaigned against nuclear testing.

Do this: Hard-won authority can be spent on causes beyond your field; expertise carries a platform and a responsibility.
Principle 3

Stay humble outside your expertise

His vitamin-C claims overreached the evidence.

Do this: Great standing in one area doesn't transfer; hold yourself to the evidence especially where you're not the expert.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

What is Linus Pauling famous for?

Explaining the nature of the chemical bond using quantum mechanics (1954 Nobel Prize in Chemistry) and his later peace activism against nuclear testing (1962 Nobel Peace Prize) — the only person with two unshared Nobels.

Was Pauling always right?

No — his later strong advocacy of high-dose vitamin C was controversial and not supported by evidence, a reminder that great authority in one field doesn't guarantee correctness outside it.

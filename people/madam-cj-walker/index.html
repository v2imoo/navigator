Madam C. J. Walker

Born to formerly enslaved parents, she built a haircare empire and a sales army of Black women — from nothing.

4 min read · 1900s–1919

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Sarah Breedlove — Madam C. J. Walker — was born to formerly enslaved parents on a Louisiana plantation, orphaned young, and worked as a laundress earning little. Suffering from a scalp condition, she developed her own line of haircare products for Black women, then built a company to make and sell them, becoming one of the first self-made female millionaires in America. Her genius was distribution: she trained and employed thousands of 'Walker agents' — Black women who sold door to door, gaining income and independence.

Walker became a major philanthropist and activist, funding education and civil-rights causes. She is a study in building a business from your own unmet need, in a distribution model that empowered the very people it employed, and in rising from the hardest possible start to wealth and influence through relentless enterprise.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Build from your own unmet need

Walker created products for a need she personally had.

Do this: A problem you live with yourself, unsolved by the market, can be the seed of a real business.
Principle 2

Design distribution that empowers

Her agents earned income and independence selling her products.

Do this: Build a sales or distribution model that lifts the people in it; their success becomes your growth engine.
Principle 3

Turn enterprise into uplift

She funded education and civil rights from her wealth.

Do this: Wealth built from a community can be reinvested to lift it; let success become a platform for others.

Who was Madam C. J. Walker?

Born Sarah Breedlove to formerly enslaved parents, she built a haircare empire for Black women and became one of the first self-made female millionaires in America, employing thousands of saleswomen.

How did Walker build her business?

Through a distribution model of trained 'Walker agents' — Black women who sold her products door to door, gaining income and independence — paired with her own products built from a need she personally had.

C. V. Raman

He discovered why the sea is blue — and won India its first Nobel in science, on home-built equipment.

4 min read · 1920s–1970

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

C. V. Raman became the first Asian and first Indian to win a Nobel Prize in a science, awarded in 1930 for the discovery of the 'Raman effect': when light passes through a transparent material, a small fraction of it scatters and changes wavelength, revealing information about the molecules it passed through. The insight began with his curiosity, on a sea voyage, about why the ocean and sky are blue — which he traced to the scattering of light.

Remarkably, he made his Nobel-winning discovery using simple, inexpensive, often home-built equipment, proving world-class science didn't require lavish resources. Raman Spectroscopy is now a vital tool across chemistry, physics and medicine. He is a study in curiosity about everyday phenomena, in doing great science with modest means, and in pioneering world-class research from the developing world.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Chase curiosity about the everyday

Raman's discovery began with 'why is the sea blue?'

Do this: The deepest questions often hide in ordinary phenomena; follow the childlike 'why' about things everyone takes for granted.
Principle 2

Do great work with modest means

He won a Nobel on simple, home-built equipment.

Do this: World-class results don't require lavish resources; ingenuity and focus can outrun a bigger budget.
Principle 3

Pioneer from where you are

He proved frontier science could come from India.

Do this: You don't need to be at the established centre to do frontier work; prove it can be done from where you stand.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Raman effect?

C. V. Raman's discovery that when light passes through a material, a small fraction scatters and changes wavelength, revealing information about the material's molecules — the basis of Raman spectroscopy.

Why is C. V. Raman historically significant?

In 1930 he became the first Asian and first Indian to win a Nobel Prize in a science, and he did it using simple, often home-built equipment — proving frontier science could come from the developing world.

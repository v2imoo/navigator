George Carlin

He turned comedy into social criticism, fought a Supreme Court case over 'seven dirty words', and spared nothing from his scrutiny.

4 min read · 1960s–2008

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

George Carlin transformed stand-up comedy into a vehicle for sharp social and philosophical criticism. He dissected language, politics, religion, consumerism and the hypocrisies of American life with ferocious wit and precision — most famously his 'Seven Words You Can Never Say on Television' routine, which led to a 1978 US Supreme Court case on broadcast decency. He reinvented his act across decades, growing darker and more incisive as he aged, refusing to be a mere entertainer.

Carlin treated comedy as a way to make people see clearly what they'd been trained to ignore. He is a study in using humour as a tool of criticism and truth-telling, in questioning everything including language itself, and in defending the freedom to say the unsayable.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Use humour to reveal truth

Carlin made people see what they ignored.

Do this: Comedy can slip past defences and reveal hard truths; humour is one of the most disarming tools of criticism.
Principle 2

Question everything, including language

He dissected the words people use unthinkingly.

Do this: Examine even the language and assumptions everyone takes for granted; that's where hidden manipulation lives.
Principle 3

Defend the right to say the unsayable

His 'seven words' became a free-speech landmark.

Do this: The freedom to say uncomfortable things is worth defending on principle, even when the words themselves provoke.

Frequently asked questions

Who was George Carlin?

A comedian who transformed stand-up into sharp social and philosophical criticism, dissecting language, politics and hypocrisy — famous for his 'Seven Words You Can Never Say on Television' routine that led to a 1978 Supreme Court case.

Why is Carlin significant beyond comedy?

He treated comedy as a tool for truth-telling and questioning everything, including language itself, and his 'seven words' routine became a landmark in the debate over free speech and broadcast decency.

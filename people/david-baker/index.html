David Baker

He didn't just predict how proteins fold — he learned to design brand-new ones nature never made, and won the Nobel.

4 min read · 2000s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

David Baker, a biochemist at the University of Washington, pioneered the computational design of entirely new proteins — molecules that don't exist in nature, built atom by atom to perform specific functions, from vaccines to materials to tiny machines. His lab created the Rosetta software and even a citizen-science game, Foldit, that crowdsourced protein-folding puzzles. He shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work (alongside the AlphaFold team's breakthrough on predicting protein structures).

Where others learned to read the language of proteins, Baker learned to write it. He is a study in moving from understanding nature to designing with it, in combining computation and biology into a new engineering discipline, and in the creative leap from prediction to invention.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Move from reading to writing

Baker went from predicting proteins to designing them.

Do this: The deepest mastery is creative, not just interpretive; aim to design with a system once you understand it.
Principle 2

Combine computation with your field

He fused software and biochemistry.

Do this: Bringing computational power into a traditionally wet-lab science can open an entirely new engineering discipline.
Principle 3

Crowdsource hard problems

Foldit turned folding puzzles into a game.

Do this: Some hard problems can be opened up to many minds through clever design; crowdsourcing can crack what one lab can't.

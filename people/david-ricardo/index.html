A self-made financier who proved why even the best at everything should still specialise and trade.

Part I — The story

The story

David Ricardo made a fortune as a stockbroker and financier before turning to economics, where he produced one of the most elegant and counterintuitive ideas in the field: comparative advantage. He showed that two parties both gain from trade when each specialises in what it produces relatively most efficiently — even if one party is better at producing everything. The logic underpins the case for free trade to this day.

Ricardo built economics into a more rigorous, model-based discipline, reasoning from simplified assumptions to sharp conclusions. He is a study in the power of a precise model to reveal a truth intuition misses, in opportunity cost as the key to specialisation, and in the discipline of reasoning carefully from clear premises.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Specialise by relative strength

Ricardo showed you gain by doing what you're relatively best at.

Do this: Focus on where your advantage is largest, not where you're merely good; trade for the rest.
Principle 2

Think in opportunity cost

Comparative advantage is really about what you give up.

Do this: Judge any use of time or resources by what you forgo to do it, not just its standalone value.
Principle 3

Reason from a clean model

He derived sharp conclusions from simple assumptions.

Do this: Strip a problem to a clear model; the simplified version often reveals what the messy whole hides.

Frequently asked questions

What is comparative advantage?

David Ricardo's principle that two parties both gain from trade when each specialises in what it produces relatively most efficiently — even if one party is more efficient at producing everything.

Why does comparative advantage matter?

It is the core economic case for free trade and specialisation: focusing on your relative strengths and trading for the rest makes everyone better off, which is counterintuitive but mathematically sound.

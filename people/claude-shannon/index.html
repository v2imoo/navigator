Claude Shannon

The mathematician who invented information theory — the foundation of the entire digital age.

4 min read · 1937–2001

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Claude Shannon single-handedly created information theory, the mathematical foundation on which the entire digital world rests. In a landmark 1948 paper he showed that all information — text, sound, images — could be represented in bits, and established the fundamental limits of how information can be compressed and transmitted. Every computer, phone and internet connection descends from his insight.

Earlier, as a master's student, he had shown that electrical circuits could perform logic — the idea that makes digital computers possible. Shannon combined profound theoretical genius with a playful, hands-on curiosity, building juggling machines and a mechanical mouse that solved mazes. He pursued ideas for their own sake, and in doing so laid the groundwork for computing, communications and AI.

Shannon is arguably the most important figure most people have never heard of — the quiet genius whose abstract mathematics underpins every digital device and every byte transmitted. His work is a reminder that the deepest, most world-changing ideas often come from pure, curiosity-driven thinking.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Find the deep abstraction

Shannon reduced all information to bits — one idea underpinning everything digital.

Do this: Look for the fundamental abstraction that unifies a messy domain.
Principle 2

Follow curiosity for its own sake

He pursued playful ideas that turned out foundational.

Do this: Pursue genuinely interesting problems; breakthroughs often hide in play.
Principle 3

Establish the limits

He defined what's provably possible in compression and transmission.

Do this: Understand the fundamental limits of a problem before trying to beat them.

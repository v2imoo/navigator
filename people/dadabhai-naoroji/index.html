Dadabhai Naoroji

He proved with economics that British rule was draining India's wealth — and became the first Indian in Britain's Parliament.

4 min read · 1850s–1917

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Dadabhai Naoroji, the 'Grand Old Man of India', was a pioneer of Indian nationalism who built his case against colonial rule on rigorous economics. His 'drain theory' documented how Britain was systematically extracting India's wealth — through salaries, pensions, and trade arrangements — impoverishing the country, an argument laid out in his book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India. He carried the case into the heart of the empire, becoming in 1892 the first Indian elected to the British House of Commons.

An early president of the Indian National Congress, he mentored later leaders and framed the economic argument for independence. He is a study in winning a political argument with hard economic evidence, in taking your case into the opponent's own institutions, and in patient, long-game nation-building.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Win the argument with hard evidence

Naoroji quantified the colonial 'drain'.

Do this: Rigorous data can win a political argument that rhetoric alone cannot; quantify the injustice you're fighting.
Principle 2

Take the case into the opponent's house

He entered Britain's own Parliament.

Do this: Carrying your argument into the heart of the opposing institution gives it a force outside protest never achieves.
Principle 3

Play the patient long game

His work laid groundwork decades before independence.

Do this: Foundational political change is a long game; frame the arguments and mentor those who will carry them forward.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Dadabhai Naoroji?

The 'Grand Old Man of India' — a pioneer of Indian nationalism whose 'drain theory' documented how British rule extracted India's wealth, and who in 1892 became the first Indian elected to the British House of Commons.

What is the 'drain theory'?

Naoroji's economic argument, backed by data, that Britain was systematically draining India's wealth through salaries, pensions and trade arrangements — impoverishing the country and undermining the moral case for colonial rule.

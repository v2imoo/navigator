Michael Burry

A one-eyed doctor-turned-investor who saw the housing bubble no one else would, and bet a fortune against it.

4 min read · 2000s–present

Part I — The story

The story

Michael Burry, a physician who taught himself investing and ran the fund Scion Capital, did what almost no one else did before the 2008 financial crisis: he read through the actual mortgage loan documents, saw that the US housing market was a bubble built on bad loans, and bet massively against it using credit default swaps. His own investors doubted and pressured him as the bet took time to pay off — but when the market collapsed, Scion made enormous profits. His story was immortalised in Michael Lewis's The Big Short.

Burry's edge was doing the deep, unglamorous homework everyone else skipped, and the conviction to hold a lonely position under intense pressure. He is a study in independent analysis against the herd, in the willingness to be alone and doubted when the data is on your side, and in the discipline to hold through pain until you're proved right.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Do the homework no one else does

Burry read the actual mortgage documents.

Do this: An edge often comes from the deep, tedious analysis everyone else is too lazy to do; go to the primary source.
Principle 2

Stand alone when the data backs you

He held a lonely bet against the herd.

Do this: If your analysis is sound, being alone is not a reason to doubt it; the crowd is frequently, confidently wrong.
Principle 3

Endure the pressure until vindication

He held through doubt before the payoff.

Do this: A correct contrarian bet can look wrong for a long time; the discipline to hold through the pain is what captures the gain.

In their words

It is ludicrous to believe that asset bubbles can only be recognised in hindsight.

I don't have to be right about everything, only about the things that matter.

Colonel Harland Sanders

He franchised his fried chicken recipe at 62, after a lifetime of failures — proof it's never too late.

4 min read · 1950s–1970s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Harland Sanders worked a long string of jobs — farmhand, railway fireman, insurance salesman, service-station operator — and ran a roadside restaurant in Kentucky where he perfected a pressure-fried chicken recipe with a secret blend of herbs and spices. When a new highway bypassed and ruined his restaurant, leaving him broke in his sixties, he hit the road to franchise his recipe, cooking for restaurant owners and signing them up one by one. From that late, desperate start grew Kentucky Fried Chicken, one of the world's largest fast-food chains.

Sanders became the white-suited 'Colonel', a globally recognised brand icon. He is a study in persistence through repeated failure, in the power of the franchise model to scale a single good idea, and in the truth that a breakthrough can come at any age if you refuse to quit.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Refuse to let age stop you

Sanders built KFC starting in his sixties.

Do this: A fresh start is possible at any age; experience is fuel, not an expiry date.
Principle 2

Franchise to scale one good idea

He multiplied his recipe through other owners' restaurants.

Do this: When you have one proven, repeatable formula, licensing it to others can scale it far faster than doing it alone.
Principle 3

Persist through repeated failure

A lifetime of setbacks preceded his success.

Do this: Treat each failure as data, not a verdict; the breakthrough often comes after the failures most people quit on.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Colonel Sanders?

Harland Sanders, the founder of Kentucky Fried Chicken, who perfected his pressure-fried chicken recipe and began franchising it in his sixties after his restaurant was ruined — building one of the world's largest fast-food chains.

Why is Colonel Sanders an inspiring story?

He endured a lifetime of failed jobs and ventures and was broke in his sixties when he started franchising KFC — proof that persistence and a single good idea can produce a breakthrough at any age.

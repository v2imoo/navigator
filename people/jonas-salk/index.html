Jonas Salk

He developed the vaccine that ended polio's terror — then refused to patent it, asking: 'Could you patent the sun?'

4 min read · 1940s–1990s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

In the early 1950s polio was one of the most feared diseases in the world, paralysing and killing thousands of children every year. Jonas Salk developed the first effective, safe vaccine, proven in a massive 1954 field trial involving over a million children. When it was declared successful in 1955, he became an instant national hero — and then made a decision that stunned the business world: he refused to patent it, so that it could reach as many people as possible, as cheaply as possible.

Asked who owned the patent, Salk replied, 'Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?' He later founded the Salk Institute, a renowned centre for biological research. He is a study in aiming the whole of one's work at relieving human suffering, in choosing impact over fortune, and in the idea that some discoveries belong to everyone.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Aim at the suffering that matters most

Salk targeted the disease terrifying a generation of parents.

Do this: Point your hardest work at the problem whose solution would relieve the most human pain.
Principle 2

Choose reach over rent

He declined a patent so the vaccine could spread fast and cheap.

Do this: Sometimes maximising impact means forgoing the money; decide which you're really optimising for.
Principle 3

Prove it at scale before you claim it

The vaccine was validated in a vast, rigorous trial.

Do this: For anything that touches lives, earn trust through a trial large and clean enough to be undeniable.

Frequently asked questions

What did Jonas Salk do?

He developed the first safe and effective polio vaccine, proven in a massive 1954 field trial and declared successful in 1955, ending the terror of a disease that paralysed and killed thousands of children a year.

Why didn't Salk patent the polio vaccine?

He wanted it to reach as many people as cheaply as possible; asked who owned the patent, he famously replied, 'Well, the people, I would say. Could you patent the sun?'

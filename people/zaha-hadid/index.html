Zaha Hadid

She bent architecture into flowing, impossible-looking forms — and became the first woman to win the Pritzker.

4 min read · 1980s–2016

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Zaha Hadid, born in Baghdad and based in London, revolutionised architecture with sweeping, fluid, seemingly gravity-defying forms that earned her the title 'Queen of the Curve'. For years her radical designs were considered unbuildable and existed only on paper, but advances in computation and engineering let them rise — museums, stadiums, opera houses and transport hubs of extraordinary, flowing geometry. In 2004 she became the first woman to win the Pritzker Prize, architecture's highest honour.

She persisted for decades through rejection as an outsider — a woman, an Arab, an avant-gardist — before the world caught up to her vision. She is a study in holding to a radical aesthetic until technology and taste catch up, in persistence against being doubly an outsider, and in expanding the very vocabulary of a discipline.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Hold your radical vision

Hadid's 'unbuildable' designs eventually rose.

Do this: A vision ahead of what's currently possible isn't wrong; hold to it until technology and taste catch up.
Principle 2

Persist as the outsider

She broke through as a woman and an Arab in the field.

Do this: Being doubly an outsider makes the climb harder and longer; sustained persistence is how the barrier finally gives.
Principle 3

Expand the vocabulary

She added new forms to architecture's language.

Do this: The most lasting creators don't just use the existing vocabulary of their field — they expand what it can express.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Zaha Hadid?

An Iraqi-British architect known as the 'Queen of the Curve' for sweeping, fluid, gravity-defying forms, and the first woman to win the Pritzker Prize (2004).

Why were Hadid's designs long considered unbuildable?

Her radical, flowing geometry existed only on paper for years until advances in computation and engineering made it constructible — vindicating a vision ahead of its technical time.

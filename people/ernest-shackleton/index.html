Ernest Shackleton

The explorer who failed at his goal but achieved the greatest survival-leadership feat in history.

4 min read · 1901–1922

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Ernest Shackleton is celebrated not for achieving his goal — he never reached his objective — but for one of the greatest feats of leadership and survival ever recorded. When his ship the Endurance was crushed by Antarctic ice in 1915, stranding his crew of 27 on the ice with no hope of rescue, Shackleton got every single one of them home alive through two years of almost unimaginable hardship.

Shackleton's leadership under extreme adversity is studied in business schools worldwide. He kept up morale relentlessly, adapted his plans as conditions changed, made bold decisions under pressure (including an 800-mile open-boat voyage for help), and prioritised his people's survival above his ambition and ego. His example shows that in crisis, a leader's first job is to keep the team together, hopeful and alive — and that adaptability and care for people matter more than the original plan.

Shackleton is the definitive study in crisis leadership — that when the plan collapses, the leader's job becomes keeping the team alive, hopeful and united, through relentless morale, bold adaptation, and putting people above ego. His failure at his goal produced a triumph of leadership that outlasts any success.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Put your people first

Shackleton prioritised his crew's survival above his ambition.

Do this: In a crisis, your first duty is the survival and morale of your team.
Principle 2

Adapt when the plan collapses

He abandoned his goal and improvised to save everyone.

Do this: When the situation changes utterly, abandon the plan and adapt to the new reality.
Principle 3

Sustain morale relentlessly

He kept hope and unity alive through two brutal years.

Do this: Actively maintain morale and unity; despair is as deadly as any external threat.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Shackleton a leadership legend if he failed?

Because when his ship was crushed and his crew stranded in Antarctica with no rescue, he brought all 27 men home alive through two years of extreme hardship — a feat of crisis leadership studied worldwide.

What are Shackleton's leadership lessons?

Put your people's survival above your ego and ambition, adapt completely when the plan collapses, and sustain morale and unity relentlessly — the leader's core job in a crisis.

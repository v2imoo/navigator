Alexander Fleming

A messy lab and a sharp eye for an accident gave the world its first antibiotic — and saved untold millions.

4 min read · 1920s–1950s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Alexander Fleming's most famous discovery came from a lucky accident he was prepared to notice. Returning from holiday in 1928, he found that a mould had contaminated one of his bacterial cultures — and that the bacteria around the mould had been killed. Where others might have discarded the spoiled dish, Fleming investigated, identifying the mould's antibacterial substance, which he named penicillin: the world's first true antibiotic.

Fleming himself couldn't mass-produce it; that took the later work of Florey and Chain, but his observation opened the antibiotic age that has since saved untold millions of lives. He is a study in the prepared mind that turns an accident into a discovery, in the value of curiosity over tidiness, and in how a single noticed anomaly can change medicine forever.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Notice the anomaly

Fleming investigated the 'spoiled' dish others would discard.

Do this: Don't automatically clean up or ignore the unexpected result; the anomaly you're tempted to discard may be the discovery.
Principle 2

Prepare your mind for luck

His accident became a discovery because he was ready to see it.

Do this: Chance favours the prepared mind; deep expertise is what lets you recognise the meaning of a lucky accident.
Principle 3

Open the door, even if others walk through it

He found penicillin; others made it usable.

Do this: Making the key observation is a complete contribution even if others complete the application; start the chain.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

How did Fleming discover penicillin?

In 1928 he noticed that a mould contaminating a bacterial culture had killed the surrounding bacteria; rather than discard the dish, he investigated and identified its antibacterial substance — penicillin, the first true antibiotic.

Why did it take time for penicillin to be used?

Fleming couldn't mass-produce it; the later work of Howard Florey and Ernst Chain turned it into a usable drug, opening the antibiotic age that has saved untold millions.

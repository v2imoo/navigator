Tim Berners-Lee

The scientist who invented the web — and gave it away for free, so it could belong to everyone.

4 min read · 1989–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Tim Berners-Lee, a British scientist working at CERN, invented the World Wide Web in 1989 — writing the first web browser, server, and the core standards (HTML, URLs, HTTP) that let documents anywhere be linked and accessed through the internet. It became one of the most transformative inventions in human history, reshaping communication, commerce, knowledge, and society itself.

His most consequential decision was to give it away. Berners-Lee and CERN made the web's standards open and royalty-free, available to anyone, refusing to patent or commercialise them — a choice that allowed the web to be adopted universally and grow without friction or gatekeepers. He has since campaigned for an open, free web that serves humanity rather than narrow interests. He is a study in building open standards that others can freely build on, and in the extraordinary, counter-intuitive power of giving an invention away to maximise its impact.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Give it away to maximise impact

Berners-Lee made the web free and open, so it could be adopted universally.

Do this: Sometimes giving an invention away, not owning it, is what maximises its impact.
Principle 2

Build open standards

Open, royalty-free standards let anyone build on the web without friction.

Do this: Open standards others can freely build on can grow far beyond anything you'd control alone.
Principle 3

Remove the gatekeepers

Refusing to patent the web kept it free of toll-takers.

Do this: Design so there are no gatekeepers; frictionless adoption is its own kind of power.

Why did he give the web away?

Berners-Lee and CERN made the web's standards open and royalty-free rather than patenting them — a choice that let the web be adopted universally and grow without gatekeepers, maximising its impact on humanity.

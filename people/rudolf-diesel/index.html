Rudolf Diesel

He invented a far more efficient engine that still powers the world's ships, trucks and trains — then vanished mysteriously.

4 min read · 1890s–1913

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Rudolf Diesel set out to build a more thermodynamically efficient engine than the steam engines and early petrol engines of his day, and in the 1890s succeeded — the compression-ignition engine that bears his name, which ignites fuel through heat of compression rather than a spark. Far more efficient and able to run on heavier, cheaper fuels, the diesel engine came to power the world's ships, trucks, trains, generators and heavy machinery.

Diesel envisioned his engine empowering small businesses and even running on vegetable oils. In 1913 he vanished overboard from a ship crossing the English Channel, a death never fully explained. He is a study in pursuing efficiency from thermodynamic first principles, in an invention whose impact vastly outran its inventor's life, and in vision for how technology might empower the many.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Design from thermodynamic first principles

Diesel targeted efficiency at the physics level.

Do this: Attack a problem at the level of its fundamental laws; a first-principles redesign can beat incremental tweaks decisively.
Principle 2

Optimise for efficiency

His engine won by wasting far less energy.

Do this: A large, durable efficiency advantage is a moat; where you can convert more input into useful output, you win long-term.
Principle 3

Design for flexibility

His engine could run on many fuels.

Do this: Build in flexibility — his engine's ability to use cheaper, varied fuels widened its adoption enormously.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Rudolf Diesel?

The engineer who invented the compression-ignition (diesel) engine in the 1890s — far more efficient than the engines of his day — which came to power the world's ships, trucks, trains and heavy machinery.

How did Rudolf Diesel die?

He vanished overboard from a ship crossing the English Channel in 1913, a death that was never fully explained and remains a mystery.

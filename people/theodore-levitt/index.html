Theodore Levitt

He asked what business you're really in — and gave marketing its most quoted line about drills and holes.

4 min read · 1960s–2000s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Theodore Levitt's 1960 Harvard Business Review article 'Marketing Myopia' is one of the most influential pieces ever published in business. Its argument: companies fail when they define themselves by their product rather than the customer need they serve. The railroads declined, he argued, because they thought they were in the railroad business rather than the transportation business. The insight is captured in the line most often attributed to him: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole.

Levitt pushed companies to ask what underlying need they truly satisfy — an idea that directly anticipates 'jobs to be done'. As a Harvard professor and HBR editor he shaped marketing thought for decades. He is a study in defining your business by the customer's goal rather than your current product, in guarding against the myopia of loving what you sell, and in the staying power of a single sharp reframing.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Define yourself by the need, not the product

Levitt said railroads failed by thinking in rails, not transport.

Do this: Ask what customer need you truly serve; a product-bound definition blinds you to change and competition.
Principle 2

Sell the hole, not the drill

His famous line reframes every product as a means to an end.

Do this: Focus on the outcome the customer actually wants; your product is just one way to deliver it.
Principle 3

Guard against loving your product

Myopia comes from attachment to what you currently make.

Do this: Stay loyal to the customer's goal, not to your current solution, so you can evolve when the world does.

In their words

People don't want a quarter-inch drill. They want a quarter-inch hole.
The purpose of a business is to create and keep a customer.
Creativity is thinking up new things. Innovation is doing new things.

What is the 'quarter-inch hole' idea?

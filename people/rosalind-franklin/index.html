Rosalind Franklin

Her photograph exposed the shape of the double helix — a contribution long overshadowed, now set right.

4 min read · 1950s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Rosalind Franklin was an expert in X-ray crystallography whose meticulous images of DNA — especially the famous 'Photo 51' — provided the critical evidence for its double-helix structure. Watson and Crick's 1953 model drew directly on her data, which was shown to them without her knowledge. She died of cancer at 37, four years before the Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins; the prize is not awarded posthumously, and her role was long underplayed.

Franklin was a rigorous, exacting experimentalist who insisted on getting the data right before drawing conclusions, and who went on to do pioneering work on the structure of viruses. Her story has become central to conversations about credit, gender and science. She is a study in experimental rigour, in the quiet precision that produces decisive evidence, and in the long-overdue correction of who gets remembered for what.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Get the data right first

Franklin refused to leap to a model before the evidence was solid.

Do this: Resist the pull to conclude early; the rigorous dataset is what makes any model defensible.
Principle 2

Master the decisive technique

Her crystallography skill produced the single most important image.

Do this: Deep mastery of one hard method can make you the person whose work everyone else depends on.
Principle 3

Document your contribution

Much of her credit was lost because others controlled the narrative.

Do this: Record and claim your work clearly; evidence of what you did protects it when memory is contested.

Frequently asked questions

What did Rosalind Franklin contribute to DNA?

Her X-ray crystallography, especially 'Photo 51', provided the key evidence for DNA's double-helix structure — data that Watson and Crick used in their 1953 model, shown to them without her knowledge.

Why didn't Franklin win the Nobel Prize?

She died of cancer in 1958 at 37, four years before the 1962 prize went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins; the Nobel is not awarded posthumously, and her role was long underappreciated.

Mark Cuban

The hustler who sold at the top of the dot-com boom — and turned relentless preparation into a fortune.

4 min read · 1983–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Mark Cuban built and sold Broadcast.com, an early internet audio and video streaming company, to Yahoo for around $5.7 billion in 1999 — near the absolute peak of the dot-com bubble, in a deal whose timing proved extraordinarily shrewd. A self-made entrepreneur who had hustled since childhood selling garbage bags and stamps, he turned that windfall into a diverse fortune, buying the Dallas Mavericks and becoming a prominent investor and television personality on Shark Tank.

Cuban's edge is preparation and effort. He preaches that you don't have to be the smartest, but you can out-work and out-prepare everyone — that knowledge and relentless hustle are the great equalisers available to anyone. He is also candid about the role of timing and luck, having sold at the perfect moment. He is a study in relentless preparation as a competitive advantage, in the value of selling into euphoria, and in self-made hustle from nothing.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Out-prepare everyone

Cuban preaches that relentless effort and knowledge beat raw talent.

Do this: You can't always be smartest, but you can out-work and out-prepare anyone.
Principle 2

Sell into euphoria

He sold Broadcast.com near the dot-com peak — superb timing.

Do this: Be willing to sell when others are euphoric and prices are irrational.
Principle 3

Treat knowledge as the equaliser

He believes anyone can win by learning more than the competition.

Do this: Knowledge is the one advantage available to anyone willing to do the work.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

What made Mark Cuban rich?

Building and selling Broadcast.com to Yahoo for about $5.7 billion in 1999 — with extraordinary timing, near the absolute peak of the dot-com bubble — then diversifying into sports, investing and media.

What's Cuban's core philosophy?

That relentless preparation and effort are the great equalisers — you don't have to be the smartest, but you can out-work and out-prepare everyone, and knowledge is an advantage available to anyone.

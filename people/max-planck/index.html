Max Planck

A reluctant revolutionary — to solve one stubborn problem he proposed that energy comes in packets, and broke classical physics open.

4 min read · 1900s–1940s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Max Planck launched the quantum revolution almost against his own instincts. Trying to solve the 'black-body radiation' problem that classical physics could not explain, in 1900 he proposed that energy is not continuous but comes in discrete packets, or 'quanta'. He regarded it at first as a mathematical trick, but it turned out to describe reality, overturning classical physics and opening the quantum age that Einstein, Bohr and others would build.

A deeply conservative man, Planck also observed that new scientific truths triumph not by persuading opponents but because the opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up with the idea. He is a study in following a reluctant but necessary break with orthodoxy, in solving the one anomaly that cracks open a whole field, and in the hard social reality of how paradigms actually change.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Follow the anomaly

Planck focused on the one problem classical physics couldn't explain.

Do this: The anomaly that your current framework can't handle is exactly where the next breakthrough hides; chase it.
Principle 2

Accept the uncomfortable answer

He embraced quanta even against his instincts.

Do this: When the math forces a conclusion you dislike, follow it; reality doesn't owe you comfort.
Principle 3

Know how paradigms really shift

He saw that science advances generation by generation.

Do this: New ideas win less by converting sceptics than by the next generation adopting them; be patient with resistance.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

What did Max Planck discover?

That energy is not continuous but comes in discrete packets called 'quanta' — proposed in 1900 to solve the black-body radiation problem, launching quantum theory and overturning classical physics.

What is 'Planck's principle' about how science changes?

His observation that new scientific truths triumph not by converting their opponents but because those opponents eventually die and a new generation grows up accepting the idea.

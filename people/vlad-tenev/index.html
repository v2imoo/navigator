Vlad Tenev

He made stock trading free and app-native — democratising investing, and igniting fierce debate about its risks.

4 min read · 2010s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Vlad Tenev, a Bulgarian-born immigrant, co-founded Robinhood in 2013 with the mission to 'democratise finance for all' by making stock trading commission-free and radically simple on a mobile app. It pressured the entire industry to drop commissions and brought a new generation of young, first-time investors into the markets — reaching a peak of cultural attention during the 2021 'meme stock' frenzy.

Robinhood also drew serious criticism and scrutiny — over how it made money (payment for order flow), its game-like design, and a controversial trading halt during the 2021 surge — raising real questions about whether frictionless trading serves or harms retail investors. He is a study in democratising access to a gated system, in business models whose incentives deserve scrutiny, and in the double edge of removing friction.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Democratise the gated system

Robinhood opened investing to first-timers.

Do this: Removing cost and complexity barriers to a gated system can unlock a vast new user base — and reshape the industry.
Principle 2

Examine your incentives honestly

Its revenue model drew scrutiny.

Do this: Understand how your business actually makes money and whether those incentives align with your users' interests.
Principle 3

Weigh the cost of removing friction

Frictionless trading can harm as well as help.

Do this: Removing friction isn't always pro-user; consider whether some friction protects the very people you're serving.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Vlad Tenev?

The Bulgarian-born co-founder and CEO of Robinhood, the app that pioneered commission-free, mobile-first stock trading and brought a new generation of first-time investors into the markets.

Why is Robinhood controversial?

Over how it makes money (payment for order flow), its game-like design that may encourage risky trading, and a disputed trading halt during the 2021 meme-stock surge — raising questions about whether frictionless trading truly serves retail investors.

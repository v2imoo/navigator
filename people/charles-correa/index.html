Charles Correa

He designed for India's climate and crowds — open-to-sky spaces, low-cost housing, and a vision for its cities.

4 min read · 1950s–2015

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Charles Correa was independent India's most influential architect and a pioneering thinker on its cities. He developed principles of climate-responsive design suited to the tropics — open-to-sky spaces, courtyards, natural ventilation and shade — and applied them from cultural institutions to low-cost urban housing. He was a passionate advocate for the urban poor and for thoughtful planning of India's fast-growing cities, helping shape New Bombay (Navi Mumbai).

Correa insisted architecture must grow from local climate, culture and resources, not imported glass boxes. He is a study in designing with the climate rather than against it, in architecture as a tool for equitable cities, and in rooting modern design in local conditions.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Design with the climate

Correa worked with the tropics, not against them.

Do this: Let local climate shape your design — ventilation, shade, open space; working with conditions beats fighting them with technology.
Principle 2

Build for equity

He focused on housing and the urban poor.

Do this: Direct your craft toward those most in need; design can be a tool for more equitable cities, not just monuments.
Principle 3

Root the modern in the local

He rejected imported glass boxes for Indian reality.

Do this: Modern design should grow from local climate, culture and resources, not be copied from elsewhere.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Charles Correa?

Independent India's most influential architect and urban thinker, who pioneered climate-responsive design for the tropics, championed low-cost housing and the urban poor, and helped plan Navi Mumbai.

What were Correa's design principles?

Climate-responsive design suited to India — open-to-sky spaces, courtyards, natural ventilation and shade — rooted in local climate, culture and resources rather than imported glass-box modernism.

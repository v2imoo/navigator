Andy Grove

The refugee-turned-CEO who made Intel the microprocessor king and taught that 'only the paranoid survive'.

4 min read · 1968–1998

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Andy Grove, Intel's third employee and defining CEO, turned the company into the dominant maker of the microprocessors at the heart of the personal-computer revolution — and became one of the most respected management thinkers of his age. A Hungarian refugee who fled the Nazis and then the Soviets, he brought a hard-edged discipline and intensity to everything he did.

His most famous decision was betting Intel's future on microprocessors by exiting the memory-chip business it was built on, when Japanese competition made it unwinnable — a wrenching pivot he framed with the idea of the 'strategic inflection point'. He preached constant vigilance: only the paranoid survive.

Grove's book High Output Management remains a classic, and his management system of objectives and key results (OKRs) spread across the tech industry. He's a case study in decisive strategic pivots and in disciplined, paranoid leadership.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Recognise strategic inflection points

Grove spotted when the ground shifted and bet the company on the new game.

Do this: Watch for the moment your industry fundamentally changes, and pivot hard.
Principle 2

Kill your old business if you must

He exited memory — Intel's origin — to win in microprocessors.

Do this: Abandon a legacy business when a better future demands it.
Principle 3

Stay paranoid

Grove believed constant vigilance was the price of survival.

Do this: Assume complacency is fatal; keep scanning for the threat that ends you.

In their words

Only the paranoid survive.
Success breeds complacency. Complacency breeds failure.
Bad companies are destroyed by crises; good companies survive them; great companies are improved by them.

Frequently asked questions

What is a 'strategic inflection point'?

Grove's term for a moment when the fundamentals of a business change so much that it must transform or decline — as when Intel abandoned memory chips for microprocessors.

What management ideas did Andy Grove leave?

His book High Output Management and the OKR system (objectives and key results), which spread widely across the technology industry.

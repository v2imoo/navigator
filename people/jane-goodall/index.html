Jane Goodall

With patience and no formal training, she revealed that chimps make tools — and that the line between us and them is thin.

4 min read · 1960s–2025

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Jane Goodall arrived in Tanzania's Gombe in 1960 with no university degree, just patience and a notebook, and over years of quiet observation transformed science's understanding of chimpanzees. She discovered that they make and use tools — a finding so startling it forced humanity to redefine the line between people and animals — and that they have distinct personalities, emotions and social bonds, which she insisted on describing by giving the chimps names rather than numbers, against scientific convention.

She later became one of the world's foremost conservationists and advocates for animals and the environment, tirelessly until her death in 2025. She is a study in patient, immersive observation over credentials, in the courage to challenge scientific orthodoxy, and in turning discovery into a lifelong mission of advocacy.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Observe patiently and immersively

Goodall learned by living among the chimps for years.

Do this: Deep, patient, firsthand observation can reveal what detached study misses; immerse yourself in what you want to understand.
Principle 2

Challenge the orthodoxy

She described chimps with names and emotions against convention.

Do this: When convention blinds the field to what you actually observe, trust your evidence over the accepted method.
Principle 3

Turn discovery into mission

She became a lifelong advocate for conservation.

Do this: Let what you learn obligate you; the deepest work often becomes a lifelong cause, not just a finding.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Jane Goodall important?

Arriving in Gombe in 1960 with no degree, she transformed science's understanding of chimpanzees — discovering they make tools and have personalities and emotions — and became one of the world's foremost conservationists until her death in 2025.

What was revolutionary about Goodall's findings?

Her discovery that chimpanzees make and use tools forced humanity to redefine the line between people and animals, and her insistence on their individual personalities and emotions challenged scientific convention.

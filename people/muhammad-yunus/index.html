Muhammad Yunus

He lent $27 to 42 villagers and started a movement that put banking — and dignity — in the hands of the poorest.

4 min read · 1970s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

As an economics professor in famine-era Bangladesh, Muhammad Yunus lent $27 of his own money to a group of 42 poor villagers trapped by predatory lenders — and watched them repay reliably and lift themselves. From that experiment grew the Grameen Bank and the model of microcredit: tiny collateral-free loans to the very poor, especially women, to start micro-enterprises. The model spread worldwide and earned Yunus and Grameen the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.

Yunus went on to champion 'social business' — companies built to solve social problems rather than maximise profit. In 2024 he was appointed Chief Adviser of Bangladesh's interim government. He is a study in challenging the assumption that the poor are uncreditworthy, in designing systems around trust and the dignity of the borrower, and in treating business as a tool for social ends.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Challenge the disqualifying assumption

Yunus rejected the idea the poor were uncreditworthy.

Do this: Question the assumption that excludes your intended beneficiaries; it's often wrong and the opportunity hides behind it.
Principle 2

Design around trust

Grameen's group model replaced collateral with social trust.

Do this: When you can't use the usual guarantees, engineer a system where trust and community do the work instead.
Principle 3

Treat business as a tool for a social end

His 'social business' optimises for impact.

Do this: A company can be built to solve a problem rather than just to profit; decide what you're really optimising for.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Muhammad Yunus?

A Bangladeshi economist who pioneered microcredit and founded the Grameen Bank, giving tiny collateral-free loans to the very poor; he won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize and in 2024 became Chief Adviser of Bangladesh's interim government.

What is microcredit?

Yunus's model of lending very small, collateral-free sums to poor borrowers — especially women — to start micro-enterprises, using group trust rather than collateral, which spread worldwide as a tool against poverty.

Tu Youyou

She found a cure for malaria in a 1,600-year-old Chinese text — and tested it first on herself.

4 min read · 1970s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

During the Vietnam War, malaria was killing soldiers faster than combat, and the parasite had grown resistant to existing drugs. Tu Youyou led a secret Chinese research programme that screened thousands of traditional remedies; the breakthrough came when she read a 4th-century text describing sweet wormwood steeped in cold water. Realising heat was destroying the active compound, she changed the extraction method and isolated artemisinin — now the core of the world's most effective malaria treatments, credited with saving millions of lives.

To prove its safety, Tu and her colleagues tested the compound on themselves before human trials. She worked for decades in obscurity, without a doctorate or foreign training, and was awarded the Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2015 — the first for a scientist working in mainland China. She is a study in mining old knowledge with new rigour, in solving the right problem with patient persistence, and in staking yourself on your own work.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Mine old knowledge with new rigour

Tu found the clue in an ancient text, then applied modern chemistry.

Do this: Don't dismiss traditional or historical knowledge; test it with current methods — the answer may be waiting there.
Principle 2

Question the method, not just the material

Changing from hot to cold extraction unlocked the compound.

Do this: When a promising lead fails, suspect your process before abandoning the idea.
Principle 3

Stake yourself on the result

She tested the compound on herself to establish safety.

Do this: Conviction sometimes means bearing the first risk yourself — though verify, never endanger others.

Frequently asked questions

What did Tu Youyou discover?

Artemisinin — a highly effective anti-malarial compound she isolated from sweet wormwood after finding a clue in a 4th-century Chinese text and correcting the extraction method — now central to malaria treatments that have saved millions.

Why is Tu Youyou historically significant?

In 2015 she became the first scientist working in mainland China to win a Nobel Prize in a science, achieved without a doctorate or overseas training, after decades of largely unrecognised work.

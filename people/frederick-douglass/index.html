Frederick Douglass

The man who taught himself to read in secret — and used the written and spoken word to help free a people.

4 min read · 1841–1895

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Frederick Douglass escaped slavery and became the most influential African American of the nineteenth century — a towering orator, writer and statesman whose words helped drive the abolition of slavery. Denied education by law, he taught himself to read and write in secret as a child, understanding even then that literacy was the pathway from bondage to freedom.

Douglass wielded the power of communication like few in history. His autobiographies and speeches, delivered with extraordinary force and eloquence, made the reality of slavery undeniable to audiences who had never faced it, and he advised presidents and shaped national debate. His life is a testament to self-education, to the transformative power of the word, and to relentless moral persistence against overwhelming odds.

Douglass is a model of self-education, persuasive power and moral courage — proof that the disciplined mastery of the word, and unrelenting persistence in a just cause, can move a nation, whatever one's starting circumstances.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Educate yourself relentlessly

Douglass taught himself to read against every obstacle.

Do this: Take charge of your own education; no one can give it or fully deny it.
Principle 2

Master communication

His oratory and writing made an unseen reality undeniable.

Do this: Develop the power to communicate; it can move minds and change history.
Principle 3

Persist in a just cause

He pressed on against overwhelming odds for decades.

Do this: Sustain relentless persistence in the cause you believe is right.

In their words

Once you learn to read, you will be forever free.
If there is no struggle, there is no progress.
It is easier to build strong children than to repair broken men.

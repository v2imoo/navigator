Part I — The story

The story

Adam Smith, a Scottish moral philosopher, effectively founded economics as a discipline with his 1776 masterwork The Wealth of Nations. His central insight was that in a free market, individuals pursuing their own self-interest are led, as if by an 'invisible hand', to produce outcomes that benefit society as a whole — and that the division of labour, specialisation and exchange are the engines of a nation's wealth. It reframed how the world understood commerce and prosperity.

Smith is often caricatured as a cheerleader for pure greed, but his earlier Theory of Moral Sentiments grounded his economics in sympathy and ethics; he saw markets as embedded in a moral order. He is a study in finding the hidden order beneath apparent chaos, in the power of specialisation and exchange, and in the balance between self-interest and the moral fabric that contains it.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Look for the hidden order

Smith saw coordination emerging from uncoordinated self-interest.

Do this: Before imposing control, ask whether an order is already emerging from the incentives of the parts.
Principle 2

Harness specialisation

He showed the division of labour multiplies output.

Do this: Let people and teams specialise and exchange; focused expertise plus trade beats everyone doing everything.
Principle 3

Ground self-interest in ethics

His economics sat inside a moral philosophy.

Do this: Self-interest builds prosperity only within a framework of trust and norms; don't mistake the engine for the whole machine.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

What is 'The Wealth of Nations'?

Adam Smith's 1776 founding work of modern economics, arguing that free markets, the division of labour, and self-interest guided by an 'invisible hand' create a nation's prosperity.

What is the 'invisible hand'?

Smith's metaphor for how individuals pursuing their own self-interest in a free market are led to produce outcomes that benefit society as a whole, without anyone intending it.

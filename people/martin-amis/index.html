Martin Amis

The sharpest prose stylist of his generation — a satirist who skewered modern excess and defended wit against cant.

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Martin Amis was one of the most distinctive and influential British novelists of his generation — a dazzling prose stylist whose satirical novels, including Money and London Fields, skewered the greed, vanity and excess of late-20th-century life with ferocious wit and linguistic energy. The son of novelist Kingsley Amis, he made his own name and became a defining literary voice, as celebrated for his essays and criticism as for his fiction.

Amis was a fierce defender of style, irony and free expression, and a sharp critic of cant and cliché — what he called 'the war against cliché'. He is a study in treating prose as a craft to be perfected sentence by sentence, in satire as a lens on a culture's excesses, and in defending wit and candour against the pressure to be inoffensive.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Perfect prose sentence by sentence

Amis treated style as a craft to be honed relentlessly.

Do this: Treat your sentences as the unit of work; relentless attention to style is what separates good writing from great.
Principle 2

Wage war against cliché

He hunted and killed the lazy phrase.

Do this: Cliché is dead thinking; chase it out of your work and replace it with something precisely, freshly your own.
Principle 3

Defend candour against cant

He prized wit and honesty over being inoffensive.

Do this: Don't sand your voice down to avoid all offence; honest, witty expression is worth defending against the pressure to blandness.

In their words

Being inoffensive, and being offended, are now the twin addictions of the culture.
Style is not neutral; it gives moral directions.
We are all going to die, and that makes us comedians.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Martin Amis?

One of the most distinctive British novelists of his generation, a dazzling prose stylist whose satirical novels like Money and London Fields skewered late-20th-century excess, as celebrated for his essays as his fiction.

What did Martin Amis stand for as a writer?

The craft of style sentence by sentence, a 'war against cliché', and the defence of wit, irony and candour — famously observing that 'being inoffensive, and being offended, are now the twin addictions of the culture'.

Amos Tversky

Kahneman's brilliant partner, whose work on judgement would have shared the Nobel had he lived.

4 min read · 1969–1996

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Amos Tversky was the brilliant cognitive psychologist whose partnership with Daniel Kahneman produced one of the most consequential collaborations in the history of social science. Together they discovered and documented the systematic errors in human judgement — the heuristics and biases that shape how people assess probability, risk, and value — work that founded behavioural economics and transformed psychology, economics, medicine, law, and beyond.

Tversky was renowned for the sharpness and clarity of his mind — colleagues joked about an intelligence test based on how quickly you realised he was smarter than you. Their collaboration was so fused that they flipped a coin to decide author order on their first papers. Tversky died of cancer in 1996; the Nobel Prize, which cannot be awarded posthumously, went to Kahneman alone in 2002, who said the honour was shared. He is a study in the power of deep intellectual partnership, and in the rigorous discovery of how the mind really works.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Find a great thinking partner

Tversky and Kahneman's fused partnership produced more than either could alone.

Do this: Seek a true intellectual partner; the right collaboration multiplies both of you.
Principle 2

Prize clarity and rigour

Tversky was famed for the sharpness and precision of his thinking.

Do this: Pursue clarity and rigour relentlessly; sloppy thinking hides real errors.
Principle 3

Study how the mind actually works

He documented the real, systematic flaws in human judgement.

Do this: Base decisions on how minds actually work — flaws and all — not how they should.

In their words

The secret to doing good research is always to be a little underemployed. You waste years by not being able to waste hours.
Whenever there is a simple error that most laymen fall for, there is always a slightly more sophisticated version of the same problem that experts fall for.
It's frightening to think that you might not know something, but more frightening to think that the world is run by people who believe they know exactly what's going on.

David Ogilvy

The 'Father of Advertising' who insisted the consumer is not a moron, and that research beats bluster.

4 min read · 1948–1999

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

David Ogilvy shaped modern advertising by treating it as a discipline grounded in research and respect for the customer rather than clever slogans and guesswork. He founded his agency in New York in 1948 and built it into a global powerhouse on the belief that the purpose of advertising is to sell, and that knowing what actually works beats creative ego.

Before advertising he had been a chef, a door-to-door salesman, and a pollster — experiences that taught him how ordinary people really think and buy. His campaigns married strong, research-backed ideas with long, informative copy, and his book Ogilvy on Advertising became a bible for the industry.

Ogilvy's enduring lessons — respect the consumer's intelligence, sell rather than entertain for its own sake, and base decisions on evidence — remain foundations of effective marketing decades later.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Respect the customer

'The consumer isn't a moron' — Ogilvy insisted on treating audiences as intelligent.

Do this: Assume your audience is smart; inform and persuade rather than talk down.
Principle 2

Base creativity on research

He grounded campaigns in what evidence showed actually sold.

Do this: Let evidence, not ego, decide what wins — test and learn.
Principle 3

Remember the goal is to sell

Ogilvy judged advertising by results, not applause.

Do this: Measure creative work by whether it achieves the business goal, not admiration.

In their words

The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife.
If it doesn't sell, it isn't creative.
Never write an advertisement you wouldn't want your family to read.

Frequently asked questions

Why is David Ogilvy called the Father of Advertising?

For professionalising the field — grounding it in research and respect for the consumer — and for campaigns and writing that defined modern advertising practice.

What was his core belief?

That advertising exists to sell, should respect the customer's intelligence, and must be based on evidence of what actually works.

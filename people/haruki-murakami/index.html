Haruki Murakami

A jazz-bar owner who decided at 29 to write a novel — and became one of the world's most-read living authors.

4 min read · 1980s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Haruki Murakami ran a Tokyo jazz bar until, at 29, watching a baseball game, he was struck by the sudden conviction that he could write a novel — and did. He went on to become one of the most widely read and translated living authors, with a distinctive style blending the mundane and the surreal: ordinary people slipping into dreamlike, parallel realities, soundtracked by jazz and Western pop, in works like Norwegian Wood, Kafka on the Shore and The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle.

Murakami is also famous for his disciplined routine — rising before dawn to write, then running long distances — which he describes as essential to the stamina creative work requires. He is a study in beginning a vocation late on a sudden conviction, in forging a singular style, and in the daily discipline and physical stamina that sustain long creative work.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Begin on conviction, even late

Murakami started writing at 29 on a sudden certainty.

Do this: A vocation can begin at any point; when conviction strikes, act on it rather than deferring to your past path.
Principle 2

Forge a singular style

His blend of mundane and surreal is unmistakably his.

Do this: Develop a voice that is recognisably yours; a distinctive style is itself a lasting advantage.
Principle 3

Build the stamina for the work

He runs to sustain the endurance writing demands.

Do this: Creative work is physical and long; a disciplined routine and real stamina are what let you finish big things.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Haruki Murakami?

One of the most widely read living authors, a Japanese writer known for a surreal, globally beloved style blending the mundane and the dreamlike in works like Norwegian Wood and Kafka on the Shore.

What is Murakami's approach to writing?

Famous discipline — rising before dawn to write, then running long distances — which he describes as building the physical and mental stamina that sustained, long-form creative work requires.

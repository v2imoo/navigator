Vint Cerf

He co-wrote the rules that let any network talk to any other — the quiet grammar of the internet.

4 min read · 1970s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

In the 1970s Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed TCP/IP, the set of protocols that lets independent computer networks interconnect and exchange data reliably — the technical foundation of the internet. The genius was in the design philosophy: a simple, open, universal standard that any network could adopt, with no central owner, letting the system grow without permission. That openness is why the internet could scale from a handful of research machines to billions of devices.

Cerf has spent the decades since as a tireless advocate for the open internet, and worked on interplanetary networking for communication across the solar system. Widely called a 'father of the internet', he shared the Turing Award in 2004. He is a study in the power of open, decentralised standards, in designing for growth you can't yet imagine, and in building something whose success means no one owns it.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Design open standards

TCP/IP spread because anyone could adopt it freely.

Do this: An open, permissionless standard can out-grow any proprietary one; openness is a growth strategy.
Principle 2

Build for a scale you can't yet see

The protocols were designed to expand far beyond their origins.

Do this: Architect for orders of magnitude more than you need today; the best infrastructure outruns its creators' imagination.
Principle 3

Decentralise ownership

No single entity controls the internet's core, by design.

Do this: Sometimes the way to make something thrive is to ensure no one — including you — controls it.

Frequently asked questions

What did Vint Cerf invent?

With Bob Kahn, the TCP/IP protocols that let independent computer networks interconnect and reliably exchange data — the open, universal foundation on which the internet is built.

Why is TCP/IP's openness important?

Because it's an open standard with no central owner, any network could adopt it and the system could grow without permission — the key reason the internet scaled to billions of devices.

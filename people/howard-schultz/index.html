Howard Schultz

The leader who sold not coffee but a 'third place' — turning a commodity into an experience people pay a premium for.

4 min read · 1987–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Howard Schultz did not found Starbucks, but he transformed it from a handful of Seattle coffee-bean shops into one of the world's most recognisable brands by selling something far bigger than coffee. Inspired by the espresso bars of Italy, he grasped that people wanted a 'third place' — somewhere between home and work — and built Starbucks around the experience, atmosphere, and ritual rather than the drink itself. That reframing turned a cheap commodity into a premium product people line up for daily.

Raised in public housing in Brooklyn, Schultz built a culture that invested unusually in employees — offering health benefits and stock to part-time 'partners' — believing that how you treat staff determines the customer experience. He returned as CEO twice to steer the company through crises, and remains chairman emeritus. His story is a masterclass in branding, in selling an experience over a product, and in the conviction that employee experience and customer experience are inseparable.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Sell the experience, not the product

Schultz built Starbucks around atmosphere and ritual — the 'third place' — not the coffee itself.

Do this: Reframe a commodity as an experience people will pay a premium to have.
Principle 2

Invest in your people

He gave part-time staff benefits and equity, believing they create the customer experience.

Do this: Treat employee experience as the foundation of customer experience.
Principle 3

Build a brand that travels

A consistent experience let Starbucks scale the same feeling worldwide.

Do this: Make the experience consistent enough to scale the brand anywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Did Howard Schultz found Starbucks?

No — he joined the existing bean-shop company and transformed it, reframing it around the 'third place' experience and scaling it into a global brand.

What is the 'third place'?

Schultz's concept that people want a comfortable space between home and work — Starbucks sold that experience and atmosphere, not just coffee, which justified premium prices.

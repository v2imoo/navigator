Kiichiro Toyoda

He turned a loom company into a carmaker — and planted the 'just-in-time' idea that reinvented manufacturing.

4 min read · 1930s–1950s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Kiichiro Toyoda took his family's automatic-loom business and, against considerable risk, steered it into automobile manufacturing in the 1930s, founding Toyota Motor Corporation. More importantly, he introduced the philosophy of 'just-in-time' production — making only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed — to eliminate waste and inventory. That idea, later developed by Taiichi Ohno into the Toyota Production System, became the foundation of lean manufacturing worldwide.

Toyoda built a culture of continuous improvement and problem-solving at the source — principles that would make Toyota a global benchmark for quality and efficiency. He is a study in the courage to pivot an established business into an entirely new industry, in designing systems to relentlessly eliminate waste, and in planting a philosophy that outlasts and outgrows its founder.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Pivot into the bigger opportunity

Toyoda moved from looms to cars when he saw the future.

Do this: Be willing to steer an established, comfortable business into a larger emerging industry.
Principle 2

Eliminate waste systematically

Just-in-time was built to remove every unneeded inventory and step.

Do this: Design your operation to produce only what's needed, when needed; waste is the enemy of quality and cost.
Principle 3

Solve problems at the source

His culture fixed root causes on the factory floor.

Do this: Push problem-solving down to where the work happens; fix causes, not symptoms.

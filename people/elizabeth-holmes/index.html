Elizabeth Holmes

She raised billions on a blood-testing machine that never worked — and was convicted of fraud. A study in how hype outruns truth.

4 min read · 2000s–2020s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford to found Theranos in 2003, promising a revolutionary device that could run hundreds of medical tests from a single drop of blood. She raised hundreds of millions of dollars, assembled a star-studded board, graced magazine covers as a visionary, and reached a valuation around $9 billion — all while the core technology never actually worked. The company used standard commercial machines for most tests and produced unreliable results that put real patients at risk.

Investigative reporting exposed the fraud; Theranos collapsed, and in 2022 Holmes was convicted of defrauding investors and sentenced to prison. Her story is included here as a cautionary tale. She is a study in how 'fake it till you make it' becomes criminal when the product touches people's health, in the warning signs of hype outrunning substance, and in the real human cost when truth is sacrificed to the narrative.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Never fake what touches people's safety

Theranos's lies endangered patients.

Do this: 'Fake it till you make it' is catastrophic — and criminal — when the product affects health or safety; truth is non-negotiable there.
Principle 2

Heed the warning signs

Secrecy and unverifiable claims masked the fraud.

Do this: Extreme secrecy, refusal to allow independent validation, and hype far ahead of evidence are red flags; demand proof.
Principle 3

Separate narrative from substance

Her story outran her technology for years.

Do this: A compelling founder narrative is not evidence the thing works; always test the substance beneath the story.

In their words

(Included as a cautionary tale — her claims proved false.)

John A. Roebling

He pioneered the wire-cable suspension bridge and designed the Brooklyn Bridge — a project that cost him his life.

4 min read · 1840s–1869

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

John A. Roebling, a German immigrant to America, pioneered the modern wire-cable suspension bridge, inventing techniques for spinning strong steel cables and building a series of ever-longer spans. His masterwork was the Brooklyn Bridge, designed to cross the East River with a span far beyond anything attempted — but during the survey work he suffered an accident and died of tetanus before construction. His son Washington took over, was himself disabled by caisson disease, and the bridge was completed under the direction of Washington's wife Emily Roebling.

The bridge stands as a monument to a multi-generational feat of engineering and perseverance. He is a study in pioneering a new structural form, in vision that outlasts its originator, and in a family's collective determination to finish what was begun.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Pioneer the new form

Roebling invented wire-cable suspension techniques.

Do this: Developing a genuinely new structural method opens possibilities that incremental improvement never could.
Principle 2

Build what outlasts you

He designed a bridge he'd never see completed.

Do this: Great works can require more than one lifetime; design so that others can carry your vision to completion.
Principle 3

Prove it on ever-larger spans

He worked up to the Brooklyn Bridge gradually.

Do this: Establish a new capability on progressively bigger challenges, building the evidence and skill for the ultimate span.

In their words

Frequently asked questions

Who was John A. Roebling?

A German-American engineer who pioneered the modern wire-cable suspension bridge and designed the Brooklyn Bridge, dying before construction began; it was completed by his son Washington and daughter-in-law Emily.

Why is the Brooklyn Bridge a story of perseverance?

Its designer John Roebling died before building started, his son Washington was disabled by caisson disease, and it was ultimately completed under the direction of Washington's wife Emily — a multi-generational feat.

Louis Pasteur

He proved disease is caused by microbes you can't see — and built the science that has saved more lives than almost any other.

4 min read · 1850s–1890s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Louis Pasteur overturned the belief that disease arose spontaneously from bad air, proving instead that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases — the germ theory that underpins all of modern medicine. He showed that heating liquids killed the microbes that spoiled them (pasteurisation), developed the first laboratory-made vaccines for anthrax and rabies, and saved the French wine, beer and silk industries along the way.

Pasteur combined rigorous experiment with relentless practical application; he was as comfortable solving a brewer's spoilage problem as unravelling the cause of an epidemic. His rabies vaccine, first used on a boy bitten by a rabid dog in 1885, made him a national hero. He is a study in letting careful observation overturn received wisdom, in the marriage of pure science and practical problem-solving, and in the idea that 'chance favours the prepared mind'.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Let experiment overturn dogma

Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation with a decisive experiment.

Do this: When the received view can be tested, design the experiment that settles it — don't argue, demonstrate.
Principle 2

Prepare your mind for chance

His breakthroughs came because he was ready to see what accidents revealed.

Do this: Do the groundwork so that when luck appears, you're equipped to recognise and seize it.
Principle 3

Marry pure science to real problems

He solved industrial spoilage and epidemics with the same tools.

Do this: Aim your deepest research at a concrete problem; the application sharpens the science.

Frequently asked questions

What is germ theory?

Pasteur's proof that specific microorganisms cause specific diseases — overturning the idea that illness arose spontaneously, and becoming the foundation of modern medicine, hygiene and vaccination.

What is pasteurisation?

Pasteur's process of heating liquids such as milk and wine just enough to kill the microbes that spoil them or spread disease, without ruining the product — still used worldwide today.

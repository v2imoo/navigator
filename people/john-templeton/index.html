John Templeton

He bought at the point of maximum pessimism — including 100 shares of every cheap stock on the eve of World War II.

Part I — The story

The story

John Templeton was one of the first great global contrarian investors. In 1939, as war broke out and markets panicked, he borrowed money and bought 100 shares of every stock trading below one dollar on the New York exchange — 104 companies, many in bankruptcy. Most recovered spectacularly. He went on to build the Templeton Growth Fund, hunting for bargains all over the world decades before global investing was common, and compounded wealth for investors across half a century.

His guiding principle was to buy at 'the point of maximum pessimism', when fear had driven prices far below value, and to sell when optimism peaked. Later in life he turned to large-scale philanthropy and the study of science and spirituality. He is a study in contrarianism grounded in valuation, in looking where others won't, and in the discipline of acting against the crowd's emotion.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Buy at maximum pessimism

Templeton bought when fear had crushed prices below value.

Do this: The best bargains appear when others are most afraid; act when pessimism is at its peak, not its onset.
Principle 2

Look where others won't

He invested globally and in distressed names decades early.

Do this: Hunt in the markets and situations the crowd avoids; that's where mispricing lives.
Principle 3

Ground contrarianism in value

His courage rested on buying real assets cheaply, not mere defiance.

Do this: Being contrarian only works when you're also right on value; pair the nerve with the numbers.

In their words

The time of maximum pessimism is the best time to buy.
Bull markets are born on pessimism, grow on scepticism, mature on optimism and die on euphoria.
The four most dangerous words in investing are: 'this time it's different.'

Frequently asked questions

What is John Templeton famous for?

Pioneering global contrarian value investing — famously buying 104 cheap stocks on the eve of World War II — and the principle of buying at 'the point of maximum pessimism'.

What did Templeton do later in life?

He turned to large-scale philanthropy, founding the Templeton Prize and funding research at the intersection of science and spirituality.

B. V. Doshi

He fused modernism with India's climate, culture and life — and built homes and campuses rooted in place.

4 min read · 1950s–2023

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Balkrishna Doshi worked early with Le Corbusier and Louis Kahn, then spent his long career adapting modern architecture to India — its climate, culture, rituals and ways of living. His buildings, from university campuses to the low-cost housing of Aranya (which gave dignified, adaptable homes to tens of thousands), were humane, climate-responsive and rooted in place rather than imposed from the West. In 2018 he became the first Indian to win the Pritzker Prize.

Doshi saw architecture as the creation of environments for life, not just buildings. He is a study in adapting a global idea to local reality, in designing for how people actually live, and in architecture as a humane, social act rather than a monument.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Adapt the global to the local

Doshi reshaped modernism for Indian life and climate.

Do this: Don't import a design wholesale; adapt it to the climate, culture and real lives of the place it serves.
Principle 2

Design for how people actually live

His housing responded to real daily life.

Do this: Study how people truly live and build for that, not for an abstract ideal; architecture should serve its inhabitants.
Principle 3

Treat building as a social act

He saw architecture as creating environments for life.

Do this: Your work shapes how people live together; design with that social responsibility in mind, not just aesthetics.

In their words

Design converts shelter into a home, housing into a community.
When life and architecture meet, that is real architecture.
I owe this honour to my guru, Le Corbusier.

Who was B. V. Doshi?

An Indian architect who worked with Le Corbusier and Louis Kahn, then adapted modernism to India's climate and culture — designing humane campuses and low-cost housing — becoming the first Indian to win the Pritzker Prize in 2018.

What was distinctive about Doshi's architecture?

It was humane, climate-responsive and rooted in how Indians actually live — as with his Aranya housing that gave dignified, adaptable homes to tens of thousands — rather than modernism imposed from the West.

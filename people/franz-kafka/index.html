Franz Kafka

He wrote nightmares of powerlessness so precise that his name became an adjective for the modern condition.

4 min read · 1910s–1924

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Franz Kafka, an insurance clerk in Prague who wrote at night, produced a small body of fiction that captured the anxiety, alienation and absurdity of modern life so precisely that 'Kafkaesque' entered the language. In The Metamorphosis a man wakes as a giant insect; in The Trial a man is arrested and prosecuted by an inscrutable authority for a crime never named. His work renders the nightmare of the individual crushed by faceless systems and incomprehensible power.

Kafka published little in his lifetime and, dying of tuberculosis at 40, asked his friend Max Brod to burn his manuscripts — Brod refused, and so the work survived. He is a study in naming a feeling no one had named, in writing with unsettling precision about powerlessness, and in how deeply personal, private work can capture a universal condition.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Name the unnamed feeling

Kafka captured a modern dread no one had articulated.

Do this: If you can give precise form to a feeling people sense but can't name, your work will lodge permanently in the culture.
Principle 2

Write the private truth

His intensely personal fears became universal.

Do this: The most private, specific anxieties, rendered honestly, often turn out to be the most widely shared.
Principle 3

Pursue the work for its own sake

He wrote at night, publishing almost nothing.

Do this: Do the work because it must be done, not for recognition; some of the most important art is made with no audience in view.

Frequently asked questions

What does 'Kafkaesque' mean?

Named after Franz Kafka, it describes the nightmarish experience of an individual trapped by faceless, incomprehensible bureaucracy and power — the condition his fiction captured with unsettling precision.

Why did Kafka's work almost not survive?

Dying at 40, he asked his friend Max Brod to burn his manuscripts; Brod refused and published them instead, which is why works like The Trial and The Castle exist at all.

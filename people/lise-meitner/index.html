Lise Meitner

She explained the physics of splitting the atom — and was denied the Nobel that went to her collaborator.

4 min read · 1900s–1960s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Lise Meitner was a pioneering physicist who, with chemist Otto Hahn, spent decades studying radioactivity. When Hahn's experiments produced puzzling results, it was Meitner — by then a refugee from Nazi Germany because of her Jewish heritage — who, with her nephew Otto Frisch, provided the theoretical explanation: the uranium nucleus had split in two, releasing enormous energy. She named the process 'nuclear fission' and calculated its energy using Einstein's E=mc².

Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery, one of the most criticised omissions in Nobel history; Meitner, who refused to work on the atomic bomb, was later honoured with an element named after her. She is a study in the theorist who explains what the experiment reveals, in doing profound work amid persecution and exile, and in the injustices of scientific credit.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Supply the explanation, not just the data

Meitner interpreted what Hahn's experiments showed.

Do this: The theorist who explains why a result occurs can matter as much as the one who produced it; understanding is the discovery.
Principle 2

Persevere through persecution

She did landmark work as a refugee in exile.

Do this: Even under persecution and displacement, focused work can continue and reach the heights; adversity need not end it.
Principle 3

Hold your moral lines

She refused to work on the atomic bomb.

Do this: Scientific capability comes with moral choices; decide in advance what you will not lend your talents to.

Frequently asked questions

What did Lise Meitner discover?

With her nephew Otto Frisch, she provided the theoretical explanation for nuclear fission — the splitting of the uranium nucleus and release of energy — naming the process and calculating its energy with Einstein's E=mc².

Why is Meitner's Nobel omission famous?

The 1944 Nobel Prize for the discovery of fission went solely to her collaborator Otto Hahn, overlooking her crucial theoretical role — widely regarded as one of the greatest injustices in Nobel history.

David Packard

The co-founder who built Silicon Valley's original great company — and its management culture.

4 min read · 1939–1993

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

David Packard, with Bill Hewlett, founded Hewlett-Packard in a Palo Alto garage in 1939 — the origin story that gave Silicon Valley its founding myth — and built it into a model technology company admired as much for how it was run as for what it made. HP began with electronic test instruments and grew into computing, defining the Valley's engineering culture along the way.

Packard's lasting contribution was the 'HP Way': a management philosophy built on trusting and respecting employees, decentralised decision-making, management by objectives, and a belief that profit was a result of doing good work, not the sole goal. It was a humane, high-trust culture that influenced generations of technology companies.

Packard, who also served as US Deputy Secretary of Defense, wrote The HP Way to capture his approach. He is a foundational figure in both Silicon Valley and modern management — proof that culture and trust can be a company's most durable asset.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Trust and respect people

The HP Way assumed employees were capable and motivated, and treated them so.

Do this: Build a high-trust culture that assumes the best of capable people.
Principle 2

Decentralise decisions

Packard pushed authority down to those closest to the work.

Do this: Give decision-making to the people nearest the problem.
Principle 3

Make profit a result, not the goal

He believed good work produced profit, not the reverse.

Do this: Focus on doing excellent work; treat profit as the outcome, not the mission.

In their words

Profit is not the proper end of management — it makes the other ends possible.
More companies die of indigestion than starvation.
You should never take on a job for money alone.

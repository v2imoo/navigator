Srinivasa Ramanujan

With almost no formal training he produced thousands of theorems from an unearthly intuition — some still being understood today.

4 min read · 1910s–1920

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Srinivasa Ramanujan, born into poverty in South India with almost no formal mathematical training, produced thousands of original results of breathtaking depth, seemingly conjured from pure intuition — he said a goddess revealed them to him. Working in isolation, he filled notebooks with theorems on infinite series, number theory and continued fractions. In 1913 he wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy, who recognised his genius and brought him to England, beginning one of the most famous collaborations in mathematics.

Ramanujan died at just 32, but his notebooks have kept mathematicians busy for a century, with results still being proved and applied in areas like string theory. He is a study in raw, untutored genius, in intuition that leaps ahead of proof, and in the vast potential unlocked when a hidden talent finds the right champion.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Honour raw intuition

Ramanujan leapt to results ahead of any proof.

Do this: Deep intuition is real and valuable; trust it as a source of discovery, even before you can fully justify it.
Principle 2

Find your champion

Hardy recognised and unlocked his genius.

Do this: A hidden talent often needs one person who sees it and opens the door; seek — and be — that champion.
Principle 3

Leave a deep well of work

His notebooks still yield new mathematics.

Do this: Work of real depth keeps giving long after you; aim to leave problems and results others can mine for generations.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Srinivasa Ramanujan?

A largely self-taught Indian mathematical genius who produced thousands of profound, original results from seemingly pure intuition before dying at 32; his notebooks still yield new mathematics a century later.

What was Ramanujan's collaboration with Hardy?

After Ramanujan wrote to Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy in 1913, Hardy recognised his genius and brought him to England, beginning one of the most famous partnerships in the history of mathematics.

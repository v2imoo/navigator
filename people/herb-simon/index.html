Herbert Simon

The polymath who won a Nobel for showing we don't optimise — we settle for 'good enough'.

4 min read · 1947–2001

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Herbert Simon was one of the great polymaths of the twentieth century — making foundational contributions to economics, psychology, computer science, and management, and winning the Nobel Prize in economics along with the Turing Award, computing's highest honour. His central idea, 'bounded rationality', challenged the economic fiction of the perfectly rational actor: real humans have limited information, time, and mental capacity, so they don't optimise — they 'satisfice', choosing the first option that is good enough.

This insight reshaped how we understand decision-making in organisations and markets, and Simon was also a founding father of artificial intelligence, building some of the first programs that could reason and solve problems. He studied how experts develop intuition through recognising patterns from vast experience. He is a study in bounded rationality and satisficing as a realistic model of decisions, in deep cross-disciplinary thinking, and in understanding the real limits of the human mind.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Accept bounded rationality

Simon showed we decide with limited information and capacity, not perfectly.

Do this: Design decisions around real human limits — limited information, time, and attention.
Principle 2

Satisfice when optimising is impossible

He showed we rationally choose 'good enough' rather than optimal.

Do this: When full optimisation is impossible, deliberately choose the first good-enough option.
Principle 3

Build expertise through pattern recognition

He studied how experts develop intuition from vast experience.

Do this: Develop expertise by accumulating experience until good patterns become intuitive.

In their words

In an information-rich world, the scarce resource is attention.

Frequently asked questions

What is 'bounded rationality'?

Herbert Simon's idea that real humans, having limited information, time and mental capacity, don't optimise their decisions — they 'satisfice', choosing the first option that is good enough, which reshaped how we understand decision-making.

Why is Herbert Simon considered a polymath?

He made foundational contributions across economics, psychology, computer science and management — winning both the Nobel Prize in economics and computing's Turing Award, and helping found artificial intelligence.

Muhammad Ali

'The Greatest' — who backed his boast with genius in the ring and courage outside it, at enormous personal cost.

4 min read · 1960–1981

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Muhammad Ali was one of the greatest and most charismatic athletes in history — a three-time heavyweight boxing champion whose speed, skill, and showmanship redefined his sport, and whose boast that he was 'The Greatest' he spent a career proving true. He pioneered a kind of psychological warfare and self-promotion, predicting rounds and taunting opponents, that made him a global icon and changed how athletes marketed themselves.

But Ali's enduring greatness lies as much outside the ring. At the peak of his career he refused, on grounds of conscience and faith, to be drafted into the Vietnam War — a stand that cost him his title, his boxing licence, and nearly four years of his prime, and brought the threat of prison. He accepted the enormous cost rather than betray his principles, and was ultimately vindicated. He is a study in backing supreme self-belief with the work to prove it, and in the courage to sacrifice everything for conviction.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Back self-belief with the work

Ali boasted he was the greatest — then did the work to prove it true.

Do this: Bold self-belief is only powerful when you do the work to back it up.
Principle 2

Pay the price for conviction

He sacrificed his title and prime years rather than betray his principles.

Do this: Real conviction means being willing to pay a genuine cost for it.
Principle 3

Master the psychological game

His taunts and predictions were strategy, not just showmanship.

Do this: The mental and psychological game can be as decisive as the physical one.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Muhammad Ali considered 'The Greatest'?

For redefining boxing with his speed, skill and showmanship as a three-time heavyweight champion — and for backing his famous boast with the work and results to prove it true.

What did Ali sacrifice for his principles?

At his peak he refused to be drafted into the Vietnam War on grounds of conscience and faith — costing him his title, boxing licence, and nearly four years of his prime, with the threat of prison — a stand he was ultimately vindicated for.

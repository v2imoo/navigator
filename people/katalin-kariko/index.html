Katalin Karikó

Demoted, dismissed and denied grants for decades, she kept working on the idea that would later vaccinate the world.

4 min read · 1980s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Katalin Karikó spent decades convinced that messenger RNA could be used to instruct the body to make its own medicines — an idea most of her field dismissed. She faced repeated grant rejections, was demoted at the University of Pennsylvania, and struggled for funding for years. With her collaborator Drew Weissman she finally solved the key problem: modifying mRNA so the body wouldn't reject it as a threat. That breakthrough became the platform for the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna COVID-19 vaccines, delivered to billions within a year of the pandemic.

Karikó and Weissman shared the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine. Her story is one of the great examples of scientific perseverance — conviction maintained through decades of institutional discouragement. She is a study in holding to an idea the field has written off, in the long lag between unglamorous research and world-changing payoff, and in the quiet stubbornness that outlasts rejection.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Hold the unpopular conviction

Karikó stayed on mRNA for decades while the field dismissed it.

Do this: If your evidence and reasoning hold, persist through rejection; consensus is often late, not right.
Principle 2

Solve the one blocking problem

Modifying mRNA to evade immune rejection unlocked everything.

Do this: Identify the single obstacle standing between your idea and viability, and pour everything into it.
Principle 3

Separate worth from recognition

Her value didn't change when institutions overlooked her.

Do this: Don't let titles, grants or applause define whether your work matters; the work's merit is independent of its reception.

Frequently asked questions

What did Katalin Karikó discover?

With Drew Weissman, how to modify messenger RNA so the body wouldn't reject it — the breakthrough that made mRNA vaccines possible, including the COVID-19 vaccines delivered to billions.

Why is Karikó's story notable?

She pursued mRNA for roughly four decades through grant rejections and a demotion, before her once-dismissed idea became world-changing — earning her the 2023 Nobel Prize in Medicine.

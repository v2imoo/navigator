Sally Ride

She broke NASA's gender barrier, then spent her life getting girls into science.

4 min read · 1980s–2012

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Sally Ride, a physicist, became the first American woman in space in 1983 aboard the Space Shuttle Challenger, enduring a barrage of sexist press questions to do so. She flew twice and later served on the investigation boards for both shuttle disasters, Challenger and Columbia, known for her rigour and willingness to speak hard truths. After NASA she devoted herself to science education, founding a company to inspire young people — especially girls — to pursue careers in science and engineering.

Ride used her barrier-breaking status to open doors for others. She is a study in being the first through a barrier under intense scrutiny, in rigour and honesty in high-stakes investigation, and in turning a pioneering role into a mission to widen the path behind you.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Go first under scrutiny

Ride broke the barrier despite sexist pressure.

Do this: Being first invites intense, unfair scrutiny; enduring it with composure opens the door for everyone who follows.
Principle 2

Speak hard truths in high stakes

She was candid on the disaster investigations.

Do this: When lives and integrity are at stake, rigour and willingness to say the uncomfortable truth matter most.
Principle 3

Widen the path behind you

She devoted her later life to girls in science.

Do this: Use the access your pioneering earned to bring others through; a first is most valuable when it's not the last.

In their words

What did Sally Ride do after NASA?

Steve Jobs

The founder who proved that taste, not just technology, is a durable competitive advantage.

4 min read · 1976–2011

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Steve Jobs did not invent the personal computer, the digital music player, the smartphone or the tablet. In every case, working versions already existed. What he did — repeatedly, across four decades and three companies — was take a category that engineers had made possible and consumers found baffling, and turn it into something an ordinary person wanted to own. His real product was not hardware. It was the removal of friction, and the insistence that a machine could be beautiful.

Adopted at birth and raised in what became Silicon Valley, Jobs was shaped by two opposing currents: the hobbyist electronics culture of the Homebrew Computer Club, and a counter-cultural search for meaning that took him to a Reed College calligraphy class and, briefly, to India. The calligraphy is not a footnote. When the Macintosh shipped in 1984 with beautiful proportionally-spaced fonts, it was because Jobs had sat in a class about serifs and letter-spacing that had, at the time, no conceivable practical use. He later called it the first time he trusted that the dots would connect looking backward, even when they could not be connected looking forward.

Apple, exile, and the wilderness

With Steve Wozniak he co-founded Apple in 1976; the Apple II made the company one of the fastest-growing in American history and Jobs a millionaire before thirty. But the same intensity that drove the products made him difficult to manage, and in 1985, after the Macintosh underperformed and he lost a boardroom fight with the CEO he had personally recruited, Jobs was stripped of operational power and left the company he had founded.

The wilderness years are the ones most people skip, and they are the ones that made him. At NeXT he built an over-engineered, commercially disappointing computer whose software would later become the foundation of every modern Apple product. And in 1986 he bought a struggling computer-graphics division from Lucasfilm, renamed it Pixar, and spent a decade and much of his fortune keeping it alive until Toy Story in 1995 made it the most important animation studio in the world. The lesson he took from Pixar — that a company of artists managed for the long term could produce hit after hit — came back to Cupertino with him.

The return and the second act

By 1997 Apple was ninety days from insolvency. It bought NeXT to get a modern operating system, and got Jobs back in the bargain. What followed is the most valuable corporate turnaround in history. He cut the product line from dozens of confusing models to four. He struck a truce with Microsoft for a cash lifeline. Then came the disciplined cadence that defined the second act: the iMac (1998), the iPod and iTunes (2001–03), the iPhone (2007), and the iPad (2010). Each was late to its market and each redefined it.

Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works.Steve Jobs

Jobs was diagnosed with a rare pancreatic cancer in 2003 and, characteristically, first tried to treat it on his own terms before surgery. He ran Apple through years of illness, staged the iPhone and iPad launches as cultural events, and resigned as CEO in August 2011, handing the company to Tim Cook. He died six weeks later. The company he rescued would become the first to be worth a trillion dollars, then three.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Say no to a thousand things

Focus was Jobs' central discipline. On returning to Apple he killed most of the product line, and he described focus not as choosing what to do but as choosing what not to do. A strategy is defined by its exclusions.

Do this: Before adding, ask what you will remove. If everything is a priority, nothing is.
Principle 2

Own the whole stack

Rivals licensed and unbundled; Jobs integrated hardware, software and services so tightly that the experience could not be copied piecemeal. Controlling every layer let him control the feel of the product.

Do this: Integrate the parts that determine the experience; outsource only what doesn't.
Principle 3

Start from the experience, work back to the technology

He was famous for insisting you begin with the customer experience and work backward to the engineering, not the reverse — the opposite of how most technical companies operate.

Do this: Define how it should feel first. Let that dictate the tech, not the other way around.
Principle 4

Treat taste as a moat

Anyone could buy the same components. What competitors could not buy was Jobs' refusal to ship anything that offended his sense of craft — the fonts, the curves, the boxes. Taste, consistently applied, became a durable advantage.

Do this: Make a standard of quality non-negotiable; over years it compounds into a brand rivals can't buy.
Principle 5

Stage the reveal

Jobs turned product launches into theatre — the 'one more thing', the single hero image, the story arc. The presentation was part of the product, shaping how the world received it.

Do this: Control the moment your work meets the world; the framing is part of the work.

Frequently asked questions

Did Steve Jobs invent the iPhone?

No individual invented it, and Jobs was not an engineer. He set the vision, imposed the standards, and made the integration decisions — touchscreen over stylus, one button, a full browser — that turned existing technologies into a product people wanted.

Why was Jobs fired from Apple?

After the Macintosh sold below expectations, Jobs lost a power struggle in 1985 with CEO John Sculley and the board, and was stripped of operational control. He resigned and founded NeXT.

What did Jobs learn at Pixar?

That a long-term, craft-driven company of talented people could produce hits repeatedly — a model of patient, quality-obsessed management he brought back to Apple.

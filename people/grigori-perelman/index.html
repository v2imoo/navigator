Grigori Perelman

He cracked one of maths' hardest problems, then turned down the Fields Medal and a million dollars, and walked away.

4 min read · 2000s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Grigori Perelman, a reclusive Russian mathematician, solved the Poincaré conjecture — one of the most important and stubborn open problems in mathematics, a century old and one of the seven million-dollar 'Millennium Prize' problems. He posted his proof online in 2002–03 without fanfare or formal publication, let others verify it, and then did something almost unheard of: he declined the Fields Medal (mathematics' highest honour) in 2006 and the one-million-dollar Millennium Prize in 2010, and withdrew from mathematics and public life altogether.

He objected to the ethics and politics of the mathematical community and felt the recognition was unjust and beside the point. He is a study in pure intrinsic motivation, in indifference to prestige and money, and in a radical integrity that refuses the rewards most people chase.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Work for the problem, not the prize

Perelman solved it, then refused the rewards.

Do this: The deepest work is done for its own sake; intrinsic motivation outlasts and outperforms the pursuit of recognition.
Principle 2

Let the work speak, skip the fanfare

He posted the proof online without publishing formally.

Do this: You don't need the usual machinery of credit; sometimes putting the work out and letting others verify it is enough.
Principle 3

Hold radical integrity

He walked away over the field's ethics.

Do this: Being willing to refuse what everyone else chases — on principle — is a rare and clarifying form of integrity.

Frequently asked questions

What did Grigori Perelman achieve?

He solved the Poincaré conjecture, a century-old problem and one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, posting his proof online in 2002–03 — then declined both the Fields Medal and the million-dollar prize.

Why did Perelman refuse the prizes?

He was indifferent to money and fame, objected to the ethics and politics of the mathematical community, and felt the recognition was unjust and beside the point — withdrawing from mathematics and public life entirely.

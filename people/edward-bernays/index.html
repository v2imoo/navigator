Edward Bernays

The 'father of public relations' who applied psychology to shaping public opinion — for better and worse.

4 min read · 1919–1990

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Edward Bernays essentially invented modern public relations, applying his uncle Sigmund Freud's insights about the unconscious to the systematic shaping of public opinion. He argued that people could be influenced not by appealing to reason but to deeper desires and emotions, and he pioneered techniques — the staged event, the third-party endorsement, the manufactured trend — that still define PR today.

His campaigns were famously effective and often ethically fraught: he helped make it socially acceptable for women to smoke by staging a suggestive publicity stunt, and reshaped everything from bacon-and-eggs breakfasts to political messaging. His book Propaganda laid out a philosophy of using communication to guide the public that remains both influential and deeply controversial.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Appeal to desire, not just reason

Bernays influenced people through emotion and the unconscious.

Do this: Understand that people are moved by desire and identity, not logic alone.
Principle 2

Engineer third-party credibility

He used endorsements and staged events to shape opinion indirectly.

Do this: Influence through credible third parties rather than direct self-promotion.
Principle 3

Mind the ethics

His manipulative campaigns are a warning as much as a model.

Do this: Persuasion is powerful; use it responsibly, aware of where influence becomes manipulation.

In their words

The conscious and intelligent manipulation of opinion is an important element in society.
If you can influence the leaders, you automatically influence the group.
Ideas can be as contagious as any virus.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Edward Bernays called the father of public relations?

Because he essentially invented the field — applying Freudian psychology to systematically shape public opinion, pioneering techniques like staged events and third-party endorsements still central to PR.

Why is Bernays a controversial figure?

His techniques were effective but often manipulative — shaping opinion by appealing to unconscious desires — raising enduring ethical questions about persuasion and propaganda.

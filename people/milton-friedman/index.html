Milton Friedman

He led the counter-revolution against big government — arguing that free markets and free people rise together.

Part I — The story

The story

Milton Friedman was the twentieth century's most influential advocate for free markets and limited government, the intellectual counterweight to Keynesian orthodoxy. He argued that inflation is 'always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon' (monetarism), that free markets are the best engine of both prosperity and individual freedom, and that many well-intentioned government interventions do more harm than good. He won the Nobel Prize in 1976 and reached the public through his book and series Free to Choose.

Friedman's ideas shaped the economic policies of the 1980s and remain central to debates about the role of the state; critics argue his faith in markets underweighted inequality and market failure. In the spirit of fair presentation: supporters credit him with reviving economic freedom, while opponents hold his doctrines responsible for rising inequality. He is a study in arguing a case with rigour and clarity, in the link he drew between economic and political freedom, and in the power of communicating ideas to the public.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Argue with rigour and clarity

Friedman made complex economics vivid and accessible.

Do this: Win arguments by making your reasoning clear enough for anyone to follow, not by complexity.
Principle 2

Trace the second-order effects

He highlighted the unintended consequences of interventions.

Do this: Before backing any policy or action, ask what it incentivises and what it will cause downstream.
Principle 3

Communicate ideas to the public

Free to Choose took economics to millions.

Do this: Ideas gain power when you can explain them to a general audience; popularisation is leverage, not dilution.

In their words

There's no such thing as a free lunch.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Milton Friedman?

The twentieth century's leading advocate of free markets and limited government, a Nobel laureate (1976) known for monetarism — the view that inflation is a monetary phenomenon — and for popularising economics through Free to Choose.

What are the main criticisms of Friedman?

Critics argue his strong faith in free markets underweighted inequality, market failures and the social costs of deregulation; supporters credit him with reviving economic freedom and taming inflation.

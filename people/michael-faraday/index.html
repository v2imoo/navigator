Michael Faraday

A bookbinder's apprentice with no formal maths who discovered how to turn magnetism into electricity — and lit the modern world.

4 min read · 1820s–1860s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Michael Faraday left school at thirteen and taught himself science while apprenticed to a bookbinder, reading the volumes he was binding. He rose to become one of history's greatest experimentalists, discovering electromagnetic induction — the principle that a changing magnetic field generates an electric current — which is the basis of every electric generator and transformer in the world. He also built the first electric motor and laid the groundwork for field theory.

Faraday had almost no mathematical training; he thought in physical pictures and lines of force, and left it to James Clerk Maxwell to render his intuitions into equations. He turned down a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society, preferring the bench. He is a study in how curiosity and relentless experiment can outrun formal credentials, in thinking in concrete mechanisms rather than abstractions, and in the humility of a man who wanted only to understand.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Teach yourself relentlessly

Faraday built a world-class education from books he was binding.

Do this: Formal credentials are not the gate; sustained self-directed study can take you to the frontier.
Principle 2

Think in physical pictures

He pictured lines of force where others saw only equations.

Do this: Build a concrete mental model of how something works before reaching for the formalism.
Principle 3

Experiment, don't just theorise

His discoveries came from the bench, not the blackboard.

Do this: When you can test an idea with your hands, do it — the apparatus teaches what thought alone cannot.

Frequently asked questions

What did Michael Faraday discover?

Electromagnetic induction — that a changing magnetic field produces an electric current — the principle behind every generator and transformer, plus the first electric motor and foundational ideas of field theory.

Was Faraday a trained mathematician?

No — he left school at thirteen and was largely self-taught, thinking in physical pictures rather than equations; James Clerk Maxwell later gave his ideas their mathematical form.

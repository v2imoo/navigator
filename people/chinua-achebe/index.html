Chinua Achebe

He told the African story from the inside — and gave a continent its own voice in world literature.

4 min read · 1950s–2013

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Chinua Achebe's 1958 novel Things Fall Apart — the story of Okonkwo and the arrival of colonialism in an Igbo community — is the most widely read work of African literature, translated into dozens of languages. Writing in English but infusing it with Igbo proverbs, rhythms and worldview, Achebe told the African story from the inside, countering centuries of narratives that had portrayed Africa only through colonial eyes.

He insisted that Africans tell their own stories, famously critiquing how Western literature had depicted the continent. He is a study in reclaiming the narrative of your own people, in adapting a borrowed language to carry your own culture, and in the power of telling a story from the inside that others had only told from without.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Tell your own people's story

Achebe countered outsiders' narratives from the inside.

Do this: Those who live a story should tell it; reclaiming your own narrative is an act of power and truth.
Principle 2

Adapt the borrowed tool to your voice

He bent English to carry Igbo culture.

Do this: You can take a dominant medium or language and infuse it with your own culture rather than being absorbed by it.
Principle 3

Counter the single story

He challenged one-sided depictions of Africa.

Do this: Where a people has been reduced to a single, external narrative, offer the fuller truth from within.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Chinua Achebe important?

His 1958 novel Things Fall Apart is the most widely read work of African literature; by telling the African story from the inside, in English infused with Igbo worldview, he gave the continent its own voice in world literature.

What was Achebe's central concern?

That Africans should tell their own stories, countering centuries of narratives that depicted the continent only through colonial, external eyes — reclaiming the narrative from within.

Alexander Graham Bell

The teacher of the deaf whose telephone patent became one of the most valuable in history.

4 min read · 1874–1922

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Alexander Graham Bell invented the telephone and secured, in 1876, what became one of the most valuable patents ever granted. A teacher of the deaf whose work on sound and speech led him to transmit the human voice electrically, Bell won a famous race to the patent office and then built the Bell Telephone Company, which grew into the vast AT&T telephone monopoly.

Bell's achievement combined genuine invention with the crucial business step of patenting and commercialising it fast. The telephone network he founded became a textbook network effect — each new telephone made every other one more valuable — and grew into one of the most powerful companies in the world. Bell himself moved on to restless invention across many fields.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Secure ownership fast

Bell won the race to the patent office, and the patent made the fortune.

Do this: Move fast to protect and own a breakthrough; the rights can be worth more than the idea.
Principle 2

Build the network

The telephone's value grew with every new user connected.

Do this: Build products whose value compounds as more people join the network.
Principle 3

Commercialise the invention

Bell founded the company that turned the patent into an empire.

Do this: Pair invention with the company that captures its value at scale.

Frequently asked questions

What made Bell's telephone patent so valuable?

It gave him ownership of a foundational invention, which he commercialised through the Bell Telephone Company — and the telephone network's compounding value (a network effect) grew into the AT&T monopoly.

What was Bell's background?

He was a teacher of the deaf whose work on sound and speech led him to transmit the human voice electrically — inventing the telephone.

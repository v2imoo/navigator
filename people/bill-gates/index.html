Bill Gates

The founder who saw that software, not hardware, would rule computing — and built a platform monopoly on it.

4 min read · 1975–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Bill Gates built Microsoft into the dominant force of the personal-computer era by grasping a then-radical idea: that the money would be in software, not the machines. His masterstroke was licensing MS-DOS to IBM in 1980 without giving up the right to sell it to others — letting Microsoft's operating system spread across every PC clone that followed.

A Harvard dropout obsessed with code, Gates paired programming skill with ferocious commercial instinct. Windows and Office became near-universal, protected by the switching costs of file formats and the network effects of everyone using the same tools. For years Microsoft was the most valuable company in the world.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Own the platform

Gates understood that controlling the operating system — the layer everything runs on — was worth more than any single application.

Do this: Control the platform layer others must build on, not just a product on top.
Principle 2

Keep the rights that matter

The IBM deal's genius was retaining the right to license DOS to everyone else — the economics were in the contract.

Do this: In any deal, guard the rights that let your best asset scale.
Principle 3

Exploit switching costs and standards

File formats and ubiquity locked users in; leaving Office meant leaving everyone you worked with.

Do this: Build standards and formats that make leaving your product costly.

In their words

Your most unhappy customers are your greatest source of learning.
Success is a lousy teacher. It seduces smart people into thinking they can't lose.

Frequently asked questions

How did Microsoft become so dominant?

By owning the operating system — the platform all PC software ran on — spread through a licensing deal with IBM, and protected by switching costs and standards like Office file formats.

What does Bill Gates do now?

He left Microsoft's board in 2020 and focuses on the Gates Foundation, directing large-scale philanthropy toward global health.

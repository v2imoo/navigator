Eleanor Roosevelt

She remade the role of First Lady, then drove the world's first Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

4 min read · 1930s–1962

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Eleanor Roosevelt transformed the role of American First Lady from ceremonial hostess into an active public force, holding her own press conferences, writing a daily newspaper column, travelling the country as her husband Franklin's eyes and ears, and advocating for the poor, workers, women and civil rights. After his death, she served as a delegate to the new United Nations and chaired the commission that drafted the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights — the foundational document of the modern human-rights movement.

Overcoming a painfully shy childhood, she became one of the most admired and influential public figures of her century. She is a study in expanding a constrained role into a platform for change, in moral conviction turned into institutions and documents, and in growth from timidity into fearless public purpose.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Expand a constrained role

Eleanor turned a ceremonial position into a force for change.

Do this: Even a role defined narrowly by others can be expanded through initiative; redefine the job by what you do in it.
Principle 2

Turn conviction into institutions

She helped create a lasting human-rights document.

Do this: Encode your values into durable institutions and documents; that's how conviction outlives the moment.
Principle 3

Grow past your timidity

She overcame deep shyness to lead publicly.

Do this: Fearfulness is not destiny; deliberate practice of courage can turn a shy person into a powerful public voice.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Eleanor Roosevelt significant?

She transformed the role of US First Lady into an active force for the poor, workers and civil rights, and after her husband's death chaired the commission that drafted the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

What is the Universal Declaration of Human Rights?

The foundational 1948 document of the modern human-rights movement, setting out basic rights for all people; Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the UN commission that drafted it.

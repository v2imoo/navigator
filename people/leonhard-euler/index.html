Leonhard Euler

He wrote more mathematics than anyone ever — much of it after going blind, computing it all in his head.

4 min read · 1730s–1783

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Leonhard Euler was the most prolific mathematician in history, producing an enormous body of work that touched nearly every branch of the subject — calculus, number theory, graph theory (which he founded with the Königsberg bridges problem), topology, mechanics and more. Much of our modern mathematical notation, including the symbols for the function f(x), the constant e, and the use of i and pi together, comes from him. His identity e^{iπ}+1=0 is often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics.

Remarkably, Euler lost his sight late in life yet remained astonishingly productive, performing complex calculations entirely in his head and dictating to assistants. He is a study in sheer sustained productivity, in giving a field the language and notation it thinks in, and in the mind's capacity to work past devastating physical loss.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Give the field its notation

Euler's symbols shaped how mathematics is written.

Do this: Creating the notation and language others think in is a quiet but enormous contribution; clear notation unlocks progress.
Principle 2

Sustain prolific output

His volume of quality work is unmatched.

Do this: Consistent, disciplined productivity over a lifetime compounds into a body of work no single flash of genius can rival.
Principle 3

Work past the loss

He did his greatest work blind, in his head.

Do this: Severe limitation need not end the work; the mind can often adapt and continue where the body fails.

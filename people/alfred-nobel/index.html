Alfred Nobel

The inventor of dynamite who, confronting his own legacy, rewrote it — founding the prizes that bear his name.

4 min read · 1863–1896

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Alfred Nobel was a chemist and industrialist who invented dynamite — making the dangerous explosive nitroglycerin safe and practical to handle — and built a vast international industrial empire on explosives and armaments, becoming enormously wealthy. He held hundreds of patents and was a restless inventor, but his products' association with destruction weighed on him.

The pivotal moment came when a French newspaper, mistakenly believing he had died, published a scathing obituary condemning 'the merchant of death' who grew rich by enabling people to kill. Confronted with how he would be remembered, Nobel rewrote his will, leaving the bulk of his fortune to establish the Nobel Prizes — for physics, chemistry, medicine, literature and peace — rewarding those who confer the greatest benefit on humankind. He is a study in confronting your own legacy honestly, and in the deliberate choice to redefine how you will be remembered.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Confront your legacy honestly

A premature obituary forced Nobel to face how he'd be remembered.

Do this: Ask honestly how you'll be remembered — while you still have time to change it.
Principle 2

Choose to redefine yourself

He rewrote his will to leave a legacy of benefit, not destruction.

Do this: You can deliberately redefine the legacy you leave, whatever you built before.
Principle 3

Make an invention safe and usable

Dynamite's genius was taming a dangerous substance for practical use.

Do this: Taming a powerful but dangerous technology into something safe and usable is itself the breakthrough.

Frequently asked questions

What did Alfred Nobel invent?

Dynamite — making the dangerous explosive nitroglycerin safe and practical to handle — on which he built a vast industrial empire, holding 355 patents in total.

Why did Nobel found the Nobel Prizes?

A French newspaper, wrongly believing he had died, published an obituary condemning him as a 'merchant of death'. Confronted with how he'd be remembered, he rewrote his will to leave his fortune for the prizes honouring those who benefit humankind.

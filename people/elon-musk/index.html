Elon Musk

The founder who bet on first principles to make electric cars and reusable rockets inevitable.

4 min read · 1995–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Elon Musk built two companies that reshaped their industries — Tesla in electric cars and SpaceX in rockets — by refusing to accept the received wisdom that either was impossible or uneconomic. His signature method is first-principles reasoning: strip a problem to physics and cost of raw materials, then rebuild the answer from there.

After co-founding and selling PayPal, Musk poured his fortune into SpaceX (2002) and Tesla (joined 2004), nearly going bankrupt in 2008 before both survived. SpaceX went on to make orbital rockets reusable — slashing launch costs and ending a decades-long stagnation — while Tesla forced the entire auto industry toward electric vehicles.

Musk's style — extreme ambition, brutal timelines, and enormous personal risk — produces both breakthroughs and controversy. He acquired Twitter (now X) in 2022 and launched the AI company xAI, extending a pattern of entering industries others considered settled. His fortune, tied largely to Tesla, swings dramatically with its stock.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Reason from first principles

Musk breaks problems down to physical fundamentals and rebuilds, ignoring 'how it's always been done' — which is how rockets got cheap.

Do this: Ask what's physically true and necessary, then rebuild the solution from there.
Principle 2

Attack the real cost driver

He targets the true cost floor — raw materials, reusability — rather than accepting inherited price structures.

Do this: Find the fundamental cost driver and re-engineer it, not the surface price.
Principle 3

Set impossible timelines

Aggressive, often-missed deadlines force radical rethinking and speed, at the cost of predictability.

Do this: Use ambitious targets to force step-changes, accepting they'll sometimes slip.

In their words

When something is important enough, you do it even if the odds are not in your favour.
Failure is an option here. If things are not failing, you are not innovating.

Frequently asked questions

What is first-principles thinking, in Musk's use?

Breaking a problem down to fundamental truths — physics and raw-material costs — and reasoning up from there, rather than copying existing solutions. It's how he reframed rocket and battery costs.

What are Musk's main companies?

Tesla (electric vehicles), SpaceX (rockets), plus Neuralink, xAI, and X (formerly Twitter).

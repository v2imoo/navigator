Coco Chanel

The couturier who built a global luxury house by taking things away — corsets, colour, clutter — rather than adding them.

4 min read · 1910–1971

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Gabrielle 'Coco' Chanel invented modern womenswear largely by subtraction. In an era when fashionable women were laced into corsets, buried in fabric and forbidden the colour black outside mourning, she stripped the wardrobe down to comfort, movement and a severe, expensive simplicity. Almost everything now considered a classic — the jersey dress, the collarless tweed suit, the quilted bag on a chain, the little black dress, costume jewellery worn without apology — traces to decisions she made against the grain of her time.

Her origins were the opposite of luxury. Born into poverty and placed in a convent orphanage after her mother's death, she learned to sew from nuns. The austere black-and-white of that convent, and the plain lines of its architecture, would echo through her entire aesthetic. She sang in cabarets, where the song 'Coco' gave her the name, before a series of wealthy protectors gave her the capital and connections to open first a hat shop, then boutiques in Deauville and Biarritz.

Selling comfort as luxury

Chanel's first radical act was material. She made high fashion out of jersey — a cheap, stretchy knit previously used for men's underwear — because it let women move. During the First World War, as women took on work and needed clothes they could actually function in, her relaxed, sporty silhouettes read as both practical and modern. She was selling freedom, and pricing it as luxury.

No.5 and the business behind the myth

In 1921 she launched Chanel No.5, a perfume built on synthetic aldehydes that made it abstract rather than floral — 'a woman's perfume with a woman's scent', not a bouquet. Crucially, the fragrance and licensing business, not the couture, became the financial engine of the house; a scent can be sold to millions at a margin no hand-made dress can match. The deal she struck with the Wertheimer family to manufacture and distribute No.5 made her wealthy and, later, the subject of decades of renegotiation — a reminder that the terms of a licensing deal can matter more than the product.

In order to be irreplaceable one must always be different.Coco Chanel

Collaboration, exile, and the comeback

Chanel closed her couture house at the outbreak of the Second World War and spent the occupation years in the Ritz in a relationship with a German officer — conduct that saw her leave for Switzerland afterward under a cloud that has never fully lifted, and which any honest account has to name. Then, in 1954, aged seventy and stung by the return of Dior's cinched, corseted 'New Look', she reopened. Paris critics panned the comeback; American women bought it in volume. The relaunched tweed suit made her, in her seventies and eighties, more globally influential than ever. She was still working when she died at the Ritz in 1971.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Compete by removing, not adding

Chanel's signature move was subtraction — fewer layers, less colour, no corset. In a crowded market, the boldest differentiation can be taking things away until only the essential, and the expensive-looking, remains.

Do this: Ask what everyone in your category adds by default — then win by removing it.
Principle 2

Build the annuity behind the art

The couture made the name; the perfume and licences made the money. She paired a scarce, prestige product with a scalable, high-margin one that carried the same brand.

Do this: Pair a prestige product that builds the brand with a scalable one that funds it.
Principle 3

Make yourself the brand

Chanel dressed as her own ideal customer and lived the image — the tan, the jersey, the strings of pearls. The founder as living advertisement made the brand legible and hard to copy.

Do this: Embody the standard you sell; a consistent living example markets better than copy.
Principle 4

Own your terms

Her lifelong wrangling over the No.5 royalties shows the cost of signing away economics early. The value of a hit is captured or lost in the contract, not the creation.

Do this: Negotiate the economics of your best asset as carefully as you make it.
Principle 5

Ignore the critics, watch the customers

Her 1954 comeback was savaged by the French press and embraced by American buyers. She trusted the market's wallet over the reviewers' verdict.

Do this: When critics and customers disagree, follow the ones who pay.

Frequently asked questions

What did Coco Chanel actually invent?

She popularised, more than invented: jersey daywear, the collarless tweed suit, the little black dress, costume jewellery, and the abstract modern perfume. Her contribution was a whole aesthetic of expensive simplicity.

How did Chanel make its money?

Not mainly from couture but from perfume and licensing — above all Chanel No.5 — which could be sold at scale and high margin in a way hand-made clothes never could.

Is the story of Chanel entirely admirable?

No. Her relationship with a German officer during the occupation of France, and wartime conduct, remain a serious and legitimate stain on her legacy that any full account must include.

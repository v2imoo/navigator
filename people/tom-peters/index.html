Tom Peters

His 1982 blockbuster launched the whole genre of management books — and told companies to obsess over action and customers.

Part I — The story

The story

Tom Peters, with co-author Robert Waterman, wrote In Search of Excellence in 1982 — a study of America's best-run companies that became one of the best-selling business books ever and effectively created the modern management-guru genre. It argued that excellent companies shared traits like a bias for action, closeness to the customer, autonomy and entrepreneurship, and 'sticking to the knitting' (focusing on what you know). It arrived when American business felt it was losing to Japan, and its optimistic, people-centred message struck a nerve.

Peters became a wildly energetic speaker and prolific author, championing passion, boldness and treating people as the heart of business. He is a study in the power of a bias for action over endless analysis, in putting customers and people first, and in communicating management ideas with infectious energy.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Keep a bias for action

Peters prized doing over endless analysis.

Do this: Favour action and experiment over analysis paralysis; you learn more from doing than from planning forever.
Principle 2

Stay close to the customer

He found great companies obsess over customers.

Do this: Keep direct, constant contact with your customers; distance from them is where decline begins.
Principle 3

Stick to the knitting

He urged focus on what you truly know.

Do this: Concentrate on your core competence rather than sprawling into things you don't understand.

In their words

Test fast, fail fast, adjust fast.
Excellent firms don't believe in excellence — only in constant improvement and constant change.
If a window of opportunity appears, don't pull down the shade.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Tom Peters?

A management author whose 1982 book In Search of Excellence (with Robert Waterman) became a blockbuster that created the modern management-guru genre, championing a bias for action, customer focus and people-centred management.

What were the traits of excellent companies?

Peters identified eight, including a bias for action, staying close to the customer, autonomy and entrepreneurship, productivity through people, and 'sticking to the knitting' — focusing on what you know.

Archimedes

He grasped the principle of buoyancy in his bath, cried 'Eureka!', and anticipated calculus two thousand years early.

4 min read · 3rd century BC

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Archimedes of Syracuse was the greatest scientist and mathematician of the ancient world. He discovered the principle of buoyancy — leaping from his bath shouting 'Eureka!' when he realised how to measure volume by displacement — established the law of the lever ('give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth'), calculated pi to remarkable accuracy, and developed methods that anticipated integral calculus by nearly two thousand years. He also designed ingenious war machines to defend his city.

He was reportedly killed by a Roman soldier during the siege of Syracuse, absorbed in a geometry problem to the end. He is a study in uniting pure mathematics with practical engineering, in the flash of insight that comes when a problem is deeply held, and in intellectual passion sustained to the last moment.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Hold the problem until insight strikes

Archimedes's 'Eureka' came from deep immersion.

Do this: Immerse yourself fully in a problem; the breakthrough insight often arrives unbidden once your mind is saturated with it.
Principle 2

Unite theory and application

He was both a pure mathematician and an engineer.

Do this: The deepest work often joins abstract theory to practical building; don't wall off the two.
Principle 3

Find the lever

He saw how a small force, well-placed, moves enormous weight.

Do this: Look for the point of leverage where a small, well-placed effort produces an outsized result.

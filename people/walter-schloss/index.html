Walter Schloss

No office, no analysts, no company visits — just cheap stocks and a 45-year record that humbled Wall Street.

4 min read · 1950s–2000s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Walter Schloss learned investing directly from Benjamin Graham and became perhaps the purest practitioner of deep-value investing ever. From a tiny one-room operation with no research staff, no company meetings and barely any contact with management, he simply bought large numbers of statistically cheap stocks — trading below book value, out of favour, ignored — and held them until they recovered. Over roughly 45 years he compounded capital at around 15–20% a year, beating the market handsomely.

Warren Buffett held him up as a model of integrity and discipline, a 'superinvestor' who proved that extraordinary results come from a simple method relentlessly applied, not from inside information or complexity. He is a study in radical simplicity, in diversified deep value, and in the quiet temperament that lets you buy what everyone else is selling for decades on end.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Keep it radically simple

Schloss bought cheap stocks and held — no complexity, no inside access.

Do this: A simple method applied with discipline for decades beats a sophisticated one you can't sustain.
Principle 2

Buy what others are dumping

He bought ignored, out-of-favour, below-book stocks.

Do this: The best statistical bargains come from forced or emotional selling; go where the pessimism is.
Principle 3

Diversify your deep value

He owned many cheap names rather than concentrating.

Do this: When betting on statistical cheapness rather than deep knowledge of each firm, spread across many to let the odds work.

In their words

Buy cheap and don't be in a hurry to sell.
We don't own stocks that we'd be paralysed by if they went down.
I'm not very good at judging people, so I prefer to look at the numbers.

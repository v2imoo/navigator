Ludwig Mies van der Rohe

He reduced architecture to pure structure and space — and taught the world that less is more.

4 min read · 1920s–1960s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Ludwig Mies van der Rohe was a pioneer of modern architecture whose name is synonymous with minimalist elegance — 'less is more' and 'God is in the details'. The last director of the Bauhaus before the Nazis closed it, he emigrated to America and defined the glass-and-steel skyscraper, with buildings like the Seagram Building and Farnsworth House reducing architecture to clean structure, open space and immaculate proportion. His aesthetic shaped corporate and residential building across the world.

Mies pursued a radical clarity, believing that stripping away everything inessential revealed a deeper beauty and truth in structure itself. He is a study in the power of disciplined reduction, in obsessive attention to detail, and in an aesthetic so clear it became a global language.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Reduce to the essential

Mies stripped away everything inessential.

Do this: Radical reduction reveals a deeper beauty and clarity; remove until only what's necessary and true remains.
Principle 2

Obsess over the details

'God is in the details' was his creed.

Do this: In a stripped-down design, every detail carries full weight; perfect the small things when there's nowhere to hide.
Principle 3

Build a clear, teachable aesthetic

His style became a global language.

Do this: An aesthetic clear and disciplined enough becomes a language others can speak; clarity scales.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Mies van der Rohe?

A pioneer of modern architecture, last director of the Bauhaus, who defined the minimalist glass-and-steel skyscraper with buildings like the Seagram Building, under his creed 'less is more'.

What did 'less is more' mean for Mies?

That stripping architecture down to pure structure, open space and immaculate proportion — removing everything inessential — revealed a deeper beauty and truth than ornament ever could.

Dwight D. Eisenhower

He planned the largest invasion in history, then governed by a simple rule: what's important is seldom urgent.

4 min read · 1940s–1961

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Dwight Eisenhower served as Supreme Allied Commander in Europe during World War II, orchestrating the D-Day invasion of Normandy in 1944 — the largest amphibious operation ever attempted, demanding the coordination of millions of troops and fractious allied egos. He then served two terms as US president in the 1950s, presiding over postwar prosperity, launching the interstate highway system, and warning in his farewell address of the 'military-industrial complex'.

Eisenhower was a master of planning, delegation and managing strong personalities, and he gave the world the enduring 'Eisenhower matrix' for distinguishing the important from the merely urgent. He is a study in leadership through coordination rather than heroics, in the discipline of prioritising importance over urgency, and in the planning that makes decisive action possible.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Separate important from urgent

Eisenhower's matrix forces this distinction.

Do this: Sort tasks by importance and urgency; spend your best energy on what's important before it becomes urgent.
Principle 2

Lead by coordination

D-Day succeeded through managing allies, not solo heroics.

Do this: Large undertakings are won by aligning many parties and egos; coordination is the leader's real craft.
Principle 3

Plan hard, then adapt

He prized the planning process even when plans changed.

Do this: Plans may be useless but planning is indispensable — the process prepares you to adapt when reality intervenes.

In their words

What is important is seldom urgent and what is urgent is seldom important.
Plans are worthless, but planning is everything.
Leadership is the art of getting someone else to do something you want done because he wants to do it.

Frequently asked questions

What is Dwight Eisenhower known for?

Commanding the D-Day invasion as Supreme Allied Commander in WWII, then serving two terms as US president — building the interstate highways and warning of the 'military-industrial complex'.

What is the Eisenhower matrix?

A prioritisation tool attributed to him that sorts tasks by importance and urgency, so you focus on what's important rather than being driven by what's merely urgent.

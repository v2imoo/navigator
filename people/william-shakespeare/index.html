William Shakespeare

He mapped the whole range of human nature in verse — and half the phrases you speak were coined by him.

Part I — The story

The story

William Shakespeare is widely regarded as the greatest writer in the English language. A glover's son from a provincial town, with no university education, he became an actor and playwright in London and produced around 37 plays and 154 sonnets that have never left the stage or the page. His tragedies, comedies and histories gave us characters — Hamlet, Macbeth, Lear, Falstaff — of such psychological depth that they still feel startlingly modern four centuries later.

He expanded the English language itself, coining or popularising thousands of words and phrases still in everyday use. His genius was a boundless empathy — the ability to inhabit every kind of human being, from kings to fools, villains to lovers. He is a study in observing human nature with total range, in the power of language to shape thought, and in how a single mind can define a culture's imagination for centuries.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Inhabit every point of view

Shakespeare could write convincingly as anyone.

Do this: Train the empathy to see through others' eyes; understanding every perspective is a creative and a human superpower.
Principle 2

Shape the language itself

He coined words when existing ones fell short.

Do this: When the available vocabulary can't carry your idea, invent the words and forms you need; language is yours to extend.
Principle 3

Find the universal in the particular

His specific characters became universal archetypes.

Do this: Render the specific truthfully and it becomes universal; the deeply particular is what endures across eras.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Shakespeare considered the greatest English writer?

For around 37 plays and 154 sonnets of unmatched psychological depth and linguistic richness that have been performed continuously for four centuries, and for expanding the English language with thousands of words and phrases.

What made Shakespeare's characters so enduring?

A boundless empathy that let him inhabit every kind of person — kings, fools, villains, lovers — creating characters so psychologically real they still feel modern four hundred years later.

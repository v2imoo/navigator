John Maynard Keynes

He argued that in a slump, governments must spend what the private sector won't — and reshaped economic policy for a century.

4 min read · 1920s–1946

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

John Maynard Keynes transformed economics with his 1936 General Theory, written in the shadow of the Great Depression. Against the prevailing view that markets would self-correct, he argued that economies can get stuck in prolonged slumps when total demand collapses, and that governments should then step in — spending, cutting taxes, easing money — to restore demand and employment. His ideas became the foundation of macroeconomics and the dominant framework for postwar economic policy.

Keynes was also a brilliant investor, a central figure in the Bretton Woods system, and a famously sharp wit. His ideas remain fiercely debated, but their influence is undeniable. He is a study in challenging an orthodoxy when the evidence demands it, in thinking about the whole system rather than its parts, and in willingness to change your mind as facts change.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Challenge the orthodoxy when evidence demands

Keynes broke with self-correcting-market dogma in a depression.

Do this: When the prevailing theory fails the facts in front of you, have the nerve to propose a better one.
Principle 2

Think about the whole system

He analysed the economy as an interacting whole, not isolated markets.

Do this: Zoom out to the system level; behaviour that's rational for each part can produce a bad outcome for the whole.
Principle 3

Change your mind as facts change

He was famous for revising his views on new evidence.

Do this: Treat updating your beliefs as a strength, not a weakness; cling to the evidence, not the earlier position.

Frequently asked questions

What is Keynesian economics?

John Maynard Keynes's framework arguing that economies can get stuck in slumps when total demand falls, and that governments should then boost demand through spending, tax cuts or easier money to restore employment.

Why is Keynes important?

His 1936 General Theory founded modern macroeconomics and shaped postwar economic policy worldwide; his ideas on government's role in managing demand remain central — and contested — to this day.

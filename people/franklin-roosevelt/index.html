Franklin D. Roosevelt

He steadied a terrified nation with calm words, remade its government, and led it through its darkest war — from a wheelchair.

4 min read · 1930s–1945

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Franklin Roosevelt became president in 1933 at the depth of the Great Depression, with a quarter of Americans out of work and the banking system collapsing. He responded with the New Deal — a sweeping programme of relief, reform and public works that reshaped the role of government — and with 'fireside chats' that calmed a frightened public through the new medium of radio. He then led the United States through most of World War II, building the alliance that defeated the Axis.

Roosevelt did all this while paralysed from polio, largely hidden from the public. He was a master of experimentation ('take a method and try it; if it fails, try another'), of communication, and of coalition-building. He is a study in projecting calm in crisis, in bold trial-and-error over paralysis, and in leading through the mediums and alliances of the moment.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Project calm in a crisis

FDR's fireside chats steadied a panicked nation.

Do this: In a crisis, the leader's visible composure is itself a stabiliser; communicate calm as deliberately as policy.
Principle 2

Experiment boldly, abandon fast

He tried many approaches and dropped what failed.

Do this: When the path is unknown, act, measure, and switch quickly — bold trial beats cautious paralysis.
Principle 3

Build the coalition the goal requires

He assembled the alliance that won the war.

Do this: Great outcomes need coalitions; invest in the relationships and alliances the mission demands.

In their words

The only thing we have to fear is fear itself.
Take a method and try it. If it fails, admit it frankly and try another.
A smooth sea never made a skilled sailor.

Frequently asked questions

What was the New Deal?

Franklin Roosevelt's sweeping 1930s programme of relief, recovery and reform — public works, financial regulation, social security — that responded to the Great Depression and permanently expanded the role of the US federal government.

Why is FDR considered a great communicator?

His 'fireside chats' used the new medium of radio to speak directly and calmly to anxious Americans during the Depression and war, steadying public confidence in a way no president had before.

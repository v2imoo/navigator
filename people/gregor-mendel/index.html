Gregor Mendel

A monk counting pea plants in a monastery garden discovered the hidden rules of heredity — and was ignored for thirty years.

4 min read · 1850s–1860s

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Gregor Mendel was an Augustinian monk who, over eight years, bred and counted some 28,000 pea plants in his monastery garden, tracking how traits like flower colour and seed shape passed between generations. From the patterns he derived the fundamental laws of inheritance — that traits are carried by discrete units (now called genes), that they come in dominant and recessive forms, and that they segregate and assort independently. It was the birth of genetics.

Mendel published his results in 1866 to near-total silence; the scientific world wasn't ready, and his work was rediscovered only around 1900, long after his death. He is a study in the power of patient quantitative observation, in designing an experiment clean enough to reveal an invisible mechanism, and in the hard truth that being right is not the same as being recognised — sometimes the world takes decades to catch up.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Count, don't guess

Mendel's laws emerged only because he quantified thousands of crosses.

Do this: Turn a vague pattern into numbers; the ratios reveal the mechanism intuition can't see.
Principle 2

Design a clean experiment

Peas gave clear, binary traits perfect for isolating inheritance.

Do this: Choose the simplest system that exposes the variable you care about with the least noise.
Principle 3

Accept that recognition may lag

His work waited thirty years for the world to understand it.

Do this: Do the right work even if its time hasn't come; correctness outlasts applause.

Frequently asked questions

What did Gregor Mendel discover?

The laws of inheritance — that traits pass to offspring via discrete units (genes) in dominant and recessive forms that segregate and assort independently — founding the science of genetics.

Why was Mendel ignored in his lifetime?

He published in 1866 but the scientific community didn't grasp the significance; his work was rediscovered around 1900, well after his death, when biology had caught up.

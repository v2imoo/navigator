Amartya Sen

He redefined development as freedom — and proved that famines are caused by politics, not just a lack of food.

4 min read · 1960s–present

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Amartya Sen brought ethics back into economics. His work on famines showed that they are rarely caused by an absolute lack of food but by failures of distribution, entitlement and democracy — famously observing that no famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press. His 'capability approach' redefined development not as mere income growth but as the expansion of people's real freedoms and capabilities to live lives they value. He won the Nobel Prize in 1998.

Sen reshaped how the world measures progress, influencing the Human Development Index and the discourse of development as freedom. He is a study in asking what economics is ultimately for, in looking past the aggregate number to the human reality beneath it, and in uniting rigorous analysis with moral seriousness.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Ask what the metric is really for

Sen asked whether income captured what development should mean.

Do this: Interrogate your success measure; optimising the wrong number can miss the human outcome entirely.
Principle 2

Look past the aggregate to the person

He went beneath GDP to real freedoms and capabilities.

Do this: Averages hide distribution; always ask who actually benefits and whose real options expand.
Principle 3

Unite rigour with moral seriousness

He fused hard analysis with ethics.

Do this: The most powerful analysis is anchored to a clear view of what ultimately matters; don't divorce the two.

In their words

Development can be seen as a process of expanding the real freedoms that people enjoy.
No famine has ever taken place in a functioning democracy.
Poverty is the deprivation of capability, not merely of income.

Frequently asked questions

Who is Amartya Sen?

An Indian economist and philosopher, Nobel laureate (1998), known for welfare economics, his work on the causes of famines, and the 'capability approach' that redefines development as the expansion of human freedoms.

What is the capability approach?

Sen's framework defining development and well-being not by income alone but by people's real capabilities and freedoms to live lives they have reason to value — which reshaped how progress is measured.

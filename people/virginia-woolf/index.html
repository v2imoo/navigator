Virginia Woolf

She broke open the novel to follow the flow of consciousness — and argued a woman needs a room of her own.

4 min read · 1910s–1941

Contents

Part I — The story

The story

Virginia Woolf was a central figure of literary modernism who transformed the novel by abandoning conventional plot to follow the inner 'stream of consciousness' — the drift of thought, memory and perception — in works like Mrs Dalloway and To the Lighthouse. A founder of the Bloomsbury Group of writers and thinkers, she also wrote one of the foundational texts of feminist criticism, A Room of One's Own, arguing that a woman needs money and a room of her own to write fiction.

Woolf wrote with luminous attention to the texture of consciousness and the inner lives of women, while battling the mental illness that eventually took her life. She is a study in breaking a form to capture truth the old form missed, in articulating the conditions creative work requires, and in rendering the inner life with unprecedented delicacy.

Part II — The playbook

The playbook

Principle 1

Break the form to reach the truth

Woolf abandoned plot to capture real consciousness.

Do this: When the conventional form can't hold what you're trying to say, invent one that can; the method should serve the truth.
Principle 2

Name the conditions creation needs

She argued for 'a room of one's own'.

Do this: Creative work has material prerequisites — space, money, time; naming and securing them is part of the work.
Principle 3

Attend to the inner life

Her subject was the texture of thought itself.

Do this: There is vast, under-explored territory in inner experience; attending to it closely can open wholly new work.

In their words

A woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction.

Frequently asked questions

Why is Virginia Woolf important?

A central modernist who transformed the novel with the 'stream of consciousness' technique in works like Mrs Dalloway, and wrote the foundational feminist essay A Room of One's Own.

What is 'A Room of One's Own' about?

Woolf's argument that a woman needs financial independence and private space — 'money and a room of her own' — in order to write fiction, a foundational text of feminist literary criticism.
